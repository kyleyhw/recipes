## Ingredients

- 1 tbsp neutral oil
- 150 g onion, diced 1 cm
- 120 g carrots, in 1 cm half-moons
- 120 g celery, in 1 cm slices
- 600 g bone-in chicken thighs
- 1500 ml chicken stock
- 2 bay leaves
- 0.5 tsp black pepper
- 1 tsp salt
- 150 g egg noodles
- 10 g flat-leaf parsley, roughly chopped
- 1 lemon

## Method

1. Heat the oil in a large pan over medium heat. Add the onion, carrot and celery and cook 8 minutes, stirring now and then, until the onion is translucent and the celery has lost its squeak. Do not let them colour — this is a pale soup.
2. Put the chicken thighs in skin side down, pour in the stock, and add the bay leaves and black pepper. Bring to a boil, then drop to the barest simmer where a bubble breaks every second or two.
3. Simmer uncovered for 30 minutes, skimming the grey foam for the first 5. A hard boil emulsifies the fat and clouds the broth, and it cannot be cleared afterwards.
4. Lift the thighs out onto a plate and leave them 5 minutes, until cool enough to handle. Pull the skin off and discard it, pull the meat off the bones in large shreds, and put the bones back in the pan for the last 10 minutes if you have time.
5. Cook the noodles in a separate pan of boiling water for 4 minutes, drain, and rinse briefly under cold water so they stop cooking.
6. Fish the bones and bay leaves out of the broth. Return the shredded chicken, add the salt, and taste — it will want more than seems right, because there is a lot of liquid.
7. Put a nest of noodles in each of four bowls and ladle the soup over. Scatter the parsley, squeeze a quarter lemon into each bowl, and serve.

## Notes

The noodles are cooked separately and this is not fussiness. Egg noodles boiled
in the broth shed starch that clouds it and thickens it, and then keep swelling
in the pan until by the second day the soup is a solid mass of noodle. Cooked
apart and rinsed, they sit in the bowl and the leftovers are still soup.

Thighs, bone in, skin on, and the skin comes off at the end. The bone is where
the body of the broth comes from, the skin renders enough fat to carry the
flavour, and neither is pleasant to eat after half an hour in liquid. Breast
meat poached for thirty minutes is dry and stringy however good the soup.

Never boil it. The line between a clear golden broth and a cloudy grey one is
about ten degrees, and once it has gone cloudy nothing brings it back.

The lemon at the end is the thing most recipes leave out and the thing that
makes it taste finished. A soup this gentle goes flat and slightly dull without
an acid, and a quarter of a lemon squeezed in at the table wakes the whole bowl
up. Add it in the bowl rather than the pan, so it stays sharp.

Salt late, and more than you expect. A litre and a half of liquid takes a
surprising amount, and salting early means salting a broth that then reduces.

The only plain Western thing in a collection that has drifted a long way east,
and it is here for the days that call for it. It is also the best possible use
of a chicken carcass: skip the stock and simmer the bones from a roast in
1500 ml of water for an hour first.

## Storage

Four days in the fridge, noodles and soup in separate boxes, and it is better on
the second day. It is the most freezer-friendly thing in this collection: three
months, and the vegetables come back fine because they were never crisp.

Reheat the soup to a simmer and add the noodles for the last 30 seconds. Never
freeze the noodles — they thaw to paste — and never store them in the soup, for
the same reason they were cooked separately in the first place.
