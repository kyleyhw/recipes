## Ingredients

- 200 g beef sirloin
- 150 g onion, sliced 5 mm
- 600 ml dashi
- 3 tbsp light soy sauce
- 2 tbsp mirin
- 1 tbsp sake
- 1 tbsp granulated sugar
- 400 g udon noodles
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Wrap the beef and freeze it for 30 minutes, until firm at the edges and still yielding in the middle, then slice it across the grain as thinly as you can — 1 to 2 mm. Ready-sliced shabu-shabu or sukiyaki beef from an Asian grocer skips this entirely.
2. Put the dashi, soy sauce, mirin, sake and sugar in a wide pan and bring to a boil, stirring until the sugar has gone.
3. Add the onion, drop to a simmer, and cook uncovered for 6 minutes, until translucent and limp but still holding its shape.
4. Separate the beef slices and lay them over the onion. Leave 30 seconds, then turn them through and simmer 3 minutes, skimming the grey foam. It is done when no pink is left and the broth is clear.
5. Meanwhile cook the udon in a separate pan of boiling water — 1 to 2 minutes for frozen or vacuum-packed, 8 to 10 for dried — and drain well.
6. Put the noodles in two deep warmed bowls. Ladle the broth over, so the noodles are submerged, then arrange the beef and onion on top. Scatter the spring onion and eat at once.

## Notes

Three times the dashi of Gyudon, for the same weight of everything else. That is
the entire difference between the two dishes and it is not a small one: gyudon's
broth is a concentrated dressing meant to soak into rice, and this one is a soup
you drink. Make gyudon's version and pour it over noodles and you get a salty
mouthful and a dry bowl.

Everything else is the same warishita — dashi, soy, mirin, sake, sugar — and
learning it once gives you gyudon, sukiyaki and oyakodon as well. See Gyudon for
the beef-slicing problem in full.

Noodles in a separate pan, always. Boiling udon in the broth clouds it with
starch and thickens it into something claggy, and it is the commonest way to
ruin a Japanese noodle soup.

Warm the bowls. Four hundred grams of noodles and 600 ml of broth into two cold
ceramic bowls loses more heat than you would think, and this is a dish that
wants to be too hot to eat for the first minute.

Beni shoga on top is the standard finish and shichimi togarashi the other one. A
raw egg yolk works here exactly as it does on gyudon.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge, with the beef and onion in the broth and the noodles
absent. It is better on the second day.

Reheat the broth to a simmer and cook fresh noodles for it, which takes two
minutes. Never store the noodles in the soup: they go soft within the hour and
to paste by morning.

The broth with its beef freezes for two months. Noodles do not.
