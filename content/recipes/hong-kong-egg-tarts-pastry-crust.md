## Ingredients

- 320 g puff pastry (1 sheet), ready-rolled and all-butter
- 3 eggs
- 60 g caster sugar
- 200 ml water
- 60 ml evaporated milk
- 0.5 tsp vanilla extract

## Method

1. Unroll the pastry on its paper and cut 12 rounds with a 9 cm cutter, gathering and re-rolling the offcuts once. Re-roll them in the same direction the sheet was rolled, and only once — puff pastry re-rolled twice, or across its layers, comes out tough and rises crooked.
2. Press a round into each 7 cm tart tin, easing it down into the corner rather than stretching it across. Pastry stretched into a tin pulls back as it bakes and the sides collapse into the custard. Trim the rims flush with a thumb.
3. Refrigerate the lined tins for 20 minutes. Cold pastry is the whole trick with puff: warm butter merges into the dough and the layers never separate.
4. Heat the oven to 220°C fan.
5. Beat the eggs with the caster sugar until the sugar has gone but the eggs have not lightened — you want no foam at all.
6. Warm the water to hot but not boiling, about 70°C, and stir it into the eggs with the evaporated milk and the vanilla.
7. Pour the custard through a fine sieve twice, into a jug, and skim the foam off the top. This is what makes the surface glassy rather than pitted, and it takes 60 seconds.
8. Fill each shell to 5 mm below the rim — lower than for a cookie crust, because puff pastry rises and the custard has to stay below the rising edge rather than run over it.
9. Bake at 220°C for 15 minutes, until the pastry edges are risen and deep gold. Drop to 190°C and bake 8 to 10 minutes more, until the custard is set at the edge and a 2 cm circle at the centre still wobbles as one piece when you nudge the tray.
10. Cool in the tins for 10 minutes, then lift them out. The layers are at their best in the first hour and audible when you bite one.

## Notes

The custard is exactly the custard in Hong Kong Egg Tarts (Cookie Crust), and
deliberately so — the two tarts differ in their shell and in nothing else, which
is the only way to find out which one you actually prefer.

Bought puff pastry, and no apology. The traditional 酥皮 is a Chinese laminated
pastry made from two doughs — a water dough wrapped around an oil dough, folded
and turned three times — and it is a morning's work for a result most people
cannot pick out of a line-up against all-butter puff. Buy the all-butter kind;
the ones made with vegetable fat taste of the fat and no amount of technique
recovers it.

If you do want the real thing: 200 g plain flour, 100 g water, 30 g lard or
butter and a pinch of salt for the water dough; 140 g plain flour and 90 g lard
for the oil dough. Rest both 30 minutes, wrap the oil dough in the water dough,
roll and fold in three, twice, resting 20 minutes between. It is a good way to
spend a Sunday and it is not a weeknight.

Hotter than the cookie crust, and it has to be. Puff pastry needs an initial
blast to turn the water in its butter to steam and drive the layers apart; the
same 200°C that suits a shortcrust gives a pastry crust that leaks butter and
sits flat.

Do not blind-bake. The custard goes into a raw shell, which sounds wrong and is
correct: the pastry sets in the first five minutes while the custard is still
cold, and blind-baking first gives you a shell that is finished before the
filling has started.

Which of the two is better is a settled question in Hong Kong and an unsettled
one everywhere else. Tai Cheong made its name on the cookie crust; the old
cha chaan tengs and the yum cha trolleys use the flaky one, and it is the older
form.

## Storage

The first hour is when this is worth making. Flaky pastry starts taking up
moisture from the custard the moment it leaves the oven, and by the next day the
bottom is soft — the same tart that shattered at breakfast is merely pleasant at
supper.

A day in a closed box at room temperature. Refrigerating them is worse here than
for the cookie crust, since cold pastry that has gone damp does not crisp again.
Five minutes at 180°C brings back most of it if you catch it on day one.

They do not freeze once filled. The lined, unfilled tins do, for two months —
freeze them in the tins, and bake from frozen with 3 minutes added.
