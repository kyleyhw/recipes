## Ingredients

- 40 g salted duck egg (2 yolks)
- 250 g egg tofu (1 tube)
- 2 tbsp cornflour
- 3 tbsp neutral oil
- 30 g unsalted butter
- 10 g garlic, finely chopped
- 10 g bird's eye chillies, sliced into rings
- 60 ml evaporated milk
- 0.5 tsp granulated sugar
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Slit the tofu tube lengthways, slide the tofu out, and cut it into 2 cm rounds. Press them between kitchen paper for 5 minutes.
2. While they press, separate the yolks from two salted duck eggs and throw the whites away, or use the yolks from cooked vacuum-packed ones. Steam the yolks for 8 minutes, or microwave them covered for 1 minute, until they are firm all through, matt and slightly crumbly at the edge. Mash them to a fine crumb with a fork — no lump bigger than a grain of rice.
3. Turn the rounds in the cornflour, coating faces and edges, and tap off the loose flour.
4. Heat the oil in a 24 cm non-stick frying pan over medium-high heat until a pinch of cornflour fizzes. Fry the rounds 3 minutes a side without moving them, until deep gold. Lift onto kitchen paper and pour the oil out. Wipe the pan.
5. Put the pan back on medium-low heat and melt the butter. Add the garlic and chilli and fry for 30 seconds, until they smell sweet — the butter must not brown.
6. Add the mashed yolk. Stir constantly for 60 to 90 seconds. It will look like wet sand for the first half minute and then, quite suddenly, foam up into a pale orange froth that fills the pan and climbs the sides. **That foam is the dish.** Until it happens the sauce is grainy and tastes raw; the moment it does, the yolk has emulsified into the butter and it is ready.
7. Pour in the evaporated milk and add the sugar. Stir for 30 seconds, until the froth settles into a smooth sauce the colour of apricot that coats the back of a spoon.
8. Turn the heat off. Put the tofu back in and turn it gently through the sauce until every round is coated. Scatter the spring onion and serve at once, with rice.

## Notes

The foam is the whole technique and nothing else in the recipe is difficult.
Cured yolk mashed into hot butter first goes sandy and then, once it is warm
enough, its fat and lecithin come apart and re-emulsify with the butter into a
froth. Stop before that and you have grit in butter. It takes about a minute and
you will know it when you see it.

Medium-low heat, and butter rather than oil, for the same reason: get the pan
too hot and the yolk browns and turns bitter before it ever foams.

No salt goes in anywhere. Two cured yolks are carrying it, and the evaporated
milk and the half-teaspoon of sugar are both there to hold that saltiness down
rather than to sweeten anything.

Cooked vacuum-packed salted eggs are perfectly good here — better, in fact, than
in Steamed Pork Patty with Salted Egg, where the raw whites do half the work.
Here the whites are thrown away regardless, so buying eggs that are already
cooked wastes nothing.

The panel counts that 40 g as whole salted duck egg, because that is the row the
library has. Two cured yolks are considerably richer than 40 g of whole egg, so
the fat and cholesterol figures here are understated — by roughly half on the
fat.

Curry leaves are the Malaysian version and they are better: ten of them, fried
with the garlic until they crackle. They are the one ingredient in this that a
regular supermarket will not have, which is why they are a note rather than a
line.

This works on almost anything fried — prawns, chicken wings, pumpkin, squid. Egg
tofu is the cheapest and the best of them, because a custard interior against
that sauce is a better contrast than more protein.

## Storage

Eat it immediately. The sauce splits as it cools and the crust softens under it;
ten minutes is the useful window.

The sauce alone keeps two days in the fridge and reheats over low heat with a
splash of evaporated milk, whisking, which brings it back. Fry fresh tofu for it
— reheated fried egg tofu is limp.

It does not freeze, in any of its parts.
