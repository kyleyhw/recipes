## Ingredients

- 300 g beef sirloin
- 200 g onion, sliced 5 mm
- 200 ml dashi
- 3 tbsp light soy sauce
- 2 tbsp mirin
- 2 tbsp sake
- 1 tbsp granulated sugar
- 15 g ginger, in fine slivers
- 500 g cooked rice
- 10 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Wrap the beef and freeze it for 30 minutes, until it is firm at the edges and still yielding in the middle. Then slice it across the grain as thinly as you physically can — 1 to 2 mm, thin enough to see the board through. This is the whole difficulty of the dish and freezing is what makes it possible.
2. Put the dashi, soy sauce, mirin, sake, sugar and ginger in a wide shallow pan — a 24 cm frying pan is better than a saucepan — and bring to a boil, stirring until the sugar has gone.
3. Add the onion, turn the heat down to a simmer, and cook uncovered for 6 minutes. It is ready when the slices have gone translucent and limp but still hold their shape when you lift one.
4. Separate the beef slices with your fingers and lay them over the onion in one layer. Leave them for 30 seconds without stirring, until the edges have turned from red to grey-brown.
5. Turn the beef through gently and simmer for 3 minutes more, skimming the grey foam as it rises. It is done when no pink is left, the onion has gone soft, and the liquid has reduced by about a third — it should still be a broth you could spoon, not a glaze.
6. Put the hot rice in two deep bowls. Spoon the beef and onion over, then ladle enough of the broth on to darken the top centimetre of the rice. Scatter the spring onion and eat it at once, with a spoon as much as chopsticks.

## Notes

It is supposed to be wet. Every instinct trained on stir-fries says to reduce
that liquid until it clings, and reducing it is how you turn gyudon into
something else — the broth soaking down into the rice is the dish. If anything
err on the loose side.

Dashi, soy, mirin, sake and sugar is warishita, and it is the same sauce that
makes sukiyaki and oyakodon. Learn the ratio here and you have all three: this
one is roughly 3 parts soy to 2 mirin to 2 sake, with dashi as the body and a
spoon of sugar.

Buy the beef ready sliced if you can. An Asian grocer sells it frozen as
shabu-shabu or sukiyaki beef, already at the right thickness, and it skips
step 1 entirely — take half an hour off the time on this page if that is what
you have. Otherwise the freezer is not optional: nobody hand-slices raw beef to
2 mm at room temperature.

Sirloin is what this uses and ribeye is better if you are buying for it, being
fattier. Anything lean and dense — rump, silverside — comes out chewy however
thin it is cut, because there is no time in a four-minute simmer to soften
anything.

The panel says 1456 mg of sodium a serving and that is honest — three
tablespoons of soy sauce split two ways, and none of it poured away, since the
broth going into the rice is the dish. A donburi shop's version is saltier
still. Cut the soy to 2 tbsp and add a splash more dashi if that matters to you;
it will taste flatter and it will not be wrong.

Skim the foam. It is denatured protein rather than anything harmful, and left in
it makes the broth cloudy and faintly livery.

Things that go on top: beni shoga, the red pickled ginger, which is the standard
and cuts the sweetness; shichimi togarashi; or a raw egg yolk, which makes it
the tsukimi version and is worth doing if your eggs are safe raw — see Tamago
Kake Gohan for that question.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge, in its broth, covered. It is one of the few things
here that is arguably better the next day, because the onion keeps taking up
the sauce.

Reheat in a pan over medium heat for 4 minutes, adding 50 ml of water if it has
thickened. Do not boil it hard — the beef is already cooked and boiling it turns
2 mm slices to string. Keep it off the rice until you serve it, or you will be
reheating a solid block.

The beef and onion freeze for two months in their broth. Rice does not; cook
that fresh.
