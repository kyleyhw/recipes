## Ingredients

- 1 tbsp neutral oil
- 200 g fatty pork mince
- 10 g garlic, finely chopped
- 10 g ginger, finely chopped
- 40 g zha cai, finely chopped
- 1 tbsp shaoxing wine
- 1 tbsp dark soy sauce
- 3 tbsp sesame paste
- 2 tbsp light soy sauce
- 3 tbsp chilli oil
- 1 tbsp black vinegar
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 2 tsp ground sichuan pepper
- 180 g egg noodles
- 2 egg yolks
- 20 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Heat the oil in a frying pan over high heat until it shimmers. Add the pork and press it flat against the base. Fry 4 minutes without stirring, then break it up and fry 4 minutes more, until the water it released has gone and the meat is dark, dry and crumbly with crisp edges.
2. Add the garlic, ginger and zha cai and fry 60 seconds. Add the shaoxing wine and dark soy sauce and stir until the pan is dry again, about 60 seconds. Take it off the heat.
3. In each of two deep bowls, put half the sesame paste, light soy sauce, chilli oil, black vinegar, sugar and ground sichuan pepper. Stir each to a thick smooth paste with the back of a spoon.
4. Bring a pan of water to a rolling boil and put the noodles in.
5. When the noodles are 30 seconds from done, take 3 tablespoons of the boiling water out of the pan and whisk half of it into each bowl of paste. It goes from stiff and oily to a loose, glossy sauce that just runs. The starch in that water is what makes it cling to noodles rather than sitting under them — plain water off the tap does not do the same thing.
6. Drain the noodles, but not too thoroughly. Pile them straight onto the sauce.
7. Spoon the pork over one side, slip a raw egg yolk into the middle, and heap the spring onion on the other side.
8. Take it to the table and toss it hard for 20 seconds before eating — right down to the bottom of the bowl, until the yolk has broken and disappeared and every strand is the colour of the sauce. This is not optional and it is the eater's job, not the cook's.

## Notes

Three tablespoons of liquid, not seven hundred millilitres. That is the whole
difference between this and Tantanmen (Soup): the same sauce, concentrated into
a dressing that sits at the bottom of the bowl, rather than let down into a
soup.
Everything else about the two is nearly identical and they eat nothing alike.

Use the noodle water. Starchy water off the boil emulsifies the sesame paste and
then keeps it clinging to the noodles; plain hot water loosens it just as well
and lets it slide straight off into a puddle. Take it out of the pan with a
ladle before the noodles are drained, because once the water is gone it is gone.

Toss it, and toss it properly. Twenty seconds, from the bottom, before the first
mouthful. Served but untossed the sauce stays under the noodles and the top half
of the bowl tastes of nothing — this is the most common way people are
disappointed by a dry noodle dish, and it happens after the cooking is over.

Twice the sichuan pepper of the soup version, and it is deliberate. There is no
broth to dilute it, and the numbing is much more of the point here: Hiroshima's
shirunashi tantanmen is famous for arriving at a level of ma that takes a few
visits to enjoy. Start at 1 tsp if you are not sure.

The raw yolk is Hiroshima's addition and it does real work — it emulsifies with
the sesame and the chilli oil as you toss and turns the sauce from sharp to
round. It also means an egg you would eat raw, which in the UK means a British
Lion mark; Tamago Kake Gohan's notes have that argument in full.

Black vinegar is the other thing the soup version does not have. In a bowl with
no broth the sesame paste and the chilli oil are heavy on their own, and a
tablespoon of Chinkiang cuts straight through them.

The panel says 2810 mg of sodium against Tantanmen (Soup)'s 3132, and the
comparison is backwards in practice. Nobody drinks all of a bowl of soup —
leave a third of that broth and the two dishes cross over — whereas here the
sauce is at the bottom of the bowl and the whole point is to toss it through
and eat every bit of it. This is the saltier of the two as it is actually
eaten, and the number cannot show that.

This is closer to the Sichuan original than the soup is. Dan dan noodles have
little or no liquid and, in the older versions, no sesame paste either — Japan
added the soup and the sesame, and shirunashi tantanmen is a partial journey
back.

## Storage

The pork keeps four days in the fridge and freezes for two months. Make three
times as much: it is the slow part of both tantanmen recipes and it goes on
rice, on plain noodles and into lettuce cups.

The assembled bowl keeps for nothing. The sauce sets stiff round cold noodles
and the yolk is raw.

The sauce paste can be stirred together and kept two days in the fridge without
the noodle water in it. Bring it back to room temperature before loosening, or
it will not emulsify.
