## Ingredients

- 130 g unsalted butter, softened
- 50 g icing sugar
- 1 egg
- 0.25 tsp salt
- 0.5 tsp vanilla extract
- 240 g all-purpose flour
- 3 eggs
- 60 g caster sugar
- 200 ml water
- 60 ml evaporated milk

## Method

1. Beat the softened butter with the icing sugar and the salt for 2 minutes, until pale and smooth but not aerated — this is a shortcrust, not a cake, and beating air into it makes a crust that puffs and cracks.
2. Beat in one egg and the vanilla. It will look curdled for about 30 seconds and then come back together.
3. Sift in the flour and fold until there are no dry patches left and the dough comes away from the bowl in one piece. Stop there. Every extra turn builds gluten, and gluten is what turns a sandy crust chewy.
4. Wrap and refrigerate for 30 minutes, until firm enough to press without sticking to your thumb.
5. Heat the oven to 200°C fan. Divide the dough into 12 pieces of about 35 g. Press each into a 7 cm foil or metal tart tin: thumb it up the sides first, then even out the base, aiming for 3 mm everywhere. A thick base is the usual fault and it stays raw under the custard.
6. Beat the 3 eggs with the caster sugar until the sugar has gone but the eggs have not lightened — you want no foam at all.
7. Warm the water to hot but not boiling, about 70°C, and stir it into the egg with the evaporated milk. Hot water dissolves the last of the sugar and takes the custard's starting temperature up, so it sets before the crust overbakes.
8. Pour the custard through a fine sieve twice, into a jug. Skim any foam off the top with a spoon. This is the step that decides whether the tarts come out glassy or pitted, and it takes 60 seconds.
9. Fill each shell to 3 mm below the rim. Do not fill to the top: the custard rises as it sets and then sinks, and one that overflowed sticks the tart into its tin.
10. Bake at 200°C for 15 minutes, then drop to 180°C and bake 8 to 10 minutes more. They are done when the custard is set at the edge and a 2 cm circle at the centre still wobbles as one piece when you nudge the tray. A skewer test is the wrong test here.
11. Cool in the tins for 10 minutes, then lift them out. Eat warm.

## Notes

The custard is not a crème brûlée and must not be treated as one. There is no
cream in it and no yolk-heavy enrichment — whole eggs, sugar, water and a little
evaporated milk, in a ratio of about one part egg to one and three quarters
liquid by weight, which is what gives the soft, trembling, faintly savoury set a
Hong Kong tart has. Add cream and you get a Portuguese tart, which is a good
thing and a different one.

Pull them at a wobble. The residual heat finishes the middle in the tin, and a
custard baked until firm all the way through has gone past silky into rubbery.
If a tart puffs up like a soufflé the oven is too hot; open the door for ten
seconds and drop the temperature 10°C.

Sieving twice is not fussiness. Egg white does not break up completely however
long you whisk, and the strands that get through are the white specks and the
pitted surface you see in a bad tart. Skimming the foam matters for the same
reason.

Cookie crust or pastry crust is a real choice and not a compromise: this one is
short, sandy and slightly sweet, and eats like a shortbread biscuit holding
custard. See Hong Kong Egg Tarts (Pastry Crust) for the flaky one, which is the
older and more prestigious of the two and a great deal more work.

Plain flour rather than cake flour. Cake flour is softer and gives a slightly
finer crumb, and it is not sold in every supermarket; the difference is smaller
than the difference made by overworking the dough, which is free to avoid.

No custard powder. Many Hong Kong recipes put a spoonful in the crust for the
yellow colour and the vanilla-custard smell, and it is genuinely traditional.
It is left out here because it is one more thing to buy for one recipe, and
vanilla does the aroma. If you have it, swap 15 g of the flour for it.

## Storage

Best within four hours of coming out of the oven, which is not a rule so much as
a description — the crust is at its shortest and the custard at its softest, and
both go one way from there.

A day in a closed box at room temperature, and no longer, since the custard is
eggs and water. Do not refrigerate them if you can help it: cold makes the
custard weep and the crust go damp from underneath. If you must, bring them back
to room temperature and give them 5 minutes at 160°C, which recovers the crust
and not the custard.

They do not freeze. The dough does — wrap it and freeze for two months, and
thaw it overnight in the fridge before pressing.
