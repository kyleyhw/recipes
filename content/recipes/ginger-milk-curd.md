## Ingredients

- 60 g ginger
- 300 ml evaporated milk
- 150 ml water
- 2 tsp granulated sugar

## Method

1. Peel the ginger and grate it finely — a microplane is too fine, a coarse box grater is right. Pile the gratings into a square of muslin or a clean tea towel over a bowl and squeeze hard. You want 2 tablespoons of juice, which 60 g of ginger gives with some effort. Old, fibrous, strongly smelling ginger yields more and works better than young ginger.
2. Divide the juice between two bowls that hold about 250 ml each, 1 tablespoon in each. Swirl it so it coats the base. The juice separates within a minute, leaving white starch at the bottom — that is normal and it must be stirred back in immediately before the milk goes on, so do this last of all.
3. Stir the evaporated milk, the water and the sugar together in a small pan. Heat over medium heat, stirring, until it reaches 75 °C on a thermometer. Without one: take it off when the surface is steaming hard and the first small bubbles appear at the edge of the pan, and never when it is boiling.
4. Stir the ginger juice in each bowl once more, then pour half the hot milk into each in one continuous go, from about 20 cm above the bowl. The height matters — it mixes the juice through the milk in the two seconds available, which is all the mixing this gets.
5. Do not stir. Do not move the bowls. Do not cover them. Leave them completely still for 10 minutes.
6. It has set when you can rest a teaspoon flat on the surface and it does not sink, and when tilting the bowl moves the whole thing as one piece with no liquid running at the edge. Eat it warm, with the spoon.

## Notes

This is a protease setting a milk, not heat setting a custard, and it is one of
the few things in a kitchen that works like rennet. Ginger carries an enzyme,
zingibain, which cuts the milk protein in exactly the place that makes it
coagulate. That is why the juice has to be raw — heat it and the enzyme is
destroyed — and why nothing here is cooked after the pour.

Seventy-five degrees, and this is the number everything turns on. Below about
70 °C the enzyme works too slowly and you get warm ginger milk. Above about
85 °C the enzyme is denatured before it can act and you get warm ginger milk.
The window is roughly fifteen degrees wide and a thermometer removes all the
guesswork; the traditional cue is milk that steams hard but has not moved.

Evaporated milk diluted with half its volume of water, rather than fresh milk,
and this is the reliability trick. The set depends on there being enough protein
for the enzyme to knit together, and this mixture lands near 4.5% protein against
whole milk's 3.3%. It is the difference between a dish that works every time and
one that works most times. Undiluted evaporated milk sets too firm and tastes
cooked.

Pour, do not stir. The set begins within seconds, and a spoon through it at any
point in the first ten minutes breaks the curd permanently into a grainy mess
that will not come back.

If it has not set after 10 minutes, the milk was too hot or too cool, or the
ginger was young and weak. It is still a good hot drink. Nothing is recoverable
by reheating.

Old ginger, the sort with dry papery skin and visible fibres, is what this wants.
Young stem ginger with thin pale skin has much less enzyme, and is the commonest
reason a first attempt fails.

## Storage

Eat it warm, within a few minutes. This is the whole dish — warm, just set, and
faintly hot from the ginger.

It keeps two days covered in the fridge and becomes a different and perfectly
good thing: firmer, colder, closer to a panna cotta, with the ginger heat
sharper. Do not try to warm it up again, which breaks the curd back into liquid
and grains.

It does not freeze.
