## Ingredients

- 3 eggs
- 130 g brown sugar
- 80 ml evaporated milk
- 60 ml neutral oil
- 160 g all-purpose flour
- 2.5 tsp baking powder
- 0.25 tsp bicarbonate of soda
- 0.25 tsp salt

## Method

1. Line an 18 cm round tin with baking paper, cut so the sides stand 3 cm above the rim — the cake rises to about twice the depth of the batter and will climb the paper.
2. Put a rack in a wok or a wide pan, add water to 2 cm below the rack, and bring it to a rolling boil. Wrap the lid in a tea towel, gathering the corners over the handle so no cloth hangs near the flame.
3. Whisk the eggs and brown sugar hard in a large bowl for 2 minutes, until the sugar has dissolved and the mixture is smooth, glossy and a shade paler. It does not need to hold a ribbon — the baking powder does the lifting here, not beaten-in air, which is what makes this a cake you can whisk by hand.
4. Pour in the evaporated milk and the oil and whisk for 30 seconds more, until the surface is one even colour and no slick of oil sits on top.
5. Sift the flour, baking powder, bicarbonate of soda and salt straight onto the wet mixture. Fold with a spatula until the last streak of flour disappears, no more than 20 turns. Stop while the batter still looks slightly rough; it should pour thickly off the spatula in a ribbon that sinks back in 3 seconds.
6. Scrape into the tin and level the top. Tap the tin twice on the counter to bring up the big bubbles.
7. Set the tin on the rack, cover with the cloth-wrapped lid, and steam over high heat with the water at a rolling boil for 25 minutes. Do not lift the lid before 20 minutes have passed.
8. It is done when the cake has domed and split across the top, sprung back from a finger pressed into the centre, and a skewer comes out with no wet batter on it. If the middle is still slack, put the lid back and give it 5 minutes more.
9. Lift the tin out, stand it on a rack for 10 minutes, then lift the cake out by the paper. Cut into 8 wedges with a serrated knife and eat it warm, which is when it is best.

## Notes

The steaming is done hard, which is the opposite of the other steamed thing on
this site. Steamed Egg Custard wants the lowest flame in the house because heat
makes bubbles in it; this wants a rolling boil because bubbles are the point,
and a cake that heats slowly spends its raising power before the crumb has set
around it. Same pan, opposite instruction.

Wrap the lid. Twenty-five minutes of condensation dripping onto an open batter
gives a wet, pitted, sunken top, and this is the one step people leave out.

Brown sugar is the flavour of the cake and not a substitution for white. The
dark, slightly molasses taste and the tan colour both come from it. Dark
muscovado makes a stronger, almost treacly version, and Chinese slab sugar, if
you have it, is the traditional answer — chop 130 g of it and melt it in the
evaporated milk first, warm, then let it cool before it meets the eggs.

Oil rather than butter, and this is deliberate. A steamed cake is eaten warm or
at room temperature and butter sets firm as it cools, which turns a light crumb
heavy by the second day. Any flavourless oil does.

The real ma lai gao is a fermented cake: a batter left overnight with an old
starter, which is where the faint sourness and the tall open honeycomb of a good
one come from. This is the weekday version that every home cook actually makes,
and it is a genuinely different cake — lighter in flavour, finer in crumb, and
twenty-five minutes rather than two days. If you want a step toward the other
one for no extra work, leave the finished batter to stand for 30 minutes before
it goes in the tin.

There is no crust, anywhere, and that is what steaming is for. The whole cake is
the texture that the middle of a baked one has. Banana Bread, the other cake
here, is the opposite argument.

Doubling this wants a wider tin rather than a deeper one — the panel above works
that out. A double batch in the same 18 cm tin is 4 cm of raw batter, and the
outside will be dry before a skewer comes out of the middle clean.

## Storage

Three days at room temperature in a covered tin. Not the fridge, which dries a
steamed cake faster than the counter does.

To bring it back, steam a wedge for 4 minutes and it is indistinguishable from
new — this is one of the few cakes where reheating loses nothing, because
steaming is how it was made. A microwave works too: 20 seconds under a damp
cloth, and not a second longer without it, or the cut faces go leathery.

Freezes for two months, wrapped in pairs of wedges. Steam from frozen for 8
minutes; do not defrost first, which makes it soggy.
