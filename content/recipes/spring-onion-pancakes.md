## Ingredients

- 300 g all-purpose flour, for the dough
- 165 ml water, boiling
- 50 ml water, cold
- 30 g all-purpose flour, for the oil paste
- 45 ml neutral oil, for the oil paste
- 2 tsp sesame oil
- 1 tsp salt
- 0.5 tsp white pepper
- 100 g spring onions, finely sliced
- 4 tbsp neutral oil, for frying

## Method

1. Slice the spring onions 4 mm thick, green and white together, and spread them in one layer on a plate lined with kitchen paper. Leave them there until step 6. Wet spring onions steam inside the pancake and push the layers apart into one bready mass, and the paper takes off the water the knife released.
2. Put the 300 g of flour in a large bowl. Pour the boiling water over it in a slow spiral while stirring hard with chopsticks or a fork. Keep going for 30 seconds, until the flour is in ragged lumps the size of peas with no dry powder left in the bottom of the bowl. Half-scalded dough is what makes this soft enough to roll paper-thin without springing back.
3. Leave it 5 minutes, until you can put a hand on it. Add the cold water and bring it together into one mass.
4. Knead on an unfloured surface for 5 minutes. It starts sticky and ugly and finishes smooth, soft, and just tacky enough to cling to your palm and let go without tearing. Cover the bowl with a plate and rest it 1 hour at room temperature.
5. Stir the 30 g of flour, the 45 ml of neutral oil, the sesame oil, the salt and the white pepper together in a small bowl for 20 seconds, to a smooth paste that falls off the spoon in a ribbon and holds it for a second. This is the layering: fat between sheets of dough is what keeps them from welding.
6. Divide the dough into 4 pieces of about 130 g. Working with one and keeping the rest covered, roll it on a lightly oiled surface into a rectangle 30 cm by 20 cm and 2 mm thick — thin enough to see the worktop through it.
7. Spread a quarter of the paste over the whole sheet with the back of a spoon, right to the edges, and scatter a quarter of the spring onions over it.
8. Roll it up from a long edge into a tight rope. Pick the rope up by its ends and stretch it to half as long again, then wind it into a flat spiral on the worktop and tuck the loose end underneath. Cover it and shape the other three; by the time the fourth is coiled the first has had the 15 minutes of rest it needs.
9. Press each spiral down with your palm and roll it out to an 18 cm round, 8 mm thick. Press more than you roll — hard rolling squeezes the paste out of the seams and the layers go with it.
10. Heat 1 tbsp of the frying oil in a 24 cm frying pan over medium heat until a scrap of dough sizzles on contact. Fry one pancake for 3 minutes a side, pressing it flat with a spatula for the first 30 seconds only. It is done when both sides are mottled deep gold, the edge has puffed and gone pale, and it slides freely in the pan.
11. Take it out, and while it is too hot to hold comfortably, push the edges in towards the middle with two spatulas — three or four squeezes all round. The layers separate audibly and this is what makes the inside shreddy rather than solid. Fry the rest the same way, 1 tbsp of oil each.
12. Cut into six wedges and eat straight away.

## Notes

This is the Taiwanese version — scalded dough, thick, chewy, and scrunched at
the end. The northern Chinese original it descends from is thinner, crisper and
made with cold-water dough, and is a different pancake rather than a worse one.
Neither is the Korean pajeon, which is a batter and involves no dough at all.

Two ratios carry the whole recipe. The water is 72% of the flour, which is more
than a dumpling wrapper and much more than a pastry, and it is what lets the
sheet go to 2 mm. Two-thirds of that water is boiling, which cooks enough of
the starch to stop the gluten fighting back while leaving enough raw to chew.
All boiling gives a dough that tears; all cold gives one that will not roll out
and shrinks back on the pan.

The oil paste — 油酥 — is not optional and not the same as brushing with oil.
Plain oil runs out of the seams and pools; flour thickens it enough to stay
where it is put and to fry into a layer of its own. Lard instead of neutral oil
is the traditional version and is noticeably better, if that is something you
keep.

A tablespoon of oil a pancake is shallow frying, not deep, and it is measured
into the pan rather than poured because too much browns the outside before the
middle has cooked and gives a pancake that is dark and raw at once.

The dough is better the next day. Made to the end of step 4, wrapped and left
in the fridge overnight, it rolls out more easily and tastes more of wheat —
bring it back to room temperature for 30 minutes first or it will fight you.

Serve them with Ginger and Spring Onion Oil, or with a dipping sauce of 2 tbsp
light soy sauce, 1 tbsp black vinegar and a teaspoon of chilli oil. In Taiwan
the breakfast version has an egg fried into one side: crack it into the pan,
lay the pancake on top for the last minute and turn the pair over together.

## Storage

The coiled, unfried spirals are the thing to keep, not the fried pancakes.
Stack them with baking paper between, bag them, and they freeze for three
months. Roll them out from frozen — they flatten fine — and fry straight away,
adding a minute a side. This is why the recipe is worth making at four rather
than one.

Fried, they are three days in the fridge in a sealed bag and they go leathery
in it. Bring one back in a dry frying pan over medium heat, 90 seconds a side,
which re-crisps the shell; a microwave turns it into a flannel and cannot be
undone. They do not keep at room temperature past the evening, because the oil
in the layers goes stale-tasting overnight.
