## Ingredients

- 700 g beef brisket
- 2.5 litres water
- 2 bay leaves
- 400 g potato, in 2 cm cubes
- 300 g white cabbage, shredded 5 mm thick
- 3 tbsp neutral oil
- 200 g onion, finely diced
- 150 g carrots, coarsely grated
- 400 g beetroot, coarsely grated
- 2 tbsp tomato paste
- 2 tbsp white vinegar
- 4 cloves garlic, crushed to a paste with a pinch of salt
- 15 g dill, chopped
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 1.5 tsp salt
- 0.5 tsp black pepper
- 150 ml sour cream

## Method

1. Put the brisket in a large pot with the water and bring it to a boil over high heat. Skim off the grey foam as it rises, for the first 5 minutes, until what comes up is white rather than grey and the broth beneath is clear.
2. Add the bay leaves, turn the heat down until the surface barely moves, and simmer uncovered for 90 minutes. It should never boil again: a rolling boil emulsifies the fat and turns the broth cloudy, and it cannot be cleared afterwards.
3. Lift the meat out onto a board and leave it to cool. Keep the broth at a bare simmer.
4. Add the potato to the broth and cook for 10 minutes, until a knife goes into a cube with slight resistance. Add the cabbage and cook 8 minutes more, until it is limp but still has a bite at the spine.
5. While that happens, make the zazharka. Heat the oil in a 28 cm frying pan over medium heat and cook the onion for 5 minutes, until translucent at the edges and still pale. Add the carrot and cook 3 minutes.
6. Add the beetroot and cook for 8 minutes, stirring every minute. It darkens from magenta to something nearer brick, and the pan smells sweet and earthy rather than raw.
7. Stir in the tomato paste and cook 2 minutes, until it darkens a shade and stops smelling tinny. Add the vinegar and let it sizzle for 1 minute. The pan turns bright red as it hits — that is the whole trick of the dish, and step 11 explains it.
8. Pull the meat into 2 cm pieces with two forks, discarding gristle and bone.
9. Tip the zazharka and the meat into the pot. Bring it back to a bare simmer and cook 5 minutes. Do not let it boil from here on.
10. Take the pot off the heat. Stir in the garlic, dill, sugar, salt and pepper. Cover it and leave it to stand for 20 minutes — the garlic loses its rawness in the residual heat without cooking, which is why it goes in last.
11. Serve with a spoonful of sour cream in each bowl, stirred in at the table.

## Notes

The vinegar is not there to make the soup sour, and if you can taste it as
vinegar there is too much. Beetroot is red because of betanin, which breaks down
with heat and turns a dull orange-brown — and it breaks down far more slowly in
acid. So the acid goes in with the beetroot, in the pan, before either meets the
pot. A borscht made by boiling raw beetroot in broth for an hour is brown by the
time it reaches the table, and no amount of vinegar afterwards brings the colour
back.

Which is also why nothing boils after step 9. From that point the pot is held
just under a simmer.

Grate the beetroot rather than dicing it. Grated, it gives up its colour to the
oil in eight minutes; diced, it needs half an hour and by then the colour is
going.

The garlic and dill go in off the heat. Both are aromatic rather than structural
and both are wasted by simmering — a borscht that smells of raw garlic when the
lid comes off has been made correctly.

UNESCO put the culture of Ukrainian borscht cooking on its list of intangible
heritage in need of urgent safeguarding in 2022, which is a sentence about a war
rather than about a soup. It is Ukrainian, and there are as many versions of it
as there are households.

Vegetarian: leave out the meat, use 2 litres of water, and add a handful of
dried mushrooms at step 1. The colour and the method are unchanged; the depth is
not, and there is no pretending otherwise.

Traditionally eaten with pampushky — garlic bread rolls — or dark rye, and a
slice of salo alongside.

## Storage

Four days in the fridge, and it is better on the second day than the first: the
beetroot goes on colouring the broth overnight and the vinegar settles into it.

Reheat gently, to steaming and no further. Boiling reheated borscht is the other
way people end up with brown soup.

It freezes for 3 months, but the potato does not: it goes grainy and slightly
hollow. Either accept that, or leave the potato out of the batch you mean to
freeze and boil fresh cubes into it as it reheats.
