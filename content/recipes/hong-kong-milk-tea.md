## Ingredients

- 400 ml black tea, brewed very strong
- 120 ml evaporated milk
- 2 tsp granulated sugar

## Method

1. Bring 600 ml of water to a rolling boil in a small pan. Tip in 25 g of black tea leaves — a coarse Ceylon broken-leaf as the base, with a spoonful of fine dust if you have it — and boil hard, uncovered, for 3 minutes. It will foam up the sides; stir it down.
2. Take the pan off the heat, cover it, and leave it for 5 minutes. The tea should now be opaque and the colour of dark mahogany, and it should taste flatly bitter on its own. That is correct: the milk is going to take most of that away.
3. Set a fine cloth bag or a doubled square of muslin over a jug and pour the tea through it. Then pull it: pour the strained tea back into the pan, lift the pan a forearm's height above the bag, and pour it through again. Four times, in a thin stream from as high as you can manage without splashing. The tea comes out visibly smoother and slightly paler, and this is the step the whole style is named for.
4. Warm the evaporated milk in a small pan over low heat until it steams and no further. It must not boil.
5. Put 60 ml of warm evaporated milk and a teaspoon of sugar into each cup. Pour 200 ml of hot tea into each in one steady stream and stir once.
6. It is right when the colour is that of a digestive biscuit — if it is grey it wants more tea, and if it is orange-brown it wants more milk. Drink it hot.

## Notes

Evaporated milk, not fresh, and this is the whole difference between this and a
builder's tea. Tea at that strength is acidic and full of tannin, and fresh milk
poured into it thins out and can curdle at the edges. Evaporated milk is already
heat-treated and twice as concentrated, so it holds together, coats the tannin
and gives the drink the specific silky heaviness it is supposed to have. A splash
of single cream is the nearest substitute and it is not close.

Pulling the tea is not showmanship. Forcing hot tea through cloth repeatedly
aerates it and strips the fine particles that make a strong brew taste rough,
and the difference between pulled and not pulled is obvious side by side. A very
fine metal sieve will do some of it; cloth does all of it. Rinse the bag in
plain hot water and never with soap, and it will stain deep brown, which is what
a used one is supposed to look like.

Twenty-five grams of leaf to 600 ml of water is four or five times the strength
of a normal pot, and boiling it rather than steeping it is deliberate. This is a
drink built to be tasted through milk, so the tea has to be strong enough to
survive it.

Cha jau — 茶走 — is the same drink with condensed milk in place of both the
evaporated milk and the sugar: two tablespoons per cup, and nothing else. It is
sweeter, thicker and rounder, and it was originally what you ordered if fresh
dairy disagreed with you.

Iced, this is 凍奶茶: brew it as written, cool it completely, and pour it over a
full glass of ice with the milk already in. Do not pour hot tea onto ice, which
melts to a weak brown puddle.

The panel counts the evaporated milk and the sugar, which is all of the energy
here — the tea itself is water with a little potassium in it.

## Storage

Drink it within a few minutes. Milk tea left standing goes flat and slightly
bitter as it cools, and reheating it in a microwave scalds the milk.

The tea base alone keeps two days in the fridge, covered, and is genuinely
useful — it is the head start on an iced milk tea, and it is why brewing 600 ml
for 400 ml of drink is not waste. Do not add the milk until you are pouring.
