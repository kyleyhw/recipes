## Ingredients

- 1200 ml water
- 20 g dried anchovies, heads and guts removed
- 10 g dried kelp
- 360 g kimchi, sour, roughly chopped, with 60 ml of its brine
- 30 g gochujang
- 2 tbsp gochugaru
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 4 cloves garlic, minced
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 200 g luncheon meat, in 5 mm slices
- 180 g frankfurters, in 5 mm rounds
- 200 g firm tofu, in 1 cm slices
- 150 g onion, sliced 5 mm
- 200 g tteok
- 100 g instant noodles
- 40 g processed cheese
- 30 g spring onions, cut into 4 cm lengths

## Method

1. Snap the head off each anchovy and pinch out the black gut. Put them in a 24 cm shallow pan or casserole with the water and the kelp, bring to a boil, and boil uncovered for 15 minutes, lifting the kelp out at 10 minutes. Strain and set the stock aside; you will have about 1 litre. Wash the pan.
2. Stir the gochujang, gochugaru, light soy sauce, garlic and sugar together in a small bowl into a stiff paste.
3. Soak the tteok in warm water for 20 minutes and pull the cylinders apart.
4. Now build it dry. Spread the kimchi over the base of the pan, keeping its brine in the jar for the moment. Arrange the luncheon meat, frankfurters, tofu, onion and drained tteok over it in separate wedges, like spokes, and put the paste in a mound in the middle. This is not decoration: it is how the dish is served, and it is the only chance to see what is in it before everything goes the same colour.
5. Pour the stock and the 60 ml of kimchi brine down the side of the pan so the arrangement is not disturbed. The liquid should come two thirds of the way up, not cover it.
6. Bring to a boil over high heat, then drop to a steady simmer and cook for 12 minutes uncovered. Do not stir yet. The paste dissolves outward from the middle on its own and the fat renders out of the meat into the broth, which goes from dull red to glossy orange-red.
7. Stir once, to bring the paste through. Taste it. It should be sour before it is spicy — if it is not, add another 30 ml of kimchi brine rather than more gochujang.
8. Lay the instant noodles on top and push them under. Simmer 4 minutes, until they have loosened and separated but still have a core; they carry on cooking in the pan at the table.
9. Lay the cheese over the noodles and scatter the spring onions. Take the pan off the heat and carry it to the table. The cheese melts in the 30 seconds it takes to get there and is stirred in by whoever serves.

## Notes

Old kimchi, and this is the one ingredient worth waiting for. A fresh jar is
crisp and mild and makes a stew that tastes of chilli paste; three-month-old
kimchi, soft and sour and fizzing when you open it, is what the dish is built
on, and the brine is not an optional extra — it is the acid. If all you have is
a young jar, add a tablespoon of rice vinegar at step 7 and accept that it is a
stand-in.

The stock is anchovy and kelp, and water would be a different and worse dish.
The same broth is under the Tteokbokki, so a double batch covers both.

Build it dry, arranged. It looks like styling and it is actually the format:
budae jjigae arrives at the table as an uncooked arrangement in a shallow pan
and is cooked in front of you, and the reason the sections are kept apart is so
that everyone can see what they are getting before it turns into one red pot.

The noodles go in last and are meant to be underdone leaving the stove. They
sit in a pan that is still near boiling while people eat, and a block cooked
through in the kitchen is mush by the second bowl.

The cheese is not a joke and it is not a topping. A processed slice melts into
the broth without splitting, which is the whole reason it and not cheddar: it
rounds the chilli and thickens the liquid slightly, and the stew tastes
markedly flatter without it.

There is 3,300 mg of sodium in this pan before any is added, from the luncheon
meat, the frankfurters, the kimchi and the gochujang — about 800 mg a serving.
There is no salt in the recipe for that reason and none should be added.

The dish is from Uijeongbu in the years after the Korean war, made from what
came off the American bases, and the two ingredients people flinch at are the
two that make it what it is. The Uijeongbu version adds a small tin of baked
beans, which is more traditional than the cheese and much more divisive.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge, and take the noodles and the tteok out before it goes
in — both keep swelling overnight and turn to paste, and both are three minutes
to replace. What is left is a good stew base that is better on the second day
than the first.

Reheat in the pan over medium heat for 6 minutes with 100 ml of water, since it
will have thickened. Add fresh noodles at the end as before.

Do not freeze it. Tofu comes back spongy, the tteok comes back chalky, and the
emulsified sausage goes grainy. If you want to get ahead, make the anchovy
stock and the paste in advance — the stock keeps three days and freezes, and
the paste keeps a week — which is twenty of the twenty-five minutes.
