## Ingredients

- 1 tbsp neutral oil
- 100 g pork mince
- 6 g garlic, finely chopped
- 40 g ya cai
- 1 tsp light soy sauce
- 400 g mixian (2 portions)
- 300 g silken tofu (1 block)
- 2 tbsp kecap manis
- 1 tbsp sesame paste
- 2 tbsp chilli oil
- 1 tbsp black vinegar
- 30 g roasted peanuts, crushed
- 30 g chinese chives, finely chopped

## Method

1. Heat the oil in a frying pan over high heat. Add the pork, press it flat, and fry 3 minutes, breaking it up after the first minute, until it is dry, browned and crumbly.
2. Add the garlic and ya cai and fry 90 seconds, until the pickle smells toasted. Stir in the soy sauce and take the pan off the heat.
3. Warm the silken tofu: sit the unopened tub in a bowl of just-boiled water for 5 minutes, or slide the block into a pan of barely simmering water for 3. It wants to be warm, not hot, and it must not be stirred — the whole point is that it stays in soft curds.
4. Put the mixian in a sieve and pour a kettle of boiling water over it. Shake it dry and divide between two deep bowls.
5. Spoon the warm tofu over the noodles in large curds, breaking it as little as you can. It should cover them completely.
6. Pour the kecap manis over, then the sesame paste, the chilli oil and the vinegar. Spoon the pork over one side. Scatter the peanuts and the chives.
7. Take it to the table and toss it hard, right down to the bottom of the bowl, until the tofu has broken up into the sauce and every strand is beige. This is the eater's job and it is not optional.

## Notes

Sweet and savoury at once, which is the thing about this dish that people either
take to immediately or do not. Yunnan uses 甜酱油 — a local sweet soy nobody
outside the province can buy — and kecap manis is what every cook elsewhere
reaches for instead. It is sweeter and less spiced; two tablespoons is the
amount that reads as a deliberate sweetness rather than a mistake.

Real douhua is fresh soy pudding, set that morning and scooped warm, and it is
not sold in Britain. Warmed silken tofu is the substitution and it is a good
one: the texture is close, and once the bowl is tossed the difference is
invisible. Do not use firm tofu, which stays in cubes and turns this into a
completely different dish.

Warm, not hot. Tofu heated hard weeps and goes grainy, and the water it gives
off thins the sauce. Five minutes in a bowl of boiled water is enough and needs
no attention.

The toss is the recipe. Served untossed this is four separate things in a bowl,
and every mouthful is either bland tofu or a shock of chilli oil. Twenty seconds
of turning it over, from the bottom, makes it one thing — beige, savoury,
faintly sweet, and quite unlike its own ingredients.

Ya cai rather than the wet pickles. It is dry, dark and sweet-salty, and it is
the same ingredient that makes Dan Dan Noodles taste like themselves. Suan cai
would make this sour, which it should not be.

## Storage

The assembled bowl keeps for nothing: the noodles drink the sauce and the tofu
weeps into what is left.

The pork and ya cai topping keeps four days in the fridge and freezes for two
months, and it is worth making three times over — it is the same topping Dan Dan
Noodles wants, with ya cai in both.

Silken tofu does not keep once opened. Half a block left over should be eaten
cold the same day with soy sauce and spring onion, which is a fine thing in its
own right.
