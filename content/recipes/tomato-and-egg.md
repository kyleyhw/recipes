## Ingredients

- 4 eggs
- 0.5 tsp salt
- 2 tbsp neutral oil
- 400 g tomato (3 medium), in wedges
- 1.5 tsp granulated sugar
- 60 ml water
- 1 tsp light soy sauce
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Beat the eggs with half the salt for 20 seconds, until the yolks and whites are one colour and the surface carries a few bubbles.
2. Heat a wok or a 26 cm frying pan over high heat until a bead of egg dropped in sets on contact. Add three quarters of the oil and swirl it round.
3. Pour in the eggs and leave them alone for 10 seconds, until the edge has set and puffed. Then push them through in large folds with a spatula for 20 seconds more and stop while the middle is still glossy and visibly underdone — they will finish in their own heat. Tip them onto a plate.
4. Put the pan back on the heat, add the rest of the oil, then the tomatoes, the sugar and the rest of the salt. Cook over medium-high for 3 minutes, pressing the wedges against the side of the pan every so often, until the skins have loosened and curled back and the flesh has slumped into a thick orange sauce with the pieces still holding their shape.
5. Pour in the water and the soy sauce and simmer for 1 minute, until the sauce coats the back of a spoon and no thin liquid runs when you push it aside.
6. Return the eggs. Fold for 30 seconds, cutting them into the sauce with the edge of the spatula rather than stirring — you want pieces the size of a thumbnail, not shreds. It is done when every piece of egg wears sauce and there is a pool of it left in the pan.
7. Scatter the spring onion over and serve straight away, with rice, and spoon the sauce over the rice.

## Notes

The eggs come out of the pan. That is the whole recipe. Leave them in while the
tomatoes cook down and they will be rubbery brown crumbs in an orange sauce;
take them out at the point where they still look wrong and put them back for
thirty seconds at the end, and they stay soft. Two extra seconds of washing up
buys the entire dish.

Sugar is not optional and it is not there to make the dish sweet. A supermarket
tomato in February is sour and thin, and a teaspoon and a half of sugar across
400 g of them reads as tomato rather than as sugar. Grow your own or buy them in
August and cut it to half a teaspoon — taste the sauce at the end of step 5,
which is the moment to fix it.

Whether to peel the tomatoes is the one real argument about this dish. Peeled —
score a cross in the base, 20 seconds in boiling water, slip the skins off —
gives a smoother sauce and no curls of skin in the bowl. Unpeeled is what almost
everyone actually does, and the skins loosen and roll up into soft ribbons that
nobody minds. Peel them if you are feeding someone who minds.

Four eggs to 400 g of tomatoes is roughly one egg per tomato, which is the ratio
every version of this agrees on. Everything else about it is argued over:
northern cooks use more egg and less liquid and eat it drier, southern ones add
more water and treat it as something to pour over rice. This is nearer the
southern version, because the sauce is the point.

No garlic, no ginger, no oyster sauce. Things get added to this dish constantly
and none of them improve it; a spoonful of ketchup, which sounds like heresy, is
the one addition Chinese home cooks actually make, and it is for colour and acid
when the tomatoes are poor.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge, covered. It reheats better than most egg dishes because
the eggs are already sitting in sauce, which stops them drying — 90 seconds in a
covered bowl in the microwave, or 2 minutes in a pan over low heat with a splash
of water.

It does not freeze. The tomato survives and the egg comes back weeping and
grainy.

Cold from the fridge over hot rice is genuinely good, and is what the last
portion is for.
