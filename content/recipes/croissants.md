## Ingredients

- 500 g bread flour
- 55 g caster sugar
- 11 g salt
- 7 g fast-action dried yeast
- 140 ml milk, cold
- 140 ml water, cold
- 40 g unsalted butter, softened, for the dough
- 280 g unsalted butter, cold, for the block
- 1 egg

## Method

1. Stir the flour, sugar, salt and yeast together in a large bowl, keeping the salt and the yeast on opposite sides until they are through the flour. Add the cold milk, the cold water and the 40 g of softened butter and mix to a rough dough.
2. Knead for 5 minutes only. It should be smooth on the outside and still feel slightly under-worked — the folding does the rest of the gluten development, and a fully kneaded dough fights the rolling pin for two days.
3. Press it into a 20 cm square, wrap it, and refrigerate for 8 hours or overnight. It will rise slowly and that is intended.
4. Make the butter block. Put the 280 g of cold butter between two sheets of baking paper and beat it with a rolling pin into a 17 cm square, 1 cm thick, squaring the edges as you go. It has to be pliable and still cold: bent, it should curve rather than crack or smear. Refrigerate it 30 minutes.
5. Roll the dough into a 34 cm square. Lay the butter block on it at 45 degrees, like a diamond in a square, and fold the four corners of the dough over it to meet in the middle, pinching the seams shut. No butter should be showing.
6. Roll the parcel out to 20 by 60 cm, always rolling away from you and turning it 180 degrees rather than sideways. Fold it in three like a letter. That is one turn. Wrap and refrigerate 60 minutes.
7. Do two more turns the same way, chilling 60 minutes between each, always starting with the open edge on your right. Three letter folds give 27 layers of butter, which is the number this pastry has always had. Refrigerate for a final 60 minutes after the third.
8. Roll the block out to 20 by 60 cm and 4 mm thick. Trim the edges square — sealed edges cannot rise. Cut 12 triangles with a 10 cm base and a 20 cm height, working alternately point-up and point-down along the strip and cutting each in one clean stroke.
9. Cut a 1.5 cm notch in the middle of each base, stretch the triangle gently to 25 cm long, and roll it up from the base, keeping the tip underneath. Do not curve the ends inwards; an all-butter croissant is baked straight, and the crescent shape traditionally means margarine.
10. Set them on two lined trays, 6 on each, 6 cm apart. Beat the egg and brush them thinly, avoiding the cut sides. Prove at room temperature, uncovered, for 2 hours and 30 minutes. They are ready when they have nearly doubled, jiggle as a whole when the tray is shaken, and the layers are visible at the cut ends. They must not go above 26 °C or the butter melts out.
11. Heat the oven to 220 °C. Brush a second thin coat of egg, put both trays in, and drop the heat to 200 °C immediately. Bake 18 minutes, swapping the shelves at 10 minutes, until deep mahogany brown — much darker than looks right, which is where the flavour is.
12. Cool on a rack for 20 minutes. Cut one open: the crumb should be an open honeycomb of cavities with no dense stripe in the middle.

## Notes

Everything in this recipe is a temperature problem. The dough and the butter
have to be the same firmness at every roll, and the enemy at each stage is
warmth. If at any point butter starts to come through the surface or the dough
resists and springs back, stop and refrigerate for 30 minutes; nothing is lost
by waiting and everything is lost by pushing on.

Butter with 82% fat or more if you can get it — French or a good British
block. Ordinary butter carries more water, which turns to steam and tears the
layers rather than separating them. Never use a spreadable or a baking block.

Three letter folds, not four, and not a book fold. Twenty-seven layers is the
number, and more layers is not better: past about 50 the sheets are too thin to
stay separate and the crumb goes bready.

The prove is the step people cut short. Two and a half hours at 24 °C is a long
time to look at unbaked pastry, but an under-proved croissant leaks butter into
the tray and bakes dense. The jiggle test is the reliable one — the tray shakes
and the whole croissant wobbles like set jelly.

Bake them dark. A pale croissant is a raw croissant; the sugar and the milk
solids need that colour to taste of anything, and it is the commonest
difference between a home batch and a bakery one.

Yesterday's are what Almond Croissants are made from, and they are better for
the purpose than today's.

## Storage

They are at their best between one and four hours out of the oven and they are
noticeably past it by the next morning. This is not a keeping food and no
amount of technique changes that.

A day-old croissant comes back most of the way with 5 minutes at 180 °C, which
drives the moisture back out of the layers. A two-day-old one does not, and
should become an almond croissant instead.

Freeze them the day they are baked, bagged with the air pressed out, for a
month; 8 minutes at 180 °C from frozen. Better still, freeze them shaped and
unproved on the tray, bag them for a month, and prove from frozen for 4 hours
before baking as written — which is how you get croissants on a morning without
having started two days earlier.
