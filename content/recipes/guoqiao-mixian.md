## Ingredients

- 400 g bone-in chicken thighs
- 1200 ml water
- 20 g ginger, in thick slices
- 30 g spring onions
- 1 tsp salt
- 0.5 tsp white pepper
- 3 tbsp chicken fat
- 150 g chicken breast, sliced 2 mm thick
- 100 g white fish fillets, sliced 2 mm thick
- 6 quail eggs
- 40 g chinese chives, in 4 cm lengths
- 50 g pea shoots
- 60 g bean sprouts
- 400 g mixian (2 portions)

## Method

1. Put the chicken thighs in a pan with the water, ginger and spring onions. Bring to a bare simmer and skim the grey foam off for the first 5 minutes.
2. Simmer, barely moving, for 55 minutes. Lift the chicken out, pull the meat off the bones in strips, and set it aside. Strain the broth — you should have about 900 ml — and season it with the salt and white pepper. Taste it: it must be assertively seasoned, because nothing else in the bowl is.
3. Slice the raw chicken breast and the fish as thinly as your knife allows, and lay them on separate plates. Both are easier half-frozen; 30 minutes in the freezer makes a real difference. Arrange the quail eggs, chives, pea shoots, bean sprouts and pulled thigh meat on the same plates.
4. Warm two deep bowls with boiling water and pour it out. Cold china is the commonest way this dish fails.
5. Bring the broth to a rolling boil. Meanwhile put the mixian in a sieve and pour a kettle of boiling water over it to loosen and heat it, then divide it between two other bowls.
6. Divide the chicken fat between the warmed serving bowls, ladle the boiling broth in on top of it, and carry them to the table at once. The fat spreads into an unbroken film in a few seconds.
7. At the table: raw chicken first, stirred under and left 30 seconds; then the fish, 20 seconds; then the quail eggs cracked straight in; then the vegetables; then the noodles last. Eat as you go.

## Notes

**The fat is not a garnish and not optional.** A film of rendered chicken fat on
the surface stops the broth losing heat by evaporation, and it is the only
reason a bowl of soup carried from a stove can still be near boiling two minutes
later — hot enough to cook a two-millimetre slice of raw chicken through. Skim
it off and this becomes a bowl of warm soup with raw meat in it, which is a
different and worse proposition. The legend attached to the dish is about a wife
carrying lunch across a bridge to her studying husband and finding the oil had
kept it hot; whatever the truth of that, the physics is right.

**Everything hinges on the broth being at a rolling boil when it is poured.**
Warm the bowls, boil the broth hard, pour, and go. If the table is more than
twenty seconds away, cook the chicken slices in the pan for ten seconds first
and be honest about it rather than serving underdone chicken.

Two millimetres, and no thicker. This is the whole technique of the dish. A
slice that thick cooks through in half a minute in liquid at 95 degrees; four
millimetres does not, and there is no way to tell from the outside. Half-freeze
the meat and use your sharpest knife.

Yunnan ham — 宣威火腿 — is in the classic version and is not buyable outside
China. It is left out rather than substituted; prosciutto is the nearest thing
and tastes wrong here, and the dish is complete without it.

Nine hundred millilitres of broth for two is a lot, and it should be. Guoqiao
mixian arrives in a bowl big enough to look absurd, because the volume of hot
liquid is what carries the heat — a small bowl cools too fast to do its job.

## Storage

The broth keeps three days in the fridge and freezes for three months, and it is
the only part worth keeping. Make it the day before; the dish is then twenty
minutes of slicing.

The assembled bowl keeps for nothing at all. The noodles swell, and reheating a
bowl that has already cooked raw chicken in it means boiling it hard, which
turns the chicken to string.

The raw sliced chicken and fish keep one day in the fridge, covered, and no
longer. Slice them the day you eat them if you can.
