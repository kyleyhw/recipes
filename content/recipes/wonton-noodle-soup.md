## Ingredients

- 150 g prawns, peeled and deveined
- 150 g fatty pork mince
- 1 tbsp shaoxing wine
- 1 tsp light soy sauce
- 1 tsp cornflour
- 0.5 tsp granulated sugar
- 0.5 tsp white pepper
- 1 tsp sesame oil
- 10 g ginger, finely chopped
- 120 g wonton wrappers (20 sheets)
- 900 ml chicken stock
- 150 g choy sum, in 6 cm lengths
- 120 g egg noodles
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Chop half the prawns into 5 mm pieces and leave the rest whole — a wonton wants both, so there is something to bite as well as something that binds.
2. Put the pork, all the prawns, the shaoxing wine, soy sauce, cornflour, sugar, white pepper, sesame oil and ginger in a bowl. Stir hard in one direction for 60 seconds, until the mixture clings together and lifts off the spoon in one mass.
3. Keep the wrappers under a damp cloth; the top sheet cracks within a minute of being uncovered. Put a heaped teaspoon of filling in the middle of one, wet two adjoining edges with a fingertip, fold into a triangle and press the air out from the filling outwards. Then bring the two long corners together round your finger and pinch. Twenty wontons, and the first three will be ugly.
4. Bring a large pan of water to a rolling boil — water, not stock. Drop the wontons in and stir once so they do not stick to the base. Boil 4 minutes, until they have risen, the wrappers have gone translucent and slippery, and a prawn shows pink through the skin.
5. Meanwhile bring the stock to a bare simmer in a second pan and cook the choy sum in it for 2 minutes, until the stems still snap.
6. Cook the noodles in a third pan, or in the wonton water once the wontons are out — 90 seconds for thin egg noodles — and drain well.
7. Put the noodles in two deep warmed bowls, ladle the stock and greens over, and lift the wontons in with a slotted spoon. Scatter the spring onion and eat at once.

## Notes

Boil the wontons in plain water and never in the broth. They shed a great deal
of starch and a little filling, and a clear soup boiled full of wontons turns
cloudy and slightly gluey. Keeping them apart is the single thing that separates
a good bowl from a muddy one, and it costs one extra pan.

Half the prawns chopped, half whole. All chopped and the filling is a paste; all
whole and it falls apart when you bite it.

Press the air out. An air pocket expands in boiling water and bursts the wrapper,
and you lose the filling into the pan. Press from the filling outward to the
edge before the final seal.

Under a damp cloth, always. Wonton wrappers are thin and dry out faster than any
pastry — an uncovered stack is unusable in ten minutes and will not seal.

A tiny amount of filling, by Western instinct. A heaped teaspoon looks mean in a
sheet that size and it is right: a Cantonese wonton is mostly a slippery tail of
wrapper with a small dense knot at one end, and overfilled ones burst and eat
like dumplings.

The broth is the weak point of every home version, this one included. A shop
simmers pork bones and dried flounder for hours and there is no shortcut worth
pretending about — good chicken stock with the greens cooked in it is honest and
is not the same thing. A teaspoon of dried shrimp simmered in it for ten minutes
gets a little closer.

Make them all and freeze most. Twenty wontons is an hour's work the first time
and ten minutes the fifth, and they cook from frozen with a minute added.

## Storage

Uncooked wontons freeze for three months and are the whole reason to make them.
Freeze them apart on a lined tray for an hour, then bag them; boiled from frozen
they need 5 minutes rather than 4. Do not thaw them first, which glues them into
a sheet.

Cooked wontons keep a day in the fridge and reheat badly — the wrappers go
flabby. Assembled soup keeps for nothing at all.

The stock keeps three days and freezes for two months.
