## Ingredients

- 30 g salted fish
- 3 tbsp water
- 1 tsp cornflour
- 1 tbsp shaoxing wine
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 0.25 tsp white pepper
- 0.5 tsp sesame oil
- 250 g fatty pork mince
- 20 g ginger, finely minced
- 15 g spring onions, green parts only

## Method

1. Rinse the salted fish under cold water, pat it dry, and pull out any bones you can feel. Cut it into pieces the size of a thumbnail. If it came packed in oil, drain it; if it is the dry kind, leave it as it is.
2. Put the water, cornflour, shaoxing wine, sugar, white pepper and sesame oil in a mixing bowl and whisk for 20 seconds, until no cornflour is left on the bottom.
3. Add the pork and the ginger — all 20 g of it, which is more than the other patties take and is there to stand up to the fish. Stir hard with chopsticks in one direction only for 60 to 90 seconds, and stop the moment it clings to the chopsticks in one mass.
4. Put a steaming rack in a wok or a wide pan, add water to just below it, and bring it to a rolling boil.
5. Spread the meat into a shallow heatproof dish in an even layer 1.5 cm thick. Lay the pieces of salted fish over the surface, spaced apart, and press each one in until it sits level. On top, not mixed in: the flavour travels down through the patty as it steams, and a fish chopped through the mixture makes every mouthful the same and the dish exhausting.
6. Set the dish on the rack, cover with a tight lid, and steam over high heat for 12 minutes. It is done when the patty has shrunk from the sides of the dish, clear broth has gathered around it, and the fish has gone opaque and soft enough to break with a spoon.
7. Scatter the spring onion over the top and take it to the table in the dish it steamed in, with plain rice. Break a little of the fish into each portion rather than serving it in pieces.

## Notes

Worth saying plainly: Chinese-style salted fish is classified by the IARC as a
Group 1 carcinogen, on evidence linking heavy consumption — particularly in
early childhood, in southern China and Hong Kong — to nasopharyngeal cancer. It
is a traditional food eaten in small quantities and occasionally, which is how
this recipe uses it: 30 g between two people, a few times a year rather than
weekly. That is the honest version of the information, and what to do with it is
yours.

Two kinds are sold. Mui heong is fermented before salting, soft, and so pungent
that a jar of it announces itself through the lid; the firm kind is drier,
cleaner and closer to anchovy. Either works here. Start with the firm one.

No salt and no soy anywhere. The fish carries about 1800 mg of sodium in the
30 g this uses.

Twenty grams of ginger rather than fifteen. It is doing real work here — the
fish is oily and strong, and the ginger is what keeps it savoury rather than
rank.

The technique is the same in every steamed patty here and is written out at
length in Steamed Pork Patty with Preserved Bean Curd: stir until it clings and
no further, 1.5 cm thick, 12 minutes over a rolling boil.

Optional, over the rice: Lee Kum Kee's seasoned soy sauce for seafood, or Roast
Meat Soy Dressing if there is no bottle.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge, in the dish, covered. The smell gets into everything in
there, so this is one to keep in a box with a lid rather than under film.

Steam it again for 5 minutes. Do not microwave this one uncovered unless you
want the kitchen to smell of it for the rest of the evening.

The raw mixture freezes for a month, but freeze it without the fish and lay that
on after defrosting — salted fish frozen in contact with wet mince goes soapy.
