## Ingredients

- 300 g japanese short-grain rice
- 20 g dried shiitake mushrooms (5)
- 100 ml water
- 240 ml dashi
- 2 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1 tbsp mirin
- 1 tbsp sake
- 50 g aburaage (2 sheets), in thin strips
- 100 g chicken thigh, in 1.5 cm pieces
- 60 g carrots (1), in fine matchsticks
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Put the shiitake in the water and leave 20 minutes, until soft all the way through with no hard core. Lift them out, squeeze them over the bowl, cut off the stems and slice the caps thinly. Keep the soaking water — it is a third of the flavour.
2. Rinse the rice in a bowl of cold water, swirling with your hand and pouring the cloudy water off, four or five times until it runs nearly clear. Drain it in a sieve for 20 minutes.
3. Put the aburaage in a sieve and pour a kettle of boiling water over it. This takes off the oil it was fried in, which otherwise sits on top of the finished rice.
4. Tip the drained rice into a heavy 20 cm pan with a tight lid. Pour in the dashi, the shiitake water, the soy sauce, mirin and sake — about 400 ml of liquid in total. Shake the pan flat so the rice is level.
5. Lay the shiitake, aburaage, chicken and carrot on top in an even layer. **Do not stir.** The rice has to cook in liquid, not in a mixture, and anything folded through it stops the grains at the bottom from getting what they need.
6. Bring to a boil over high heat, uncovered, until the surface is bubbling hard — about 3 minutes.
7. Put the lid on, turn the heat to its lowest, and cook 12 minutes without lifting it. There is no way to check and there is nothing to check.
8. Take the pan off the heat and leave it, lid still on, for 10 minutes.
9. Take the lid off, cut down through the rice with a wet rice paddle or a spatula and fold everything together in four or five strokes, lifting rather than stirring. Scatter the spring onion over and serve.

## Notes

This is where a whole packet of dashi belongs. Every other use in this
collection puts it in a bowl of soup where it is diluted; here 240 ml of it is
absorbed into 300 g of rice and it is the only liquid the grain ever sees.

**Never stir before cooking, and barely stir after.** Both matter and for
different reasons. Before: solids folded through the rice trap steam and give an
unevenly cooked pot with a wet layer at the bottom. After: short-grain rice
bruises, and stirring it crushes the grains into paste. Cut down and lift.

The liquid is 400 ml to 300 g of rice, which is a little more than the 1.1 times
by volume plain rice wants. The extra is because the toppings take some up.
Measure it as a total rather than adding the seasonings on top of a full measure
of dashi — soy sauce is liquid, and four tablespoons unaccounted for is the
difference between rice and porridge.

Rinsing and draining are not optional with japonica. The loose starch on the
surface is what makes a gluey pot, and rice put in wet from the sieve carries
water that nothing has measured. Twenty minutes in the sieve is the standard and
it happens while the shiitake soak.

Blanch the aburaage. It is deep-fried and holds a good deal of oil; a kettle of
boiling water takes most of it out and stops a slick forming on the surface of
the rice.

What goes in it is the loosest part of the dish. Chicken and shiitake is the
commonest, and it takes almost any combination of firm vegetables, mushrooms,
and something salty: burdock, bamboo shoot, sweetcorn on its own in summer,
or nothing but shiitake and aburaage for a vegetarian version — which needs
kombu dashi rather than the packet, since the packet is a fish stock.

A rice cooker does this better than a pan, if you have one: the same
ingredients, the same "do not stir", and the ordinary white rice programme.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge, and cool it fast — spread it in a shallow dish rather
than leaving the pot on the hob. Cooked rice left warm is the one genuine
food-safety risk in this collection; the `cooked rice` note in the ingredient
library has the reason.

It reheats well from cold: a splash of water, a lid, and 3 minutes over low
heat, or 90 seconds in a microwave. Better than most rice dishes do, because the
grains are already seasoned and a little fat from the aburaage keeps them
separate.

Freezes for a month, portioned and wrapped while still warm so the steam stays
in, and reheats from frozen straight into a microwave. Onigiri made from it
freeze the same way and are the reason to make a large pot.
