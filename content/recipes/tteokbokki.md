## Ingredients

- 600 ml water
- 15 g dried anchovies, heads and guts removed
- 5 g dried kelp
- 45 g gochujang
- 1 tbsp gochugaru
- 1 tbsp granulated sugar
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 3 cloves garlic, minced
- 400 g tteok
- 100 g korean fish cakes, cut into triangles
- 20 g spring onions, cut into 4 cm lengths
- 2 eggs
- 1 tsp toasted sesame seeds

## Method

1. Snap the head off each anchovy and pinch the black gut out of the belly. Toast them in a dry 20 cm saucepan over medium heat for 2 minutes, shaking the pan twice. They stop smelling of the sea and start smelling toasted and nutty, which is the point of the step — the gut and the raw smell are what make anchovy stock taste muddy.
2. Pour in the water, add the kelp, bring to a boil over high heat and boil uncovered for 15 minutes. Lift the kelp out at the 10 minute mark: held past that it goes slippery and gives the stock a faint bitterness. Strain the rest through a sieve and throw the anchovies away. You will be left with 500 ml — top it up from the kettle if the pan has given you less.
3. While the stock boils, put the tteok in a bowl of warm tap water for 20 minutes and pull the cylinders apart with your fingers. Straight from the fridge they are hard and welded together, and 20 minutes is the difference between a soft chew and a raw core. Fresh tteok from a chiller needs none of this; frozen tteok goes in still frozen and takes 2 minutes longer in the pan.
4. Lower the eggs into a small pan of boiling water and boil for 9 minutes, then cool them under the cold tap for 1 minute and peel. The yolk is set through with no grey ring.
5. Whisk the gochujang, gochugaru, sugar, light soy sauce and garlic into the strained stock until no lumps of paste are left on the whisk.
6. Pour it into a 24 cm frying pan — wide and shallow, not a saucepan, because the sauce has to reduce — and bring to a boil. Add the drained tteok and the fish cakes.
7. Simmer for 10 minutes over medium heat, stirring every minute so nothing sticks. The sauce goes from thin and orange to thick and dark red, and it is done when a spoon dragged across the pan leaves a track that closes slowly rather than at once. A cylinder squeezed against the side of the pan gives, then springs back.
8. Scatter the spring onions over and cook 2 minutes more, until they have gone limp and dark green.
9. Serve in bowls with a halved egg on top and the sesame seeds over. Eat it hot — this is the whole of its good window.

## Notes

Three things come from a Korean or a general Asian supermarket and nowhere
else: tteok in a 500 g vacuum pack labelled tteokbokki-tteok, fish cake in flat
fried sheets in the freezer cabinet, and gochujang in a red tub. None of them
has a British supermarket substitute worth making. Japanese satsuma-age stands
in for the fish cake sheets exactly, if that is the shop you are standing in.

The stock is not optional and it is not water. Anchovy and kelp is what
separates this from the sauce on its own, which tastes of sweet chilli paste
and nothing else. If you would rather not deal with whole anchovies, the same
Korean shops sell teabag-style broth packs (다시팩) — one pack to 600 ml, boiled
15 minutes and lifted out. The figures on this page are for the loose version.

A bowl is 700 kcal and about 2,000 mg of sodium, which is 85% of a day's
reference intake, and unlike a soup you eat all of it — the sauce reduces onto
the rice cakes rather than staying in the pan. It arrives in four parts: the
gochujang, the tablespoon of light soy sauce, the fish cakes, and, unexpectedly,
the rice cake itself, which is salted in the making and accounts for about a
fifth. Dropping the soy sauce entirely and cutting the paste to 35 g takes the
bowl to roughly 1,500 mg, and it loses no heat if the gochugaru goes up to
1.5 tbsp to make up the difference. Worth doing if this is dinner, which is
what the quantities here are: a meal for two rather than the snack the name
implies, and a side for four.

Gochujang is roughly a third sugar by weight, which is why one tablespoon of
added sugar is enough here and why recipes calling for three are making
something closer to a sweet.

The two variations worth knowing. Rose tteokbokki: 100 ml of double cream
stirred in at the end of step 7, which mutes the chilli and turns the sauce
pink and rich. And a slice of processed cheese laid over the top off the heat,
which is what a Korean snack bar does and is better than it sounds.

Ramyeon noodles are the other standard addition — a block dropped in for the
last 4 minutes with an extra 150 ml of stock, at which point the dish is
rabokki and feeds three.

## Storage

Eat it the day it is made. The sauce keeps for three days in the fridge and
the rice cakes do not: rice starch hardens fastest just above freezing, so
overnight in the fridge turns a soft cylinder into a chalky one, and no amount
of reheating brings it back. This is the rare dish where the leftovers are
genuinely worse than nothing.

If you have to keep it, reheat within a few hours rather than the next day, in
a pan with 3 tbsp of water over medium heat for 4 minutes, stirring — the water
replaces what the sauce lost and the tteok resoftens while it is still only
hours old. A microwave dries the outside and leaves the middle cold.

Do not freeze the finished dish. Uncooked tteok freezes perfectly, so freeze
what is left of the bag instead and make it again.
