## Ingredients

- 450 g silken tofu
- 2 tbsp peanut oil
- 150 g fatty pork mince
- 1 tbsp ground bean sauce
- 1 tsp doubanjiang
- 3 cloves garlic, minced
- 15 g ginger, minced
- 250 ml chicken stock
- 1 tbsp oyster sauce
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1 tsp dark soy sauce
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 0.25 tsp white pepper
- 1.5 tbsp cornflour
- 4 spring onions, thinly sliced
- 1 tsp sesame oil

## Method

1. Cut the tofu into 2.5 cm cubes. Mix the cornflour with 4 tbsp of cold water and leave it beside the hob.
2. Bring 1 litre of water to a bare simmer in a 20 cm saucepan with 1 tsp of salt — bubbles rising in ones, not a rolling boil. Slide the tofu in and leave it 2 minutes, then drain. This firms the cubes enough to survive the pan; without it, half of them break up into the gravy.
3. Heat the peanut oil in a 28 cm wok over high heat for 90 seconds, until it shimmers and runs like water.
4. Add the pork. Cook for 4 minutes, pressing it flat and then breaking it up, until the grains are browned and separate.
5. Turn the heat down to medium. Add the ground bean sauce, the doubanjiang, the garlic and the ginger. Fry for 60 seconds, until the paste darkens a shade and smells savoury rather than raw.
6. Pour in the stock, then add the oyster sauce, both soy sauces, the sugar and the white pepper. Bring to a simmer and cook for 60 seconds, until the gravy is glossy brown rather than streaked.
7. Slide in the tofu. Do not stir it: push the cubes under with the back of the ladle and shake the wok instead. Simmer for 4 minutes, until every cube has taken the colour of the gravy.
8. Stir the cornflour mixture and add half of it. Simmer 30 seconds, then add the rest. Cook 30 seconds more, until the gravy coats the back of the ladle and a channel drawn through it with the ladle closes slowly rather than at once. It should be thick enough to sit on rice without running off the plate.
9. Add the spring onions and the sesame oil, shake the wok twice, and take it off the heat. Serve over rice.

## Notes

This is a draft: not yet cooked here.

What makes it the Cantonese one rather than the Sichuan one is mostly what is
missing. There is no sichuan pepper, so there is no numbing — the defining
sensation of the Chengdu dish is simply absent, on purpose, and adding a
quarter teaspoon at the end is the single change that undoes this. The chilli
is a teaspoon of doubanjiang for colour and background rather than 30 g of it
for heat. The savoury weight comes instead from ground bean sauce and oyster
sauce, which are sweeter and rounder than fermented broad beans and chilli.

And the gravy is thicker on purpose. This is a dish served over rice — 麻婆豆腐飯,
a plate of rice with the tofu spooned over it — so the sauce has to sit on top
rather than run to the edge of the plate. That is why there is half again as
much cornflour here as in the Sichuan version.

Ground bean sauce is min si jeung, the smooth savoury soybean sauce in a jar.
It is not sweet bean sauce, which is darker and much sweeter, and it is not
doubanjiang. If you cannot find it, 1 tbsp of miso plus half a teaspoon of soy
is closer than either.

Dark soy is for colour, not for salt. A teaspoon takes the gravy from grey-brown
to the deep brown the dish is supposed to be, and more than that starts to taste
of molasses.

The Sichuan original is in this collection too, under Mapo Tofu: beef, 30 g of
chilli bean paste, black beans and a proper numbing dust of sichuan pepper. They
are two dishes that share a name, rather than one being a weaker copy of the
other.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge, covered. Reheat in a frying pan over medium heat with
2 tbsp of water, shaking rather than stirring, for 3 minutes — the gravy will
have set solid when cold and loosens back with the water.

It keeps better than the Sichuan version because the cornflour holds the gravy
together, but the tofu still softens overnight. Do not freeze it: silken tofu
comes out of a freezer as sponge.
