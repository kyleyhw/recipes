## Ingredients

- 320 g puff pastry (1 sheet)
- 100 g caster sugar
- 100 ml water
- 300 ml evaporated milk
- 1 tbsp cornflour
- 6 egg yolks
- 1 tsp vanilla extract

## Method

1. Use an all-butter sheet if you can get one — it browns further and tastes of something. Take it out of the fridge 10 minutes before you start; it should bend without cracking and still feel cold.
2. Put the sugar and the water in a small pan over medium heat. Stir until the sugar has dissolved, then stop stirring and let it come to a boil for 2 minutes, until it is clear and very slightly syrupy. Take it off the heat.
3. Whisk the cornflour into 4 tablespoons of the evaporated milk in a bowl until smooth, then whisk in the rest of the milk and the egg yolks.
4. Pour the hot syrup into the yolk mixture in a thin stream, whisking the whole time. Adding it the other way round, or all at once, scrambles the yolks.
5. Return everything to the pan over low heat and stir constantly with a spatula, scraping the base and the corners, for 4 to 6 minutes. It is done when it coats the back of the spatula and a finger drawn through leaves a clean line that does not run in. Do not let it boil. Stir in the vanilla, pour it through a sieve into a jug, and leave it to cool for 15 minutes.
6. Heat the oven to 230 °C, with a shelf in the top third.
7. Roll the pastry sheet up into a tight log along its long edge. Cut the log into 12 discs, each about 2 cm thick. Stand each disc cut side up in a hole of a 12-hole muffin tin.
8. Dip a thumb in cold water and press each disc from the centre outward and up the sides, turning the tin as you go, until the pastry lines the hole with a rim standing 2 mm above it. The spiral you cut should still be visible in the base — those are the layers, and they are what rises.
9. Pour the cooled custard in until each case is four fifths full. Do not fill to the rim; it climbs as it bakes.
10. Bake for 18 to 20 minutes. They are done when the pastry is deep gold and crisp underneath, the custard has puffed up domed and settled again, and the surface carries black or dark brown blisters. The blisters are the point and are not burning.
11. Leave in the tin for 5 minutes, then lift them out with a spoon onto a rack. Eat them warm, within the hour, dusted with cinnamon if you like.

## Notes

Evaporated milk stands in for the cream a Lisbon pastel de nata uses, and it is
the Macau version rather than a compromise — Andrew Stow, who took the Portuguese
tart to Macau in the late 1980s, was working with what a Cantonese kitchen had.
It gives a lighter, milkier custard with a cleaner egg flavour and rather less
fat. If you want the Lisbon one, swap in 300 ml of double cream and expect
something much richer.

The oven must be as hot as it goes. Those blisters are sugar and protein
scorching in seconds at the surface while the custard beneath is still setting,
and at 200 °C you get a pale, evenly set tart that is perfectly nice and is not
this. If your oven tops out at 220 °C, put the tin on the top shelf and give it
2 more minutes.

Rolled and cut, never rolled flat and stamped. A stamped-out circle has its
layers lying horizontally and bakes into a flat biscuit; a cut disc has them
spiralling, so they stand upright and fan out as they rise. This is the single
biggest visible difference between a good and a bad egg tart, and it costs
nothing.

Six yolks and no whites. The whites would set firm and rubbery and dull the
colour, and the point of this custard is that it stays quivering. What to do
with six egg whites is a real question — meringue, or the Tiramisu on this site,
which needs four.

No tin is stated in the front matter and that is deliberate. A cake tin scales
by area with the serving count, which is why the panel does it; a muffin tin
scales by how many holes you fill, so doubling this means two tins or two
batches, not a deeper hole.

Hong Kong's egg tart is a different pastry — shortcrust, smooth yellow top, no
blisters — and the two get confused constantly. This is the Macau one.

## Storage

Best within an hour of coming out of the oven, warm, and genuinely worse cold.

Two days in an airtight tin at room temperature, not the fridge, which turns the
pastry damp within a few hours. Bring them back at 200 °C for 5 minutes on a
tray, which re-crisps the base almost completely; a microwave makes the pastry
soft and there is no way back from it.

The baked tarts freeze for a month. Reheat from frozen at 180 °C for 12 minutes.
The custard alone keeps three days covered in the fridge and is worth making
ahead — it has to cool before it goes into the pastry anyway.
