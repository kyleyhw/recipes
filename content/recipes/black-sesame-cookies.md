## Ingredients

- 90 g black sesame seeds
- 150 g unsalted butter, softened
- 120 g caster sugar
- 0.75 tsp salt
- 1 egg
- 200 g all-purpose flour
- 0.5 tsp bicarbonate of soda

## Method

1. Toast the sesame seeds in a dry 24 cm frying pan over medium heat for 3 minutes, shaking constantly. They start to pop and the kitchen fills with the smell; that smell is the point of the recipe and it appears all at once. Tip them straight out of the pan onto a cold plate — left in it they carry on cooking and go bitter.
2. Keep back 2 tbsp of the seeds. Grind the rest in a spice grinder or a mortar for 2 minutes, until they have gone from dry grit to a dark, slightly oily paste that clumps when pressed. This takes longer than it seems it should and stopping early is the commonest failure: unground seeds pass straight through the cookie and take their flavour with them.
3. Beat the butter, sugar and salt with an electric whisk for 3 minutes, until pale and light and holding a soft peak on a lifted beater.
4. Beat in the sesame paste for 1 minute. The mixture turns an even slate grey with no lighter streaks.
5. Beat in the egg for 30 seconds.
6. Sift the flour and bicarbonate of soda over the bowl and fold with a spatula until the last dry streak goes, no more than 15 turns. Fold in the reserved whole seeds with 5 more.
7. Cover and chill for 1 hour, until a spoon pushed into the dough leaves a trench that holds its edges.
8. Heat the oven to 175 °C and line two baking trays.
9. Roll 18 balls of 35 g and set 9 on each tray, 5 cm apart. Do not flatten them.
10. Bake for 13 minutes, swapping the shelves at 7 minutes. Colour tells you nothing here — the dough is already dark — so go by the edges, which should be set and matt while the middle is still soft and faintly glossy.
11. Leave them on the trays for 10 minutes to set, then move them to a rack.

## Notes

Black sesame, not white, and the difference is not the colour. Black seeds keep
their hulls, which is where the bitterness and most of the aroma live, and a
cookie made with the white toasted sesame seeds elsewhere in this collection
tastes mildly nutty and nothing like this. Do not substitute.

Grinding is the whole recipe. What you are after is a paste, not a powder: the
oil has to come out of the seeds for the flavour to get into the butter. A
spice grinder does it in 2 minutes, a mortar in 5 with real effort, and a food
processor never quite does — the quantity is too small for the blade to catch.

Ready-made black sesame paste from a Chinese supermarket will work at 100 g in
place of the ground seeds, but read the jar first: most of what is sold in the
drinks aisle is a sweetened powder mix, and using it makes a sugary cookie with
a grey tinge.

The salt is three-quarters of a teaspoon, which is high, and it is doing the
same job as the salt in the Chocolate Chip Cookies — holding back the sweetness
so the sesame reads as savoury and toasted rather than as a dessert flavour.

Two tablespoons of the seeds go in whole at the end. They do almost nothing for
flavour and everything for the look and the crunch, and they are the reason the
finished cookie is visibly what it is.

## Storage

Five days in a tin at room temperature. They go soft after the second day,
which suits them — the middle turns fudgy while the edge stays crisp. Four
minutes at 160 °C returns the crispness if that is what you want.

Ground sesame is half oil and oil is what goes rancid, so these keep less well
than a plain butter cookie despite being drier. Past a week they take on a
sharp, painty edge and should be thrown out rather than refreshed.

The rolled dough balls freeze on a tray in 2 hours and keep 3 months bagged.
Bake from frozen at 175 °C for 15 minutes. This is the better way to keep them,
because the flavour that fades is in the dough rather than in the baking.
