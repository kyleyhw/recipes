## Ingredients

- 200 g duck breast, skin on
- 0.25 tsp salt
- 120 g leek, in 3-4 cm lengths
- 800 ml dashi
- 3 tbsp light soy sauce
- 2 tbsp mirin
- 1 tbsp sake
- 200 g soba
- 10 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Score the duck skin in a 1 cm diagonal lattice, cutting through the skin and the fat but not into the meat. Season both sides with the salt.
2. Put the breast skin side down in a cold, dry frying pan and only then turn the heat to medium-low. Cook 8 minutes without moving it. The fat renders out slowly and the skin goes from soft and pale to thin, brown and crisp — starting from cold is what makes that happen rather than the skin seizing and the fat staying in.
3. Turn the breast over, cook 2 minutes, and lift it onto a board to rest. Do not wash the pan.
4. Stand the leek pieces on their cut faces in the duck fat and cook 3 minutes without moving them, until the faces are blackened at the edges. Turn and give them 2 minutes more.
5. Pour in the dashi, soy sauce, mirin and sake, bring to a simmer and cook 5 minutes, skimming the foam. Meanwhile slice the rested duck 5 mm thick, across the grain.
6. Cook the soba in a large pan of boiling water — 4 to 5 minutes, or as the packet says — then drain and rinse under cold running water for 20 seconds, working the strands with your hands until they stop feeling slimy. Drain hard.
7. Add the sliced duck to the simmering broth and cook 60 seconds, no longer — it is already cooked and this is only to warm it through.
8. Put the soba in two deep warmed bowls, ladle the broth and leek over, and lay the duck on top. Scatter the spring onion and eat at once.

## Notes

Cold pan, skin down, low heat. This is the only way to render a duck breast
properly and it goes against every instinct about searing meat: a hot pan
contracts the skin, traps the fat inside it and gives you a chewy grey layer.
Eight slow minutes gives crisp skin and three tablespoons of duck fat in the
pan, which is what the leek then chars in and what makes this dish worth the
duck.

Rinse the soba even though it goes into a hot bowl. Buckwheat noodles shed far
more surface starch than udon and it makes the broth cloudy and slightly
claggy; twenty seconds under a cold tap fixes it, and the hot broth brings them
straight back up to temperature.

Sixty seconds for the duck and not a moment more. It came out of the pan at
medium and it will carry on cooking in the broth — this step exists to warm it,
not to cook it, and duck taken past medium in a soup is grey and tough.

This is the soba half of Tori Nanban Udon, and the pair is worth having: same
leek, same nanban idea, same dashi-soy-mirin broth, and a completely different
bowl. Duck and buckwheat are heavier and darker; chicken and wheat are lighter
and rounder. The duck version is the older one.

Save the rendered fat. Pour what is left after step 4 into a jar — it keeps six
months in the fridge and is the best fat there is for roast potatoes.

Cheap soba is mostly wheat, and it matters here more than in a cold dish: a
noodle at 30% buckwheat tastes of very little against duck. Look at the packet
and buy the one with buckwheat first in the list.

## Storage

The broth with its leek keeps three days in the fridge and freezes for two
months. Duck does not keep well in liquid — it goes from medium to grey — so
slice only what you are eating and keep the rest of the breast whole and
separate, where it will keep two days.

Soba is the least forgiving noodle here. Cooked, it is good for an hour and then
stiffens and goes chalky, and it cannot be revived. Cook it to order, always.
