## Ingredients

- 450 g silken tofu
- 2 tbsp peanut oil
- 150 g fatty pork mince
- 30 g doubanjiang
- 1 tbsp fermented black beans
- 2 tsp sichuan chilli powder
- 3 cloves garlic, minced
- 15 g ginger, minced
- 250 ml chicken stock
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1 tbsp cornflour
- 4 spring onions, green parts only, cut into 3 cm lengths
- 1 tsp ground sichuan pepper

## Method

1. Cut the tofu into 2.5 cm cubes. Rinse the black beans under cold water for 10 seconds and chop them roughly. Mix the cornflour with 3 tbsp of cold water and leave it beside the hob.
2. Bring 1 litre of water to a bare simmer in a 20 cm saucepan with 1 tsp of salt — bubbles rising in ones, not a rolling boil. Slide the tofu in and leave it 2 minutes, then drain. This firms the cubes enough to survive the pan and seasons them from the inside; without it, half of them break up into the sauce.
3. Heat the oil in a 28 cm wok over high heat for 90 seconds, until it shimmers and runs like water.
4. Add the pork. Cook for 4 minutes, pressing it flat and then breaking it up, until the grains are dry, browned and separate and rattle against the wok rather than clumping.
5. Turn the heat down to medium. Add the doubanjiang. Fry for 90 seconds, stirring constantly, until the oil has turned deep red and the paste smells sweet rather than raw. This is the step the dish is made or lost on — over high heat the paste burns, and it takes the whole wok bitter with it.
6. Add the chilli powder, black beans, garlic and ginger. Fry for 45 seconds, until you can smell the garlic over the paste.
7. Pour in the stock and the soy sauce, and bring to a simmer.
8. Slide in the tofu. Do not stir it: push the cubes under with the back of the ladle and shake the wok instead. Simmer for 5 minutes, until every cube has taken the colour of the sauce all the way round.
9. Stir the cornflour mixture and add a third of it. Simmer 30 seconds. Repeat twice more, 30 seconds apart. The sauce should finish glossy and just thick enough to coat the back of the ladle. Added in one go it turns gluey, and there is no undoing that.
10. Add the spring onion greens, shake the wok twice, and take it off the heat.
11. Dust the ground sichuan pepper over the top and serve at once, with plain rice. The pepper goes on off the heat: cooking it drives off the aroma and leaves only the numbness behind.

## Notes

This is the modern home version, and it differs from the Chengdu original in
three ways, each of them deliberate. The meat is pork rather than beef — Chen Mapo
Doufu in Chengdu, where the dish is from, has used beef since 1862, and beef is
what the Sichuan cookery schools teach. Pork is what most kitchens outside
Sichuan cook, it is sweeter and softer, and it is a real version rather than a
corruption. Garlic and ginger are in, which a purist leaves out on the grounds
that the doubanjiang and the black beans should carry that themselves. And the
spring onion greens stand in for garlic sprouts, which are the classic finish
and are hard to buy outside a Chinese greengrocer.

Fatty mince, not lean. The pork is fried until it is dry and crisp — Sichuan
cooks call it su, and it is one of the qualities the dish is judged on — and
5% mince gives up nothing to fry in and goes grey and soft instead.

150 g of pork against 450 g of tofu is still restrained. The Sichuan school
recipes go as low as 60 g: the meat here is seasoning, not substance.

30 g of doubanjiang suits a supermarket paste. A proper aged Pixian one is drier
and much saltier — start at 20 g, and taste before adding salt anywhere else.

Silken tofu is what the dish is made with. Firm tofu will not break up, but it
will not take up the sauce either, and it eats like a different dish.

If your sichuan pepper has been open two years it is doing nothing at all. Buy
more rather than use more.

The salt in the blanching water is not in the ingredient list and not in the
panel: a teaspoon in a litre of water that gets poured away would add 575 mg of
sodium a serving to figures meant to describe what you eat. It is named in the
step instead. What the panel does count comes to 950 mg a serving, nearly all of
it from the doubanjiang, the black beans and the stock.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge, covered. It reheats better than most tofu dishes: warm
it in a frying pan over medium heat with 2 tbsp of water, shaking rather than
stirring, for 3 minutes. The sauce loosens and the tofu is softer than it was,
which is not a fault.

Grind fresh sichuan pepper over it on serving — the first lot will have faded
completely by the next day. Do not freeze it: silken tofu comes out of a freezer
as sponge.
