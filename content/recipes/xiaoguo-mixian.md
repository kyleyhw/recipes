## Ingredients

- 1 tbsp neutral oil
- 80 g pork mince
- 6 g garlic, finely chopped
- 40 g pickled mustard greens, chopped
- 1 tsp sichuan chilli powder
- 400 ml chicken stock
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 0.5 tsp granulated sugar
- 0.25 tsp white pepper
- 200 g mixian (1 portion)
- 40 g chinese chives, in 4 cm lengths
- 30 g pea shoots
- 1 tbsp chilli oil

## Method

1. Heat the oil in a small saucepan — 16 cm, one bowl's worth — over high heat until it shimmers.
2. Add the pork and press it flat. Fry for 3 minutes, breaking it up after the first minute, until the grains are separate and browned at the edges rather than grey and clumped.
3. Add the garlic and the pickled greens and fry 60 seconds, until the pickle smells toasted and has darkened.
4. Add the chilli powder and stir for 15 seconds off the direct heat — it burns in the time it takes to reach for the stock.
5. Pour in the stock, add the soy sauce, sugar and white pepper, and bring to a hard boil.
6. Add the mixian and boil for 60 seconds. That is all it needs: vacuum-packed mixian is already cooked, and two minutes turns it to paste.
7. Throw in the chives and pea shoots, count to ten, and tip the whole pot into a bowl. Spoon the chilli oil over and eat it immediately.

## Notes

The small pot is the dish and the name. Xiaoguo means small pot, and in Kunming
each bowl is cooked to order in its own brass one over a fierce burner — which
matters because the whole thing takes ten minutes and nothing is held. Doubling
it in a big pan gives you something else: the noodles sit in the liquid while
the second portion catches up and they go soft. Cook it twice.

Mixian is not vermicelli. It is round, extruded and thicker, sold vacuum-packed
and already hydrated, and it goes in at the very end for one minute. If all you
can find is dried mixian, soak it in cold water for two hours first and then
give it three minutes here — and use 70 g rather than 200, because the figure in
this recipe is a wet weight.

Chives, not spring onions. Jiu cai are flat, dark and taste of garlic rather
than onion, and a handful of them going limp in the broth is a large part of
what makes this taste like Yunnan rather than like a generic noodle soup. They
are in every Chinese supermarket in bundles.

The pickle does the sour. Yunnan uses its own 酸腌菜; the vacuum-packed suan cai
in this library is the same idea and is what is actually buyable. Do not rinse
it — the salt in it is most of the seasoning, which is why there is only a
tablespoon of soy sauce here.

A raw egg cracked into the pot with the stock is the common addition and turns
this into a slightly richer bowl; so is a spoonful of doubanjiang for anyone who
wants it redder. Neither is in the list because the plain version is the one
worth learning first.

## Storage

None. This is a ten-minute dish cooked in a one-bowl pot and there is nothing
about it that survives being kept — the noodles go on absorbing and by the time
it is cold they have drunk the broth.

The pork and pickle mixture at the end of step 4 is the part worth making ahead:
four days in the fridge, two months frozen, and it turns the dish into a
five-minute one. Make four portions of it at once.
