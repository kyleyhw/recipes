## Ingredients

- 15 g dried wood ear mushrooms
- 12 g garlic, crushed to a paste
- 2 bird's eye chillies, finely sliced
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced
- 10 g coriander, roughly chopped
- 1.5 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1.5 tbsp black vinegar
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 2 tsp sesame oil
- 2 tsp chilli oil
- 1 tsp toasted sesame seeds

## Method

1. Put the wood ear in a large bowl and cover with plenty of cold water. It swells five or six times, so use a bigger bowl than looks necessary. Leave for 40 minutes, until every piece is soft, gelatinous and fully unfurled with no hard grey lumps left in the middle.
2. Drain and go through it piece by piece. Cut away the hard knot where it was attached to the wood — a pale, tough nub, usually the size of a fingernail — and discard it. Tear anything larger than a 5 cm across into bite-sized pieces.
3. Bring a pan of water to the boil and blanch the wood ear for 90 seconds. Drain and drop it into cold water to stop it, then drain again and shake it dry.
4. Stir the garlic, chillies, spring onions, soy sauce, black vinegar, sugar and sesame oil together until the sugar has gone.
5. Toss the wood ear through the dressing with the coriander. Add the chilli oil, toss again, and scatter the sesame seeds over. Serve cold or at room temperature.

## Notes

Do not skip the blanch. Rehydrated wood ear straight from the soaking bowl is
edible in the sense that nothing will happen to most people most of the time, but
ninety seconds in boiling water is the standard practice in every Chinese kitchen
and it costs nothing. It also firms the texture and takes off the faint muddy
smell of the soaking water.

The one genuine food-safety point attached to this ingredient is different and
worth stating plainly: **do not soak wood ear at room temperature for many hours,
and never overnight on the counter.** Rehydrated fungus sitting warm is a growth
medium, and there is a well-documented and occasionally fatal foodborne illness
associated with exactly this. Forty minutes in cold water, or overnight in the
fridge, are both fine.

This dish is entirely about texture. Wood ear tastes of almost nothing — it is
crunchy, slippery and springy at once, and everything else in the bowl is there
to give it flavour to carry. That also means the dressing should be assertive to
the point of seeming too much when tasted on a spoon.

Fifteen grams of dry fungus looks like nothing in the packet and fills a mixing
bowl once soaked. Weigh it; do not eyeball it.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge in a closed box, and the texture survives completely —
this is the rare salad that does not go limp, because there is nothing in wood ear
to go limp. The coriander will darken and the dressing will get sharper as the
garlic sits in the vinegar.

Make it the morning of, or the day before, and it will be better than it was fresh.

It does not freeze; thawed wood ear goes soft and loses the entire point of it.

Dry, unopened, the fungus itself keeps for two years in a cupboard.
