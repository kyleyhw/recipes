## Ingredients

- 15 g dried wood ear mushrooms
- 15 g dried shiitake mushrooms
- 1000 ml chicken stock
- 150 g lean pork, in 5 cm matchsticks
- 150 g bamboo shoots, drained and in 5 cm matchsticks
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1 tsp dark soy sauce
- 0.5 tsp salt
- 200 g firm tofu, in 5 cm matchsticks
- 3 tbsp cornflour
- 60 ml water
- 2 eggs, beaten
- 3 tbsp black vinegar
- 2 tsp white pepper
- 1 tbsp chilli oil
- 1 tsp sesame oil
- 20 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Cover the wood ear and the shiitake with 400 ml of warm water and leave them 20 minutes, until the wood ear has swollen to five times its size and is soft with no crisp centre, and the shiitake give when squeezed. Lift them out, squeeze them over the bowl, and keep the soaking liquid.
2. Cut the hard knot off each piece of wood ear and slice it into 3 mm ribbons. Cut the stems off the shiitake and slice the caps 3 mm thick.
3. Cut the pork, bamboo shoots and tofu into matchsticks 5 cm long and 3 mm square. This is the discipline the dish runs on: everything in the bowl should be the same shape, so that a spoonful is one texture rather than five, and the only things that are not are the egg and the mushrooms.
4. Strain the mushroom liquor through a sieve, leaving the grit in the last tablespoon, and pour it into a 20 cm saucepan with the chicken stock. Bring to a boil.
5. Add the pork and stir once to separate it. Simmer 2 minutes, until no pink is left on any strand.
6. Add the wood ear, shiitake, bamboo shoots, light soy sauce, dark soy sauce and salt. Simmer 5 minutes.
7. Add the tofu and simmer 2 minutes more, stirring gently — hard stirring from here on breaks the tofu into crumbs.
8. Stir the cornflour into the water until no grit sits on the bottom of the bowl, then pour it into the pan in a thin stream, stirring the whole time. Simmer 2 minutes, until it turns from cloudy to glossy and a spoon dragged across the pan leaves a track that closes slowly.
9. Turn the heat to its lowest. Pour the beaten egg in a thin stream from 20 cm above the pan, moving it in a spiral, and do not stir for 20 seconds. The egg sets in ribbons. Stirred while it goes in, it clouds the whole pot.
10. Take the pan off the heat. Only now stir in the black vinegar, white pepper, chilli oil and sesame oil. Vinegar boiled in a soup goes flat and sharp at once, and the pepper's heat is volatile in the same way.
11. Taste and correct: it should be aggressively sour first and hot second, and if it tastes balanced in the pan it will taste of nothing in the bowl. Serve at once, with the spring onions scattered over.

## Notes

The heat is white pepper, not chilli. Two teaspoons in a litre sounds like a
mistake and is the dish — 酸辣汤 is sour-and-peppery, and the burn is the dry,
climbing one at the back of the throat rather than the front-of-mouth heat of
chilli. The tablespoon of chilli oil is for colour and depth and could be left
out; the pepper could not.

Both the vinegar and the pepper go in off the heat. Acetic acid boils off at a
little over 100 °C and piperine is not much better, so a soup soured at step 6
and simmered for five minutes has lost most of what you added. This is why a
restaurant bowl tastes so much sharper than a home one: the pot is soured at
the pass.

Chinkiang black vinegar, and three tablespoons is not a lot. Rice vinegar is
sharper and thinner and would want two. Malt or wine vinegar makes a different
soup.

Everything in matchsticks, the same matchstick. It takes fifteen of the
twenty-five minutes and it is the difference between this and a pan of bits.

Dried wood ear rather than fresh, and dried shiitake rather than fresh, because
the soaking water is a third of the flavour base here and fresh mushrooms give
you none. Do not throw it away and do not pour in the last tablespoon, which is
grit.

The version with day lily buds (金針菜, a small handful, soaked with the
mushrooms) is the Beijing one and is worth doing if your supermarket has them.
The Sichuan version written here does without.

Congealed blood cubes and shredded chicken both belong in versions of this. So
does a handful of glass noodles, which turns it into a heavier bowl and is what
a lot of takeaway versions are doing when they arrive thick and cloudy.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge. It reheats badly and should be reheated anyway: the
cornflour thins on standing and the egg ribbons toughen, so what comes back is
looser and coarser than what went in. Bring it to a bare simmer, no more, and
correct it with a teaspoon of vinegar and a pinch of white pepper off the heat,
which puts most of it right.

Do not freeze it. Cornflour-thickened liquid separates and weeps on thawing,
tofu goes spongy and holey, and the egg turns rubbery — three faults, none of
them fixable.

If you know you are keeping half, stop at step 7 and refrigerate it there.
Thicken, egg and sour each portion as you heat it, which takes four minutes and
gives you the soup as it should be rather than as it survives.
