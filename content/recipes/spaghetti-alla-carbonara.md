## Ingredients

- 200 g spaghetti
- 120 g guanciale, in 1 cm lardons
- 3 egg yolks
- 1 egg
- 50 g pecorino romano, finely grated
- 1 tsp black pepper, coarsely ground

## Method

1. Put the guanciale in a cold 28 cm frying pan and set it over medium-low heat. Cook for 10 minutes, stirring twice. Starting cold is what renders the fat out before the meat colours — a hot pan seizes the outside and traps it. It is done when the fat runs clear and the pieces are gold at the edges but still give when pressed. Crisp all the way through is bacon, and the wrong texture for this.
2. Lift the guanciale out with a slotted spoon onto a plate. Keep every drop of fat in the pan, and take the pan off the heat.
3. Bring 2 litres of water to a boil with 0.5 tsp of salt. That is less water and far less salt than pasta usually gets: less water makes it starchier, which is half the sauce, and the pecorino and the guanciale are salty enough that a properly salted pot makes the finished dish inedible.
4. Whisk the yolks, the whole egg, the pecorino and the pepper in a metal bowl until it is a thick paste with no streak of white left in it.
5. Cook the spaghetti for 1 minute less than the packet says. Reserve 300 ml of the water, then drain.
6. Whisk 2 tbsp of the warm guanciale fat into the egg paste a spoonful at a time, then 2 tbsp of the hot pasta water the same way. It loosens to the consistency of double cream. This is the tempering, and it is the step that decides between sauce and scrambled egg: the mixture is being carried up towards 60 °C slowly instead of meeting that heat all at once.
7. Tip the drained pasta into the frying pan with the guanciale fat, off the heat, and toss for 15 seconds to coat every strand.
8. Pour in the egg mixture and add the guanciale. Toss hard and without stopping for 60 to 90 seconds, adding pasta water a splash at a time. The pan stays off the heat throughout — the pasta's own warmth is all the heat the sauce needs. It is ready when the sauce coats the strands, gleams, and a line drawn through it with a spoon closes slowly rather than at once.
9. Serve immediately, with more pecorino and more pepper over the top. Carbonara does not wait: it stiffens within two minutes of leaving the pan.

## Notes

Egg proteins start to set at about 62 °C and are solid by 70. The whole method
is about crossing 60 and stopping there. With the pan on the heat when the egg
goes in you have about four seconds before it is scrambled, which is why every
step from 6 onwards happens off it.

If it does scramble, catch it early: pull the pan away, add 50 ml of cold pasta
water at once, and toss hard. Curds that have only just started to form will
usually re-emulsify. Once it looks like breakfast, it is breakfast.

Guanciale, not pancetta and not bacon. Guanciale is cured pork jowl — fattier,
softer, and with a funk pancetta has not got. Pancetta is a fair substitute.
Bacon is smoked, and the smoke takes the dish somewhere else entirely.

No cream, no garlic, no onion. The creaminess is an emulsion of egg, rendered
fat, cheese and starchy water, and cream in it is a different sauce covering for
a technique that was not done.

Pecorino alone is the Roman version, sharp and salty. Half pecorino and half
parmigiano is what several Roman kitchens actually do, and it is rounder and
less aggressive.

Two servings is the sweet spot and four is the ceiling. Past that the pasta
cools faster than you can bring the sauce together, and no pan that full can be
tossed hard enough.

If you are nervous, sit the metal bowl over the pasta pot with an inch of barely
simmering water in it and make the sauce there. It is slower and almost
impossible to get wrong.

The salt for the pasta water is named in step 3 rather than listed as an
ingredient, because most of it is poured away — the same reason the blanching
salt is left out of the steamed patties and the salt crust out of the pork
belly.

## Storage

There is none. This is one of the few dishes here that genuinely has to be eaten
the minute it is made: the emulsion tightens as it cools, and it cannot be
reheated, because anything hot enough to loosen it is hot enough to scramble it.

What Rome does with leftovers is frittata di pasta. Press the cold pasta into an
oiled frying pan over medium heat, cook 5 minutes a side until both faces are
brown and crisp, and eat it in wedges. It is a different dish and a good one.
