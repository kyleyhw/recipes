## Ingredients

- 400 ml black tea, brewed very strong
- 50 g granulated sugar
- 70 g lemon, cut into 8 slices 3 mm thick
- 400 g water, frozen into cubes

## Method

1. Bring 500 ml of water to a rolling boil in a small pan. Tip in 20 g of black tea leaves — a coarse Ceylon broken-leaf, the same tea a cha chaan teng brews for milk tea — and boil hard, uncovered, for 3 minutes. It foams up the sides; stir it down.
2. Take the pan off the heat, cover it, and leave it for 4 minutes. The tea is now opaque and the colour of dark mahogany, and tasted on its own it is flatly bitter and drying at the back of the mouth. That is correct.
3. Pour it through a fine sieve into a jug. You will have 400 ml — top it up from the kettle if the leaves have taken more than that.
4. Stir the sugar into the hot tea until the jug tilts clear with nothing grainy on the bottom. It goes in now because it cannot go in later: sugar will not dissolve in cold tea, which is why a cha chaan teng keeps a bottle of syrup under the counter, and doing it while the tea is hot saves making one.
5. Leave the jug on the worktop for 30 minutes, then refrigerate for 30 minutes, until it is cold against the back of the hand. Hot tea poured onto ice melts it straight to a weak brown puddle, and there is no way back from that.
6. Put 4 lemon slices into each tall glass. Press each slice against the side of the glass with the back of a long spoon — hard enough to see the juice run and the flesh go cloudy, not hard enough to tear through the peel.
7. Fill each glass to the brim with ice. Pour 200 ml of cold tea into each.
8. Stir for 10 seconds and drink it now. It should read sour first, sweet second and tannic underneath both, and it should be cloudy amber rather than clear — if you can see through it, the lemon has not been pressed hard enough.

## Notes

Twenty grams of leaf to 500 ml of water, boiled rather than steeped, is three or
four times the strength of a normal pot. It has to be. The drink is tasted
through 200 g of ice and 25 g of sugar, and anything brewed to a polite strength
comes out of that as brown water.

The peel is the point, and it is what separates this from lemon squash. Pressing
the slices releases oil from the skin and a little bitterness from the pith, and
that bitterness is the third flavour the drink is built on. It is also why the
spoon is yours rather than the kitchen's: peel that has been ground up and left
sitting turns the whole glass harsh within about ten minutes, so the drink is
served for you to press and then drink, at your own speed and to your own taste.
Press, do not muddle.

Twenty-five grams of sugar a glass is the cha chaan teng level and is what the
recipe is balanced at. Below 15 g the tannin takes over and the lemon stops
reading as sour and starts reading as sharp. This is not a health drink and
there is no version of it that is; it is a soft drink, and a can of cola is
35 g.

The ice is written into the ingredient list as frozen water rather than left to
the method because it is a third of what is in the glass and it is supposed to
melt. The drink is built to be diluted as you get through it, which is another
reason the tea starts as strong as it does.

Hot lemon tea — 熱檸茶 — is the same thing without the chilling: brew as
written, stir in the sugar, put 4 slices in a mug and pour 200 ml over. Press
them once and leave the spoon in. In winter a cha chaan teng will do it with
ginger and honey instead of sugar, which is a different and much older drink.

The panel overstates the lemon. The library row is lemon flesh without peel, and
what actually reaches you is the juice pressed out of four slices, not the
slices themselves — so the fibre and most of the vitamin C on the page are
sitting in the bottom of the glass rather than in the drink. What you are
really drinking is the sugar, which is all of the energy here, and tea, which
is water with a little potassium in it.

## Storage

Pour it and drink it. A poured glass is at its best for about five minutes:
after that the ice has watered it down and the pressed peel has gone bitter, and
those two happen at roughly the same rate.

The sweetened tea base is the part worth making ahead, and it is better for it —
two days in a covered jug in the fridge, and a day-old base makes a cleaner
drink than one rushed through cooling. Never put the lemon in the jug. Lemon
left standing in tea goes muddy and bitter within a couple of hours and takes
the whole batch with it.
