## Ingredients

- 750 ml red wine
- 130 g orange, sliced 5 mm thick
- 75 g granulated sugar
- 2 cinnamon sticks
- 3 star anise
- 6 whole cloves
- 60 ml brandy

## Method

1. Put the orange slices, sugar, cinnamon, star anise and cloves in a 20 cm saucepan with 250 ml of the wine — a third of the bottle.
2. Bring to a boil over medium heat, stirring until you cannot feel any grains on the bottom of the pan, then boil hard for 4 minutes without stirring. It reduces by about a third and turns syrupy: a spoon lifted out is coated and the drips come off slowly rather than running. The kitchen smells of cloves before it smells of wine.
3. Turn the heat to its lowest setting. Pour in the rest of the wine and the brandy.
4. Heat for 10 minutes, until steam is coming off the surface and small bubbles are forming at the edge of the pan and sitting there without rising. That is about 65 °C. It must not boil.
5. Take the pan off the heat, put a lid on it, and leave it 5 minutes.
6. Ladle into heatproof glasses, one slice of orange in each and the rest of the spices left in the pan. Serve at once, while it is too hot to drink in mouthfuls.

## Notes

Boiling a third of the wine into a syrup first is the whole recipe, and it is
the step most versions skip. Sugar stirred into warm wine tastes of sugar; four
minutes of hard boiling with the peel and the spices in it drives off water,
takes the oil out of the orange skin and the cloves, and leaves something that
tastes of Christmas rather than of sweetened wine. The alcohol boiled off in
those four minutes is a third of a bottle's worth at most, and the brandy at
the end more than replaces it.

Sixty-five degrees, and this is the number to care about. Ethanol boils at
78 °C, so wine held at a simmer is losing the thing you bought it for, and
above about 80 °C it starts to taste stewed and metallic. If you have a
thermometer, use it; if not, the test is bubbles that form and stay put.

Cheap, fruity and low in tannin. A Merlot, a Garnacha, a Côtes du Rhône —
anything soft and about £6. Heat makes tannin harsh and astringent, so a big
oaked Cabernet, which is the bottle people reach for because it seems a shame
to cook with something nice, makes the worst mulled wine in the room. Nothing
oaked, and nothing you would enjoy cold.

Whole spices only. Ground cinnamon and ground cloves will not dissolve, and
what they do instead is hang in the glass as grit and settle into a sludge at
the bottom. There is no way to strain it out fine enough.

Six cloves is enough for a bottle and eight is too many — clove is the one
spice here that can take over, and once it has there is nothing to be done.
Star anise and cinnamon are more forgiving.

The brandy is not optional if you want this to taste of anything by the second
glass; port is the other traditional answer and makes it sweeter and heavier.
A bottle of wine and 60 ml of brandy is about two units a glass.

For a non-alcoholic version, red grape juice makes a better base than
cranberry, take the sugar down to 30 g, and add a tablespoon of lemon juice at
the end, because the acidity is what the wine was providing.

Wine is not always vegan — many are fined with isinglass, gelatine or egg
white, none of which appears on the label. The dietary filters on this page
read the ingredient list and cannot know which bottle you bought.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge in a sealed jug, and strain the spices out before it
goes in. Left on them it keeps extracting, and by the second day the clove has
run away with it and the orange pith has turned it bitter — which is the one
way this can spoil while still being perfectly safe to drink.

Reheat it the way it was made: lowest heat, 5 minutes, no boiling. It is
noticeably better on day two than on day one, so making it the night before a
party is the right call rather than a compromise.

It freezes for three months and comes back fine, which is a strange thing to do
with mulled wine and is the sensible use for half a jug in January. The alcohol
keeps it slushy rather than solid, so use a container with room at the top.
