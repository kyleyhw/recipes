## Ingredients

- 10 g dried shiitake mushrooms
- 100 g chicken thigh, in 2 cm pieces
- 3 eggs
- 450 ml dashi, cooled
- 1 tbsp mirin
- 2 tsp light soy sauce
- 0.25 tsp salt
- 10 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Cover the shiitake with 200 ml of warm water and leave them 20 minutes, until they are soft and pliable all the way through with no hard core at the stem. Do this while the dashi cools. Squeeze them out, cut the stems off and throw them away, and slice the caps 3 mm thick.
2. Cut the chicken into 2 cm pieces and toss them with 1 teaspoon of the soy sauce.
3. Beat the eggs in a bowl with chopsticks for 30 seconds, cutting through them rather than whipping, until they are one even yellow with no ribbon of white left. Stop before they foam.
4. Stir in the cooled dashi, the mirin, the salt and the remaining teaspoon of soy sauce. The dashi must be no warmer than a room — hot dashi cooks threads of egg on the way in, and they never smooth out.
5. Pour the mixture through a fine sieve into a jug. Draw a spoon across the surface to lift off every bubble.
6. Divide the chicken and shiitake between four heatproof cups or small bowls. Pour the custard in to within 1 cm of the rim and cover each tightly with foil.
7. Put a rack in a wide pan, add water to just below it, and bring to the boil. Set the cups on the rack, turn the heat down until the water is barely moving, and steam covered for 15 minutes.
8. It is done when the surface is matt and unbroken, a skewer slipped in at the edge releases clear liquid rather than cloudy, and a gentle shake sends one slow wobble across the whole cup.
9. Lift the foil away from you so no condensation drops on it, scatter the spring onion over, and serve warm with a spoon.

## Notes

One part egg to three of dashi, and that ratio is the dish. Steamed Egg Custard,
the Cantonese one on this site, is one part egg to one and a half parts water —
twice as much egg, and it sets into something you eat with rice. This sets so
softly it barely holds its shape in the spoon, which only works because the
dashi is savoury enough to carry that little egg on its own. Water at this
dilution would taste of nothing.

That is the whole comparison worth making between the two: same technique, same
low heat, same strain, same covered cup, and two completely different results
from changing what the egg is diluted with and by how much.

Low heat, for the reason it is always low heat with egg custard. A rolling boil
boils the water inside the custard, the egg sets around the bubbles, and you get
a stiff pitted sponge instead. If your smallest flame still runs the pan hard,
prop the lid open with a chopstick.

Cover the cups. Twelve minutes of condensation dripping off the lid pits the
surface, and the foil is the only thing stopping it.

Dashi cooled, not warm. Everything else here is forgiving; this is not.

Things that traditionally sit at the bottom, one or two rather than all: a raw
prawn, a slice of kamaboko fish cake, ginkgo nuts, a sprig of mitsuba on top at
the end. They go in raw and cook in the custard.

There is no version of this without dashi. Take it out and put water in and you
have made the Cantonese custard, which is a good dish and is that one.

## Storage

Eat it warm, within a few minutes. Like every set egg custard it is at its best
the moment it comes out.

Two days covered in the fridge, and it is not embarrassing cold — chilled
chawanmushi is served in summer on purpose. To warm it, steam for 4 minutes over
the same low heat; pour off any water that has wept out first.

It does not freeze. The custard separates into curds and a puddle.
