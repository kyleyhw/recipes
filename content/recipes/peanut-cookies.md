## Ingredients

- 150 g all-purpose flour
- 150 g roasted peanuts
- 100 g icing sugar
- 0.5 tsp salt
- 0.5 tsp baking powder
- 90 ml peanut oil
- 1 egg yolk

## Method

1. Heat the oven to 170 °C. Line two baking trays with baking paper.
2. Spread the flour on a third tray and bake it alone for 8 minutes, stirring once at 4 minutes. It should smell toasted and no longer of raw paste. This step is what stops the finished cookie tasting floury, and it is the one most recipes leave out.
3. Rub the skins off the peanuts between your palms over a bowl and blow the skins away. Blitz the peanuts in a food processor in 2-second pulses until they are the texture of coarse sand — 10 pulses. Go further and the oil comes out and you have peanut butter, which will not work here.
4. Stir the toasted flour, ground peanuts, icing sugar, salt and baking powder together for 20 seconds until the colour is even.
5. Pour in the peanut oil and work it through with a spatula, then with your hand, for 1 minute. It stays as damp crumbs. It is ready when a handful squeezed hard holds together and a handful dropped from height falls apart again.
6. Roll 24 balls of 20 g each and set them on the trays 4 cm apart. Press each one down with your thumb to a 3 cm disc, 1 cm thick, and press the cracked edge back in with two fingers so the sides are smooth.
7. Beat the egg yolk with 1 tsp of water and brush it over the tops in a thin coat. A thick coat pools and bakes to a shiny brown skin that separates from the cookie.
8. Bake for 18 minutes, swapping the shelves at 9 minutes, until the tops are a deep even gold and the bases are one shade darker. They are still soft in the oven and will not look done.
9. Leave them on the trays for 15 minutes without touching them. They set as they cool, and moving one before it has is how it breaks in half.

## Notes

There is no butter and no egg in the body of this — the fat is all peanut oil,
which is what makes the texture sandy and collapsing rather than short and
snapping. Groundnut oil is the same thing under its British name. A neutral oil
works and gives up the background peanut flavour that the oil is quietly
supplying.

Grind the peanuts yourself and do it coarsely. Ready-ground peanut flour is
defatted and makes a dry, chalky cookie, and peanut butter makes a soft one
that spreads. What you want is visible grit, and the way to get it is short
pulses with the machine stopped between them.

The flour is toasted first because there is no water in this dough and
therefore very little steam to carry off the raw-flour taste during baking.
Eighteen minutes at 170 °C would not do it. This is the same reasoning behind
toasting flour for a roux.

These are 花生酥, a Chinese New Year biscuit, and they are made in quantity
because they keep for a month — which is the reason a festival biscuit is
usually a dry one.

Icing sugar rather than caster, for the same reason as in the Danish Butter
Cookies: a dough with no water in it cannot dissolve a crystal, and undissolved
caster sugar melts in the oven and leaves the cookie pocked and spread.

## Storage

A month in an airtight tin at room temperature. There is almost no water in
them and no dairy, so they keep longer than anything else baked in this
collection, and they are as good in week four as in week one.

They soften if the tin is not properly sealed, and 5 minutes at 150 °C followed
by 15 minutes of cooling brings them all the way back.

The unbaked dough does not keep well — the oil separates out of it in the
fridge overnight and cannot be worked back in. Bake the batch and store the
biscuits, which is the way round this recipe is designed.
