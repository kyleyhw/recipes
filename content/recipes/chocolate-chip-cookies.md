## Ingredients

- 170 g unsalted butter, softened
- 150 g brown sugar
- 75 g granulated sugar
- 1 egg
- 2 tsp vanilla extract
- 210 g all-purpose flour
- ¾ tsp bicarbonate of soda
- 1 tsp salt
- 200 g dark chocolate, chopped into 1 cm chunks

## Method

1. Get the butter soft before anything else. It is ready when a finger pressed into it leaves a clean dent and the block does not slide away — an hour out of the fridge in a warm kitchen, three in a cold one. Cold butter will not hold air and the cookies come out flat and dense.
2. Beat the butter, brown sugar and granulated sugar together in a large bowl for 3 minutes with an electric whisk, or 5 minutes with a wooden spoon. It goes from wet sand to the colour of clotted cream, and it is done when a lifted beater carries a soft peak that folds over on itself.
3. Beat in the egg for 30 seconds, then the vanilla for 10 seconds. The mixture slackens and turns glossy. If it looks curdled the butter was still too cold — beat for another minute and it comes back.
4. In a second bowl, stir the flour, bicarbonate of soda and salt together for 10 seconds, so the raising agent and the salt are through the flour before either meets anything wet. Bicarbonate that arrives in a lump leaves a soapy patch in the finished cookie.
5. Tip the dry ingredients into the wet and fold with a spatula until the last streak of flour disappears, no more than 15 turns. The dough should still look rough.
6. Fold in the chocolate with 5 more turns, and stop while there are still dry-looking seams.
7. Cover the bowl and chill for 1 hour. The dough is ready when a spoon pushed into it meets resistance and the trench it leaves holds its edges instead of slumping.
8. Heat the oven to 180 °C. Line two baking trays with baking paper.
9. Divide the dough into 12 and roll each piece into a ball; each one weighs 72 g. Put 6 on each tray, 6 cm apart — they spread to twice their width and will join up at 4 cm.
10. Bake both trays for 12 minutes, swapping the shelves at 6 minutes. They are done when the outer 2 cm is set and browned and the middle is still pale, domed and visibly wet. They look underbaked at this point because they are; the tray finishes them.
11. Slide the paper onto a rack and leave the cookies on it for 10 minutes. They sink into ridges as they cool and the middles set to fudge. Lift one before 10 minutes and its base tears off and stays on the paper.

## Notes

Chop a bar; do not buy chips. Chips are formulated to hold their shape in an oven — more stabiliser, less cocoa butter — so they come out as chips, which is the one thing you do not want. A chopped bar gives puddles at the bottom and a shard standing proud at the top, and it gives dust. The dust is not waste. It greys the dough as it mixes and it is most of the reason the cookie tastes of chocolate rather than of a cookie with chocolate in it.

Seventy per cent, and not lower. There are 225 g of sugar in this dough, and milk chocolate or a 50% bar simply vanishes into it.

The salt is deliberate, and it is more than most recipes ask for. A full teaspoon is what keeps the cookie from tasting flatly sweet, and you notice its absence long before you would notice its excess. If you keep flaky sea salt, press three or four flakes onto the top of each ball before it goes into the oven as well.

An hour in the fridge is the shortest rest that does anything: it firms the butter so the cookies spread half as far and stay thick. Overnight is better, and 72 hours is better still — the flour hydrates fully, the dough browns deeper, and it starts to taste of toffee rather than of sugar. Dough rested longer than a night needs 20 minutes on the counter before rolling or the balls crack as you shape them.

The 6 cm spacing is not caution. Six cookies to a tray is the most a standard 40 cm tray takes, and crowding them makes the sides steam pale while the tops brown.

## Storage

Four days in a tin at room temperature, and they are best on the first day. They go soft rather than hard, which is fixable: 4 minutes on a tray in a 160 °C oven, then 5 minutes on a rack, and the edges crisp up again. Do not microwave them — the middle turns to hot syrup and the base goes leathery.

The dough keeps better than the cookies do, and this is the way to make them. Roll the 12 balls, freeze them on a tray for 2 hours until they are hard, then bag them for 3 months. Bake from frozen at 180 °C for 14 minutes rather than 12. Do not thaw them first: a thawed ball spreads into a single puddle.

Baked cookies freeze for a month, wrapped in pairs. Thaw them in the wrapping for an hour at room temperature rather than in the oven, which dries them out.
