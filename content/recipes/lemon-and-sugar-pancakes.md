## Ingredients

- 125 g all-purpose flour
- 0.25 tsp salt
- 2 eggs
- 300 ml milk
- 30 g unsalted butter, melted
- 20 g unsalted butter, for the pan
- 1 lemon, cut into wedges
- 2 tbsp caster sugar

## Method

1. Whisk the flour and salt in a bowl and make a well in the middle. Break in the eggs and whisk them into the flour a little at a time, drawing it in from the sides — you are making a thick smooth paste first, which is what stops lumps forming later.
2. Add the milk in four goes, whisking each in completely before the next. Whisk until it is entirely smooth. This batter wants to be smooth, which is the opposite instruction to the Kimchi Pancake: there is no crispness to protect here and a lump is a lump on the plate.
3. Cover and rest 30 minutes at room temperature. The flour hydrates and the gluten relaxes, and a rested batter spreads thinner and fries tender rather than rubbery. It will also have thickened slightly; it should pour like single cream, so let it down with a tablespoon of milk if it has gone past that.
4. Stir in the 30 g of melted butter. Butter in the batter means barely any in the pan, and it is why these come out lacy rather than fried.
5. Heat a 20 cm frying pan over medium-high for 90 seconds. Wipe it with a knob of the pan butter on a piece of kitchen paper — a film, not a pool.
6. Lift the pan off the heat, pour in 50 ml of batter, and immediately tilt and swirl it so the batter runs to the edge in a thin even round before it sets. Put it back on the heat.
7. Fry for 45 seconds. It is ready to turn when the edge has dried and lifted from the pan, the surface has gone matt, and the underside is patterned gold and brown.
8. Flip it — with a palette knife, or by tossing it if you are willing — and give it 20 seconds. The second side never colours evenly and is not meant to.
9. Slide it onto a warm plate and repeat, wiping the pan with butter every second or third one. Stack them as they come; they do not stick to each other.
10. Squeeze lemon over, scatter caster sugar, roll it up, and eat it before the next one is ready.

## Notes

This is what pancakes means in Britain, and it is worth saying plainly because
the word means something else almost everywhere online. These are thin, eggy
and floppy, closer to a crêpe than to anything raised — there is no raising
agent in them at all. The Buttermilk Pancakes elsewhere in the collection are
the other thing, and the two are not versions of each other.

Four things here are called pancakes and no two of them are alike: this, a thin
batter swirled; Buttermilk Pancakes, a thick one raised by an acid; Kimchi
Pancake, a loose batter that exists to hold vegetables together; and Spring
Onion Pancakes, which is not a batter at all but a laminated dough. The word
covers all four in English and would not in any of the languages they come
from.

The first one is always wrong. The pan is not yet at an even temperature and
the fat is not yet distributed, and everyone who cooks these treats the first
as the cook's perk. Do not adjust anything on the strength of it.

Rest the batter. Thirty minutes is the minimum that does anything and overnight
in the fridge is better — the flour keeps hydrating and the pancakes get
noticeably more tender. A batter used straight away fries tough and tears when
you turn it.

Swirl off the heat. On the heat the batter sets where it lands and you get a
thick middle and a ragged edge; lifted off for the three seconds it takes to
tilt the pan, it runs where you send it.

Fifty millilitres is about a small ladle, and it is less than instinct says. A
20 cm pan takes a very thin layer, and using more gives something closer to an
American pancake without any of the things that make one good.

Lemon and caster sugar in that order, and caster rather than granulated —
granulated does not dissolve in the time it takes to eat one and grinds. Golden
syrup, or lemon curd, or Nutella are all normal; jam is not, for no reason
anyone can defend.

The batter makes a Yorkshire pudding and a toad in the hole without changing
anything except the amount of fat and the heat, which is worth knowing on a
Tuesday when you have made too much.

## Storage

Best eaten one at a time as they come out of the pan, which is how they are
made anyway — this is a dish you cook standing up with people passing plates.

Cooked pancakes keep two days in the fridge, stacked with baking paper between
and wrapped. Reheat in a dry pan for 20 seconds a side, or 15 seconds in a
microwave, which is one of the few places a microwave is the right answer
because there is no crust to ruin. They freeze for two months the same way.

The batter keeps better than the pancakes: two days in the fridge covered, and
it improves overnight. Whisk it before use, because the flour settles into a
paste at the bottom and the top pours off as milk.
