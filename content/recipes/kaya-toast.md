## Ingredients

- 2 eggs
- 1 tsp dark soy sauce
- 0.25 tsp white pepper
- 120 g white bread
- 40 g kaya
- 30 g salted butter

## Method

1. Bring 600 ml of water to a full boil in a small pan. Take it off the heat, lower the eggs in straight from the fridge, put the lid on and leave them for exactly 10 minutes. Do not put the pan back on the heat — the water starts at 100 °C and falls to about 80, and that fall is the whole method.
2. While they sit, toast the bread to a deep even gold, dry all the way through: 3 minutes in a toaster on a medium setting, or 2 minutes a side under a grill 15 cm from the element. A corner should snap when you bend it rather than fold. Cut the crusts off, square.
3. Cut the butter straight from the fridge into slabs 3 mm thick — six of them from 30 g. Do not soften it and do not spread it.
4. Spread the kaya over two of the slices, right to the edges. Lay the butter slabs on top edge to edge, close each with a second slice, and press once with your palm. The butter should go translucent at the surface and stay solid in the middle; if it has run to oil the toast was too hot and should have stood for a minute.
5. Cut each sandwich into three fingers.
6. Lift the eggs out at 10 minutes. Tap each around its equator, open it over a small shallow bowl, and scrape out the white clinging inside the shell with a teaspoon. The white should be a loose milky jelly that only just holds together and the yolk should run the moment it is broken.
7. Spoon half the dark soy sauce and half the white pepper into each bowl. Do not stir.
8. Eat at once, dipping the toast fingers into the egg.

## Notes

Thin bread, toasted dry. An ordinary 30 g supermarket slice is right and the
toasting has to go further than it feels comfortable to go — a kopitiam grills
it over charcoal until it is a cracker. This is the opposite instruction to
Hong Kong French Toast, which wants slices half as thick again and a soft
middle, and the two dishes are worth reading side by side for that.

The butter is the point, not the kaya. Cold, thick, salted, and in slabs rather
than spread: what you are eating is the contrast between hot sweet jam and cold
salt fat, and a thin scrape of soft butter gives you neither temperature nor
salt. Unsalted butter makes a duller sandwich here — if that is what is in the
fridge, add a pinch of flaky salt over the kaya.

Half-boiled, not soft-boiled. The white must be barely set and slippery, which
is a lower temperature than a soft-boiled egg sees. Simmering these for ten
minutes instead gives a hard-boiled egg and a different breakfast. It is a
barely-cooked egg either way, so buy eggs you would be happy to eat raw, and
give it a miss entirely if you are pregnant or feeding someone very young or
very old.

Dark soy sauce, not light. It is thicker and sweeter, and it is there to season
the egg rather than salt it; light soy makes the bowl taste like soup.

The kaya can be a jar — Yeo's or Glory, in the jams aisle of an Asian
supermarket, and that is what the nutrition figures here are drawn from — or it
can be Kaya, which is better and takes an afternoon.

The third thing on the tray is kopi: local coffee, roasted with sugar and
margarine and served with condensed milk. Nothing here makes it, and a strong
dark coffee with a spoonful of condensed milk is close enough to be worth doing.

## Storage

Eat it at once. Four minutes on the plate and the butter has melted into the
toast and the toast has gone soft under the kaya, which is the entire dish
undone. Nothing about this keeps, reheats or travels.

The parts keep and the assembly does not. Kaya is two weeks in the fridge, bread
is three months sliced in the freezer and toasts from frozen, and butter wants
to be cold anyway. A half-boiled egg is the one thing with no shelf life at all
— it is eaten within a minute of being opened or thrown away.
