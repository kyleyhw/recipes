## Ingredients

- 300 g choy sum
- 1.5 tbsp neutral oil
- 20 g garlic, finely chopped
- 0.25 tsp salt
- 0.25 tsp granulated sugar
- 1 tbsp shaoxing wine
- 1 tbsp oyster sauce
- 2 tbsp water

## Method

1. Wash the choy sum in a sink of cold water, lifting it out rather than pouring it off so the grit stays behind. Trim 1 cm from the stem ends and cut the rest into 6 cm lengths. Separate the thick stems from the leaves and thin tops into two piles — about two thirds stems by weight.
2. Dry it properly, in a salad spinner or a clean tea towel. Wet greens drop the wok's temperature and steam instead of frying, and that is the difference between this dish and a sad grey one.
3. Heat a wok over the highest heat you have until a drop of water flicked in skitters and vanishes within 2 seconds. Add the oil and swirl it up the sides.
4. Add the garlic and stir for 10 seconds, until it smells sweet and the edges have just turned pale gold. It must not brown — at this heat it goes from gold to bitter in the time it takes to look away.
5. Add the stems, the salt and the sugar. Stir-fry for 60 seconds, keeping everything moving, until the stems have turned a deeper, glossier green and the cut ends look glassy rather than chalky.
6. Add the leaves and pour the shaoxing wine down the hot side of the wok, where it will hiss and steam. Stir-fry for 30 seconds, until the leaves have collapsed to about a third of their bulk.
7. Add the oyster sauce and the water and toss for 20 more seconds, until every piece is coated and a little glossy sauce gathers at the bottom of the wok. It is done when a stem still snaps when you bite it. Serve at once, in a warm bowl, with the sauce poured over.

## Notes

Do not crowd the wok. Three hundred grams is what a 30 cm wok will take over
a domestic burner; more than that and the greens sweat rather than fry, and no
amount of extra heat fixes it. Doubling this means cooking it twice, which takes
five minutes and is the correct answer.

Oyster sauce is the seasoning, so there is no soy sauce here. It brings its own
salt, its own sugar and a savouriness that soy does not, and adding both makes a
brown, muddled dish. A quarter teaspoon of salt on the stems is there to draw
their water out early, not to season the finished plate.

Gai lan, Chinese broccoli, is the other vegetable this method is for, and it is
tougher: blanch it for 90 seconds in boiling water first, drain it hard, and
then pick the recipe up at step 3 with 30 seconds knocked off the stem fry. Yu
choy, ong choy and bok choy all go straight in as written. The rule is the
thickness of the stem.

The garlic is 20 g, which is six or seven cloves and looks like too much in the
bowl. It is not. This is a dish of garlic with greens in it, and half that
amount produces greens with a faint idea of garlic.

The panel counts the tablespoon of oyster sauce and the oil, which is where
almost all of the sodium and nearly all of the energy come from: the choy sum
itself is 13 kcal per 100 g.

A restaurant does this differently — blanching the greens in oiled, salted water
and pouring hot garlic oil over them, which is what the source recipe does and
what gives the vivid, uniform green you see under a heat lamp. It is the better
method for a banquet and the worse one for a Tuesday, since it dirties a pot as
well as a wok.

## Storage

Eat it immediately. Ten minutes in the bowl and the leaves have gone olive and
the stems have gone soft, which is what stir-frying them fast was for.

It keeps two days covered in the fridge and it is not the same dish — reheating
finishes the cooking that the wok deliberately stopped short of. If there is
some left over, eat it cold from the fridge rather than warming it, or chop it
and stir it through fried rice at the last second.

It does not freeze.
