## Ingredients

- 30 g dried shiitake mushrooms
- 1000 ml water
- 60 g glass noodles
- 1 tbsp neutral oil
- 15 g ginger, in fine slivers
- 10 g garlic, finely chopped
- 1.5 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 0.25 tsp white pepper
- 200 g choy sum, in 6 cm lengths
- 1 tsp sesame oil
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Cover the shiitake with 1 litre of warm water and leave 20 minutes, until soft all through with no hard core at the stem. Squeeze them out over the bowl, cut the stems off and throw them away, and slice the caps 3 mm thick.
2. Pour the soaking water into a jug, leaving the last tablespoon behind in the bowl — that is where the grit settles. This liquid is the broth; do not throw any of it away.
3. Cut the dry bundle of glass noodles in half with scissors, cover with warm water, and leave 10 minutes until translucent and pliable with a firm core. Drain.
4. Heat the oil in a saucepan over medium heat. Add the ginger and garlic and fry 45 seconds, until they smell sweet and the garlic is pale gold.
5. Add the sliced shiitake and fry 2 minutes, stirring, until they have darkened and shrunk and started to squeak against the pan.
6. Pour in the soaking water and add the soy sauce, sugar and white pepper. Bring to a boil and simmer 8 minutes.
7. Add the choy sum stems and cook 90 seconds, then the leaves and the drained noodles, and cook 90 seconds more. It is done when the noodles have gone fully clear, the stems still snap, and the broth is the colour of weak tea.
8. Off the heat, stir in the sesame oil. Ladle into two deep bowls and scatter the spring onion over.

## Notes

The soaking water is the whole recipe. Thirty grams of dried shiitake in a litre
of warm water for twenty minutes gives up more savour than a stock cube will,
and it is the reason this tastes of something despite containing no meat, no
stock and nothing that took longer than half an hour. Throwing that liquid away
and using water is the one way to ruin it.

Leave the last tablespoon in the bowl. Fine grit settles there every time and it
is unpleasant in a clear broth.

Warm water, not boiling, for the soak. Boiling water softens the outside fast
and leaves a hard core in the middle, and drives off some of what you are trying
to extract.

Fry the mushrooms before the liquid goes in. Two minutes in oil browns their
edges and adds a layer the soaking water does not have on its own — soup made by
simply boiling everything together tastes thinner, and it is the same two
minutes that a stew spends browning its onions.

Sixty grams of glass noodles looks like nothing dry and roughly triples. They
also keep drinking after they are in the bowl, so eat this within about ten
minutes or add another 100 ml of water when you reheat it.

This is the only meatless soup in the collection. It takes a soft-boiled egg, a
block of silken tofu cut into cubes, or a handful of firm tofu cut small and
fried first, and none of them changes the technique.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge, and it thickens as the noodles keep drinking — the
broth halves in volume by the next day. Add 200 ml of water per bowl when
reheating and taste it again for soy.

Better still, keep the noodles out. The broth alone keeps three days, freezes
for two months, and is worth making double for that reason; boil fresh noodles
into it in five minutes.
