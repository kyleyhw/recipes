## Ingredients

- 1 tbsp peanut oil
- 150 g fatty pork mince
- 1 tbsp shaoxing wine
- 1 tsp dark soy sauce
- 40 g ya cai
- 2 tbsp light soy sauce
- 4 tbsp chilli oil
- 1 tbsp sesame paste
- 2 tsp black vinegar
- 1 tsp ground sichuan pepper
- 200 g dried wheat noodles
- 20 g spring onions, green parts only, finely sliced

## Method

1. Heat the peanut oil in a frying pan over high heat until it shimmers. Add the pork and press it flat against the base. Fry 4 minutes without stirring, then break it up and fry 4 minutes more, until the water it released has gone and the meat is dark, dry and crumbly with crisp edges. This is the texture Sichuan cooks call su, and it is the thing the dish is judged on.
2. Add the shaoxing wine and the dark soy sauce and stir until the pan is dry again, about 60 seconds.
3. Add the ya cai and fry 90 seconds, until it smells sweet and toasty and has darkened a shade. Take the pan off the heat. Do not rinse the ya cai first and do not add salt anywhere — it brings its own, and a good deal of it.
4. In each of two bowls, put half the light soy sauce, chilli oil, sesame paste, black vinegar and ground sichuan pepper. Take the chilli oil from the bottom of the jar so it carries sediment as well as oil; the flakes are half of what you are tasting.
5. Bring a large pan of water to a rolling boil and put the noodles in. Cook them to the packet time and no longer — these want a definite bite, since they finish in a hot bowl.
6. When the noodles are 30 seconds from done, take 3 tablespoons of the boiling water out of the pan and stir half into each bowl. The stiff paste goes loose and glossy and just runs. The starch in that water is what makes the sauce cling to noodles rather than sit under them; plain hot water does not do the same thing.
7. Drain the noodles, but not too thoroughly, and pile them straight onto the sauce.
8. Spoon the pork and ya cai over the top and heap the spring onion greens on. Take it to the table.
9. Toss it hard for 20 seconds before eating — right down to the bottom of the bowl, until every strand is the colour of the sauce. This is not optional and it is the eater's job, not the cook's.

## Notes

This is the Chengdu dish rather than the Japanese one, and it is here because
of the bowl Barshu serves in Soho — small, dry, brown rather than red, and
much more savoury than the name leads people to expect. What makes it that
way is the ya cai and the dry-fried pork, not the chilli.

**Ya cai is the ingredient.** Yibin sui mi ya cai: mustard stems chopped to
rice-grain size, salted, sun-dried and fermented, sold in small foil packets in
any Chinese supermarket for about a pound. It is dark brown, dry, and sweet as
well as salty, and it is what makes dan dan noodles taste like dan dan noodles.
Zha cai and pickled mustard greens are both in this collection and neither is a
substitute — zha cai is the knobbly stem pickle and the wet ones are sour. A
bowl made without ya cai is a bowl of chilli noodles.

**Sesame paste, and only a spoonful.** The Chengdu original has none at all, and
Fuchsia Dunlop — who has consulted for Barshu since it opened — publishes the
version without it. What Barshu's bowl tastes of is a small amount: enough to
round the soy and the chilli oil and thicken the sauce, not enough to make it
the sesame noodle dish the Japanese descendant became. One tablespoon between
two is the amount. Three would be Tantanmen (Soup) without the soup.

Take the chilli oil from the bottom of the jar. Clear oil off the top brings
heat and colour and none of the toasted flake flavour that is most of the point.
Four tablespoons between two bowls sounds enormous and is roughly what a
restaurant uses.

A small bowl. This is street food and a snack, and the portion is about half
what a Western bowl of noodles would be — 100 g of dry noodles each. As a
starter before other dishes it feeds four.

Plain wheat noodles, not egg noodles. Dan dan mian is made with thin white
flour-and-water noodles; the yellow springy Cantonese egg noodle is a different
texture and reads immediately as the wrong dish. Any Chinese supermarket sells
plain dried ones, and they cost less.

The sichuan pepper goes in the bowl and not in the pan. Ground and then heated,
it loses the aroma and keeps only the numbness, which is the half nobody wants
on its own. Grind it fresh; a jar open for two years is doing nothing.

This bowl and Tantanmen (Soup) are the same dish two hundred years and two
countries apart, and they eat nothing alike. Japan added the soup, the sesame
and the volume; this is what it started as.

## Storage

The pork and ya cai keep four days in the fridge and freeze for two months.
Make three times as much: it is the slow part, and it goes on rice, on plain
noodles and into lettuce cups.

The assembled bowl keeps for nothing at all. Sauce sets stiff around cold
noodles and no amount of reheating gets it back onto them.

The sauce can be stirred together and kept two days in the fridge without the
noodle water in it. Bring it back to room temperature before loosening, or the
sesame paste will not emulsify.
