## Ingredients

- 300 g pork belly, sliced 3 mm thick
- 30 g ginger, finely grated
- 2 tbsp light soy sauce
- 2 tbsp mirin
- 1 tbsp sake
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 1 tbsp neutral oil
- 150 g onion, sliced 5 mm
- 150 g white cabbage, very finely shredded

## Method

1. Grate the ginger on the finest holes of a grater, into a bowl, and keep every drop of juice — 30 g gives about 2 tablespoons of wet pulp. Whisk in the soy sauce, mirin, sake and sugar until no grains are left on the bottom. Set it beside the hob.
2. Shred the cabbage as finely as you can manage, drop it into cold water for 5 minutes, then drain it and dry it in a towel. It goes from limp to crisp, and that is worth the five minutes.
3. Heat the oil in a 28 cm frying pan over high heat until it shimmers and moves in one sheet. Lay the pork in a single layer with the slices not touching — two batches if the pan is small.
4. Fry for 90 seconds without moving it, until the underside is deep brown and the fat at the edge has gone from white to clear. Turn and fry 60 seconds more.
5. Add the onion and fry for 2 minutes, stirring, until it has gone translucent at the edges and softened but still has some body to it.
6. Pour in the sauce. It will boil the moment it lands. Turn the pork through it for 60 to 90 seconds, until the sauce has gone from a liquid to a syrup that clings to the meat and there is almost none loose in the pan — a spoon drawn across the base should leave a trail that closes slowly.
7. Pile the cabbage on two plates, lay the pork and onion beside it, and scrape every bit of the sauce over the meat. Serve with rice.

## Notes

Grated, not chopped, and not sliced. Shogayaki is "ginger-fried", and the ginger
has to be a wet purée that coats the meat — 30 g of it, which looks like far too
much and is the correct amount. Chopped ginger gives you pork with bits of
ginger on it, which is a different and worse dish.

Do not marinate. Every instinct says to leave the pork in that sauce for an hour
and it is a mistake: raw ginger carries a protease, the same enzyme that sets
Ginger Milk Curd, and an hour of it on thin pork turns the surface to mush. The
sauce goes in at the end, once the meat is already cooked, and it takes ninety
seconds to do its job.

The sauce also goes in at the end because it would burn at the start. It is a
tablespoon of sugar's worth of mirin and sugar between them, and that
caramelises and then blackens long before the pork is done.

Belly or loin, and they are different dishes. Belly is what this is written for
— richer, and the rendered fat carries the sauce. Loin is the teishoku version
you get in a set-lunch shop: use 300 g of lean pork sliced 3 mm, cut the frying
to 60 seconds a side, and expect something cleaner and drier.

The panel reads 966 kcal and 87 g of fat a serving, which is 150 g of pork
belly each and is exactly what that costs. Two things soften it: a good deal of
the fat renders into the pan and stays there rather than reaching the plate, and
the loin version comes in around 400 kcal for the same weight of meat. This is
the rich one on purpose; if it is a weeknight dinner rather than a treat, use
the loin.

The cabbage is a component. Finely shredded raw cabbage is what cuts a sauce
this sweet and salty, and a plate of shogayaki without it is heavy by the fourth
mouthful. Shred it thinner than feels sensible.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge, in the sauce, covered. The pork firms up cold and comes
back fine.

Reheat in a dry frying pan over medium heat for 3 minutes, turning, which
re-melts the sauce onto the meat. A microwave works and gives you softer pork
and a looser sauce. Shred the cabbage fresh either way — day-old shredded
cabbage is a wet grey thing.

Cold, sliced, it is very good in a sandwich with butter and mustard, which is
not Japanese and is the best use of the last two slices.
