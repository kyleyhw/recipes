## Ingredients

- 1.5 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1 tbsp oyster sauce
- 1 tbsp mirin
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 0.25 tsp white pepper
- 1.5 tbsp neutral oil
- 150 g pork belly, sliced 3 mm, in 3 cm pieces
- 150 g onion, sliced 5 mm
- 200 g white cabbage, cut into 3 cm squares
- 400 g udon noodles
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Whisk the soy sauce, oyster sauce, mirin, sugar and white pepper together in a cup until the sugar has gone. Set it beside the hob — once the frying starts there is no time.
2. Loosen the udon. Frozen noodles go into boiling water for 60 seconds and are then drained hard; vacuum-packed ones can be rinsed under a hot tap and pulled apart with your fingers. Either way they must be separate strands before they go near the pan, and as dry as you can get them.
3. Heat the oil in a 28 cm frying pan or wok over high heat until it shimmers. Add the pork in one layer and fry 3 minutes without stirring, until the underside is browned and the fat has run.
4. Add the onion and fry 2 minutes, until it has softened at the edges. Add the cabbage and fry 2 minutes more, tossing, until the leaves have wilted but the stems still snap.
5. Add the noodles. Now stop tossing: spread them out and leave them for 90 seconds, so the strands against the metal catch and blister. Turn everything over once and give it another 60 seconds.
6. Pour the sauce round the edge of the pan rather than into the middle, so it hits hot metal and reduces on the way in. Toss for 60 seconds, until every strand is coloured and there is nothing loose in the bottom of the pan.
7. Take it off the heat, scatter the spring onion, and serve straight from the pan.

## Notes

Let the noodles catch. This is the whole difference between yaki udon and warm
noodles in sauce, and it means not touching them for ninety seconds in a pan
that is too hot for comfort. The strands that blister and brown against the
metal are the ones people remember.

Dry noodles, hot pan, and do not crowd it. Four hundred grams in a 28 cm pan is
already at the limit; if yours is smaller, fry it in two batches and combine at
step 6. Wet or clumped noodles steam, and steamed noodles never catch.

Sauce round the edge, not into the middle. A cold sauce poured onto the middle
of a full pan drops the temperature and everything stews; poured down the hot
side it is already reducing before it reaches the food. The same rule as the
shaoxing wine in every stir-fry on this site.

Oyster sauce is the Chinese-restaurant-in-Japan version and the one most people
make at home. The other standard is Worcestershire sauce, which sounds absurd
and is genuinely what a lot of Japanese recipes call for — the two are more
alike than you would think, both being fermented, sweet and sharp. A tablespoon
of it in place of the oyster sauce works.

This is the only thing in the udon section with no broth at all, and it is what
to make with a packet of noodles and whatever is in the fridge. Prawns, chicken
thigh, mushrooms and bean sprouts all go in at the appropriate point; the
technique does not change.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge, covered. It is one of the better fried-noodle leftovers
because there is no sauce left to be absorbed — it has already reduced on.

Reheat in a dry hot frying pan for 3 minutes, spread out and left alone, which
crisps it again. A microwave gives you soft noodles and is what a lunchbox gets.

It does not freeze; the cabbage weeps and the noodles go to paste.
