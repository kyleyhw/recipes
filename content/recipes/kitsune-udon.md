## Ingredients

- 50 g aburaage
- 900 ml dashi
- 3 tbsp light soy sauce
- 3 tbsp mirin
- 1 tbsp granulated sugar
- 400 g udon noodles
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Put the aburaage in a sieve and pour a kettle of boiling water over both sides, then press the sheets between your palms over the sink. A surprising amount of oil comes out, and it is oil that would otherwise sit in a slick on the broth. Cut each sheet in half into two rectangles.
2. Put 150 ml of the dashi, 1 tbsp of the soy sauce, 1 tbsp of the mirin and all the sugar in a small pan and bring to a simmer. Lay the aburaage in flat, in one layer.
3. Simmer over low heat for 15 minutes, turning the pieces once halfway. Cover them with a small lid or a circle of baking paper resting directly on them if they float. They are done when almost all the liquid has gone and each piece is deep brown, glossy and heavy — squeeze one gently against the side of the pan and sweet liquid should run out. Leave them in what is left of their liquid.
4. Put the remaining 750 ml of dashi with the remaining 2 tbsp of soy sauce and 2 tbsp of mirin in a pan and bring to a bare simmer.
5. Cook the udon in a separate pan of boiling water — 1 to 2 minutes for frozen or vacuum-packed, 8 to 10 for dried — and drain well.
6. Put the noodles in two deep warmed bowls, ladle the hot broth over until they are submerged, and lay two pieces of aburaage on top of each, spooning any leftover simmering liquid over them.
7. Scatter the spring onion and eat at once, while the aburaage is still hot enough to flood when you bite it.

## Notes

Blanch the sheets first. Aburaage is deep-fried and comes out of the packet
carrying a great deal of oil that has been sitting there since it was made; a
kettle of boiling water and a squeeze takes most of it off, and without that
step the broth ends up with a film on it and the sheets taste stale rather than
sweet. It also lets them absorb the simmering liquid, which they cannot do while
full of oil.

The aburaage is the dish. Everything else here is plain kake broth — the same
dashi, soy and mirin as Tsukimi Udon — and the whole character comes from two
rectangles of fried tofu that have spent fifteen minutes drinking a sweet
seasoning. Rushing that simmer is the only real mistake available.

Almost all the liquid should go. If there is still a pool in the pan at 15
minutes, take the lid off and give it 5 more; the sheets need to have taken it
up rather than to be sitting in it.

The sweetness is deliberate and higher than anything else in the udon section —
a full tablespoon of sugar for 50 g of tofu. Against a plain salty broth that
contrast is the point, and cutting the sugar makes a more balanced and less
interesting bowl.

Kitsune means fox, and the story is that foxes like fried tofu. Tanuki udon is
the other animal one, topped with tempura scraps instead.

Make four times as much aburaage as you need. It keeps a week, it is the same
preparation that goes into inarizushi, and the fifteen-minute simmer is the only
slow part of this recipe.

## Storage

The simmered aburaage keeps five days in the fridge in its liquid, covered, and
freezes for two months. This is the part worth making ahead, and it turns the
dish into a ten-minute one.

The broth keeps three days and freezes. The noodles keep for nothing — cook them
to order.

Assembled, it does not keep at all: the noodles soften into the broth and the
aburaage goes flabby.
