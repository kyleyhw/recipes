## Ingredients

- 300 g bread flour
- 40 g caster sugar
- 5 g salt
- 7 g fast-action dried yeast
- 150 ml milk, warmed to 35 °C
- 1 egg
- 40 g unsalted butter, softened
- 60 g unsalted butter, softened, for the topping
- 80 g icing sugar
- 1 egg yolk, for the topping
- 100 g all-purpose flour
- 10 g custard powder
- 0.5 tsp baking powder
- 1 egg yolk, for the glaze

## Method

1. Stir the bread flour, caster sugar, salt and yeast together in a large bowl for 10 seconds. Keep the salt and the yeast on opposite sides of the bowl until they are through the flour; salt sitting directly on yeast kills it.
2. Pour in the milk and the whole egg and mix to a shaggy dough with a wooden spoon, then knead on an unfloured surface for 10 minutes. It goes from sticky and tearing to smooth and elastic. Resist adding flour — this dough is meant to be tacky and it stops sticking on its own at about minute six.
3. Work the 40 g of butter in a third at a time, 4 minutes in all. The dough comes apart and looks ruined each time and comes back together each time. It is done when you can stretch a piece thin enough to see light through it without it tearing.
4. Cover the bowl and prove for 75 minutes at room temperature, until the dough has doubled and a floured finger pushed 2 cm in leaves a dent that fills back only halfway.
5. While it proves, make the topping. Beat the 60 g of butter with the icing sugar for 2 minutes until pale, beat in the first egg yolk, then sift in the all-purpose flour, custard powder and baking powder and fold to a stiff paste. Roll it into a log, wrap it, and chill it for at least 30 minutes.
6. Knock the dough back, divide it into 8 pieces of 65 g, and roll each into a tight ball by cupping your hand over it and moving it in circles against the worktop until the surface goes taut. Set them on two lined trays, 6 cm apart.
7. Prove for 45 minutes, until they are half again as big and a fingertip pressed lightly into one leaves a mark that fills slowly.
8. Heat the oven to 190 °C. Cut the topping log into 8 discs and roll each between two sheets of baking paper into a 8 cm round, 3 mm thick — slightly smaller than the bun, because it spreads.
9. Lay a round over each bun and press only the very edges down. Beat the second egg yolk with 1 tsp of water and brush it over the topping alone; egg on the exposed dough bakes to a dark ring.
10. Bake for 16 minutes, swapping the shelves at 8 minutes. The topping cracks into its pattern in the first 6 minutes, in the oven, and there is nothing to do to encourage it. They are done when the cracks have gone deep gold and the dough showing between them is pale brown.
11. Cool on a rack for 20 minutes. The topping is soft out of the oven and crisps as it cools.

## Notes

There is no pineapple. The name is for the crazed pattern the topping makes,
which looks like the skin of one, and it is worth saying out loud because it is
the first question anyone asks.

The topping is a biscuit dough, not an icing, and the crack is not something
you score or help along. It happens because the paste has no water and no
stretch: as the bun rises underneath it, the lid cannot follow and breaks. A
topping rolled too thick will not crack; a topping rolled too thin tears into
crumbs. Three millimetres is the number.

Bread flour for the bun, plain flour for the lid, and they are not
interchangeable. The bun wants gluten so it can hold gas and stay soft for a
day; the lid wants as little as possible so it stays short and breaks cleanly.
Both are on an ordinary supermarket shelf.

Custard powder is doing two jobs and neither of them is thickening: it is
where the yellow comes from and where most of the vanilla does. Cornflour will
substitute for the body and gives a pale, plain-tasting lid.

菠蘿油 is the same bun split while still warm with a 1 cm slab of cold salted
butter pushed into it, and it is the better way to eat this. The butter half
melts and half stays solid, which is the identical trick to the one in Kaya
Toast, and the two are worth eating side by side.

This is the first yeasted dough in the collection, and the same dough — through
step 4 — is what the Cocktail Buns are built on. Doubling it and splitting the
batch is the sensible way to make either.

## Storage

Two days at room temperature in a paper bag, not plastic: plastic keeps the bun
soft and turns the topping to a damp paste, which loses the only textural
contrast the thing has. By day three the bun is dry.

Reheat at 160 °C for 6 minutes, which brings back both halves — the crumb
softens and the lid re-crisps. A microwave does the first and destroys the
second.

They freeze well, baked and cooled, bagged with the air pressed out, for a
month. Reheat from frozen at 170 °C for 12 minutes. Freezing is much better
than a third day at room temperature.
