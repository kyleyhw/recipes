## Ingredients

- 100 g blanched almonds
- 30 g jasmine rice
- 700 ml water
- 60 g granulated sugar
- 100 ml evaporated milk

## Method

1. Put the almonds and the rice in a bowl, cover them with 3 cm of cold tap water and leave them for 4 hours, or overnight in the fridge. An almond is ready when you can split it in half with a thumbnail and the cut face is white all the way through rather than chalky in the middle. This is not optional: a dry almond shreds in a blender and gives up a fraction of what a soaked one does.
2. Drain them and throw the soaking water away.
3. Put them in a blender with 350 ml of the fresh water and run it on full for 2 minutes. Stop at 1 minute and scrape the sides down. It goes from gritty to the texture of thin paint, and rubbed between two fingers it should feel smooth with no grain at all.
4. Line a sieve with a doubled square of muslin over a bowl and pour the mixture through it. Gather the corners and wring the bag out until it stops dripping and the pulp inside is a dry ball.
5. Tip the pulp back into the blender with the remaining 350 ml of water, blend 1 minute and strain it the same way. This second pass is where a third of the almond is: skip it and you have thrown away a third of what you paid for.
6. Pour the strained liquid into a 20 cm saucepan and stir in the sugar.
7. Cook over medium heat for 6 minutes, stirring constantly and scraping the base with a spatula. It thickens enough to coat the back of the spoon and turns from chalky white to faintly glossy. **It must not boil.** The rice starch catches on the base the moment it does, and a scorched batch tastes burnt all the way through with no way to rescue it.
8. Take it off the heat and stir in the evaporated milk. Serve at once, hot, in bowls or heatproof glasses.

## Notes

The real thing is not made with almonds. It is made with apricot kernels — 南杏,
the sweet ones, with a small handful of 北杏, the bitter ones, which is where the
sharp perfumed almond smell of a dessert-shop bowl comes from. Bitter apricot
kernels contain amygdalin, which releases cyanide as they are broken down; they
are used in small, cooked quantities for exactly that reason, and they are not
on an ordinary supermarket shelf. So this uses blanched almonds, which are, and
the difference is real: this version is rounder, sweeter and much less
perfumed — nuttier, and less like the smell of marzipan.

**Do not close that gap by buying bitter apricot kernels and using them by
weight in place of the almonds here.** The traditional proportion is a few
bitter to a hundred sweet, and it is not a substitution this recipe has been
written for. What closes it safely is ¼ tsp of almond extract, stirred in with
the evaporated milk at the end — it is the same aroma compound, out of a bottle,
and it gets most of the way there.

The rice is not padding. Almond and water alone separates into a grey layer and
a cloudy one within a minute of sitting still, and no amount of blending fixes
it. Thirty grams of rice, gelatinised in step 7, is what holds the two together
and gives the drink the slight body it is supposed to have — the same job the
starch does in a Cantonese sweet soup.

Strain twice, and wring the bag properly. It is the difference between a drink
and almond-flavoured water, and the pulp that comes out of the second pass is
worth tasting once so you can see what the first pass was leaving behind.

A dessert shop serves this at a consistency between milk and single cream. For
the thicker 杏仁糊 — almond paste, eaten with a spoon — cut the water to 500 ml
and use 40 g of rice.

Iced, it is good and it is what comes in the cartons: cook it as written, cool
it completely, and refrigerate. It thickens in the fridge, so let it down with
a splash of milk before pouring.

A bowl carries 470 kcal, three quarters of it the almonds, and 30 g of sugar.
It is a dessert rather than a drink you have with something. What it has none of
is caffeine, which is unusual for a hot drink and is the reason this is what
gets made at eleven at night.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge, covered. It separates as it stands and that is not a
fault — whisk it back together cold, then reheat it in a saucepan over low heat,
stirring, until it steams. Do not let it boil on the way back up any more than
on the way down, and do not use a microwave, which heats it in patches and
splits it.

Do not freeze it. The starch that holds it together breaks on thawing and comes
back as grit in water, which is the one thing that cannot be whisked out.
