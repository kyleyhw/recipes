## Ingredients

- 200 g cooked rice
- 1 egg
- 1 tsp light soy sauce
- 5 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. The rice must be properly hot — straight from the cooker, or microwaved until it steams when you stir it. This is the entire technique: the heat is what thickens the egg into a sauce, and lukewarm rice leaves you with raw egg sliding off cold grains.
2. Crack the egg into a small bowl and beat it with chopsticks for 15 seconds, cutting through it rather than whipping, until the white is broken up and no clear ribbon is left. A little foam on top is right.
3. Make a well in the middle of the rice with the back of a spoon. Pour the egg in, add the soy sauce, and stir hard for 10 seconds.
4. It is done when the whole bowl has turned pale yellow and glossy, the rice moves as one soft mass rather than as separate grains, and a spoon leaves a trail that fills in slowly. Scatter the spring onion over and eat it immediately, while it is still loose.

## Notes

Raw egg, so the egg has to be one you would eat raw. In the UK that means a
British Lion mark: the scheme vaccinates the flocks and the Food Standards
Agency's position is that those eggs are safe raw, including for pregnant
people, infants and the elderly. Japanese eggs are washed, dated and sold
explicitly for this. Anywhere else, check before assuming. Freshness matters
more here than in anything else on this site — a week-old egg tastes faintly of
the fridge and there is nothing to hide it behind.

Soy-butter rice is the other two-minute bowl and the one to make when there is
no egg you trust: 10 g of cold butter stirred through the hot rice until it has
melted, then a teaspoon of soy. Add an egg yolk on top and you have the version
most people actually eat, which is neither one thing nor the other and is better
than both.

A teaspoon of soy is enough because the rice is unseasoned and there is nothing
else in the bowl. Two makes it salty rather than savoury, and there is no way
back once it is stirred in.

Things that go on, one at a time: a spoonful of natto, a torn sheet of nori,
a pinch of katsuobushi, or a few drops of sesame oil. Furikake is the shop-bought
version of all of them at once.

The yolk-only version is richer and more custardy — use two yolks and no whites,
and save the whites for a meringue you will not make.

## Storage

There is none, and this is not a limitation of the recipe. Egg on hot rice
carries on setting from the moment it is stirred in; two minutes later it is
already grainier, and cold it is inedible. Make it when you are going to eat it.
