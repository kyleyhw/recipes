## Ingredients

- 150 g walnuts
- 40 g jasmine rice
- 900 ml water
- 60 g granulated sugar

## Method

1. Put the rice in a bowl, cover it with cold water, and leave it for 30 minutes. Drain it. Do this first and the rest takes twenty minutes.
2. Heat the oven to 180 °C and spread the walnuts on a tray. Toast for 8 minutes, until they smell strongly nutty and the skins have darkened and cracked. Watch them from 6 minutes; walnuts go from toasted to acrid quickly.
3. Tip the hot walnuts into a clean tea towel, fold it over, and rub them hard against themselves for 20 seconds. Most of the papery skins will come off. Pick the nuts out and leave the loose skins behind — they carry nearly all of the bitterness, and this 20 seconds is the difference between a sweet soup and a tannic one. Getting every last piece off is not necessary.
4. Put the walnuts, the drained rice and the water in a blender and run it for 2 minutes, until completely smooth. Do it in two batches if the jug is small. Rub a drop between finger and thumb: if you can feel grit, blend for another minute.
5. Pour the mixture through a fine sieve into a saucepan, pressing the solids with the back of a ladle to get everything through. What is left in the sieve is spent and goes in the bin.
6. Add the sugar and set the pan over medium-low heat. Stir constantly with a flat-ended spatula, keeping it moving across the base and into the corners, for 5 to 8 minutes.
7. It will do nothing for several minutes and then thicken all at once. Take it off the moment it coats the back of the spatula and a finger drawn through leaves a line that holds. Do not stop stirring at any point — the rice starch catches on the base within seconds and a scorched batch tastes of it throughout.
8. Pour into bowls and serve hot.

## Notes

The rice is the thickener and it is why there is no cornflour in this. Blended
raw and cooked out, it gives a soup with body that still tastes clean, where a
starch slurry gives one that tastes faintly of starch. Forty grams to 900 ml is
the ratio; more makes it gluey.

The skins are the whole quality question. Untoasted, unrubbed walnuts make a
soup that is drinkable and slightly bitter and slightly dusty, and everyone who
has had a disappointing one has had that. Toasting also does most of the work of
the flavour, so it is two minutes of oven time buying the entire dish.

Sieving is not optional either. Walnuts have enough cell wall in them that even
a two-minute blend leaves fine grit, and it is very obvious in a smooth soup.

Black sesame soup — 芝麻糊, the better-known cousin — is this recipe with 100 g
of toasted black sesame seeds in place of the walnuts and no skins to rub off.
Everything else is identical, including the rice, the sieve and the stirring.
Walnut is the milder and sweeter of the two; sesame is darker, more bitter and
more striking.

Toasted in a dry frying pan works if you would rather not heat the oven: medium
heat, 5 minutes, shaking constantly. It is less even and quite a lot easier to
burn.

Serve it hot and in small bowls. This is rich — 150 g of walnuts across four
people — and a big bowl of it defeats most people halfway down.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge, covered. It sets almost solid when cold, which is
normal.

Reheat in a pan over low heat with 50 ml of water per bowl, stirring until it
flows again. It will catch on the base just as readily the second time, so keep
stirring. A microwave works if you stir it twice on the way through.

It freezes for two months and comes back slightly grainy; a hard whisk while
reheating puts it back together.
