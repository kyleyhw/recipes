## Ingredients

- 200 ml dashi
- 2 tbsp light soy sauce
- 2 tbsp mirin
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 110 g onion (1), sliced 5 mm thick
- 300 g chicken thigh, in 3 cm pieces
- 4 eggs
- 400 g cooked rice
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Put the dashi, soy sauce, mirin and sugar in a 20 cm frying pan and bring to a simmer over medium heat.
2. Add the onion and simmer for 3 minutes, until the slices have gone translucent at the edges and slumped but still have a bite.
3. Add the chicken in one layer and simmer for 5 minutes, turning the pieces once, until they are white all the way through and the liquid has reduced to about half a centimetre in the pan.
4. Beat the eggs badly. Ten seconds with chopsticks, so there are still ribbons of white and streaks of unmixed yolk — this is not carelessness, it is the dish. Evenly beaten egg sets into one flat sheet of omelette, and the streaks are what give oyakodon its two textures.
5. Pour two thirds of the egg over the pan in a spiral, starting at the edge. Put the lid on and cook for 60 seconds, until it has just set at the rim and is still liquid in the middle.
6. Pour the remaining third over, put the lid back on and take the pan straight off the heat. Leave it for 30 seconds. The residual heat sets the second lot only barely, and that is the layer you want on top.
7. Divide the rice between two deep bowls. Slide half the pan onto each with a spatula, keeping it in one piece and letting the sauce run down into the rice. Scatter the spring onion over and eat at once.

## Notes

Oyako means parent and child, and the dish is chicken and its egg. It is one of
the small handful of Japanese rice bowls that is genuinely a weeknight thing:
one pan, twelve minutes, and the only ingredient that needs buying is the
chicken.

The sauce is Gyudon's sauce at a different strength — dashi, soy, mirin, sugar
— and if you have made one you can make the other. That one uses 200 ml for
beef and this uses the same for chicken; what differs is that the egg here goes
in the pan rather than raw on top.

Two pours, and the second one off the heat. The mistake is to add all the egg
at once and cook until it looks done, which gives a firm sheet of scrambled egg
sitting on chicken. What you are after is the bottom layer just set into the
sauce and the top layer glossy and only half cooked, and there is no way to get
both out of one addition.

A 20 cm pan for two, or one small pan per bowl if you have them. Japanese
kitchens use a shallow lidded pan the size of a single serving, and the reason
is that the egg has to be in a layer a centimetre deep — spread across a 28 cm
frying pan it sets before the underside has met the sauce.

Mitsuba is the herb this traditionally finishes with, and it is hard to buy
outside a Japanese grocer. Spring onion is the substitution everyone makes and
it is fine; a few leaves of flat-leaf parsley are closer to mitsuba's actual
flavour and look wrong, which is why nobody does it.

Runny egg means eggs you would eat raw. In the UK that means the British Lion
mark — Tamago Kake Gohan's notes have that argument in full.

## Storage

Eat it now. The rice takes up the sauce as it sits, the egg firms, and within
twenty minutes it is a different and worse dish.

The chicken and its sauce, without the egg, keep two days in the fridge and are
a good thing to have: reheat 300 g of it in a pan with a splash of water, pour
the egg over, and dinner is four minutes rather than twelve.

It does not freeze.
