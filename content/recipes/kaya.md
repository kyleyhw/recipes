## Ingredients

- 3 eggs
- 1 egg yolk
- 150 g caster sugar
- 0.25 tsp salt
- 200 ml coconut milk
- 3 pandan leaves

## Method

1. Rinse the pandan leaves and tie each one into a loose knot. The knot is not decoration: it keeps the leaf in one piece so it can be lifted out whole at the end, and it bruises the leaf along the fold, which is where the scent comes out.
2. Whisk the eggs, the egg yolk, the caster sugar and the salt in a heatproof bowl for 60 seconds, until you can no longer feel the sugar grating under the whisk and the mixture is one even pale yellow. Whisk it flat, not fluffy — air beaten in here becomes a permanent foam on the finished jam.
3. Whisk in the coconut milk until the colour is even and no streaks of yolk are left.
4. Pour the mixture through a fine sieve into a second heatproof bowl and drop the pandan knots in. The sieve takes out the chalazae, the two white cords beside the yolk, which never dissolve and would sit in the finished jam as threads.
5. Set the bowl over a pan holding 5 cm of simmering water, the water not touching the base of the bowl. Turn the heat down until the water shows two or three bubbles a second and no more.
6. Stir constantly with a silicone spatula for 45 minutes, scraping the base and the sides of the bowl on every pass. Nothing appears to happen for the first 25 minutes; then it goes from a thin liquid to a pouring custard in about five, and thickens from there.
7. It is done at 82 °C on a thermometer, or when a spatula drawn across the base of the bowl leaves a clear stripe that takes 2 seconds to close over. It looks too thin at this point and it is not — the jam sets as it cools, and over-cooking past this is the commonest way a first batch is ruined.
8. If grains or lumps appear at any point, take the bowl off the pan immediately and give it 20 seconds with a stick blender. It comes back smooth and nobody can tell.
9. Lift out the pandan knots, pressing each against the side of the bowl to squeeze the liquid back in, and throw them away. Spoon the kaya into a clean jar and leave the lid off on the counter for 1 hour, until it is cold and spreadable. Then lid on, into the fridge.

## Notes

The pandan is the whole flavour and there is no substitute for it. Frozen
leaves, which is how an Asian supermarket usually sells them, work as well as
fresh — the leaf is thrown away and only what it gave to the custard stays, and
freezing costs that almost nothing. Pandan essence is a different smell and a
colour that belongs on a cake.

What it is for is Kaya Toast — thin toast, cold salted butter in slabs, and a
half-boiled egg beside it. It is also good on a plain steamed bun, stirred
through hot congee, or spread between the layers of a sponge.

Homemade kaya is pale yellow with a green cast, not the flat bright green of a
jar, which is coloured. If it comes out beige rather than green that is the eggs
showing through and nothing has gone wrong.

The other kaya is brown. Hainanese-style kaya, the sort Singapore coffee shops
serve, caramelises part of the sugar first, which turns the jam the colour of
toffee and gives it a bitter edge under the sweetness. To make it: melt 30 g of
the sugar in a dry pan over medium heat for 3 minutes to a dark amber, take it
off the heat, and stir the coconut milk in — carefully, it spits — before that
goes into the eggs.

This is about a third sugar by weight, which is less than the 45% a commercial
jar runs at. It tastes of coconut and egg rather than of sugar, and it is not
shelf stable at that concentration, which is why it lives in the fridge.

The bowl over water is what makes this 45 minutes rather than 20, and it is
worth the extra 25. Over direct heat this thickens fast and scrambles the
moment your attention goes elsewhere; over water it cannot get hotter than the
water and will not curdle if you keep stirring. Either way, constantly means
constantly.

The spare white from the egg yolk freezes perfectly in a small tub and is worth
keeping — whites freeze, yolks do not.

## Storage

Two weeks in the fridge in a jar with a lid, and it is best after a day. Take it
out ten minutes before you want it, since it spreads badly straight from the
cold.

This is a fresh egg custard and not a preserve. It does not go in the cupboard
at any point, and there is not enough sugar in it to make that safe. Throw it
away if it smells sour or yeasty rather than sweet, or if water has wept out at
the edge of the jar.

It freezes for three months in a tub. Defrost it overnight in the fridge and
stir it hard for 20 seconds — it comes out of the freezer looking slightly
separated and comes back together entirely.

Do not warm it up to loosen it. Heat splits it and there is no way back.
