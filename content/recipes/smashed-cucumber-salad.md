## Ingredients

- 400 g cucumber (2 short ones)
- 0.5 tsp salt
- 12 g garlic, crushed to a paste
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1 tbsp black vinegar
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 2 tsp sesame oil
- 1 tbsp chilli oil
- 1 tsp toasted sesame seeds

## Method

1. Lay a cucumber on the board and hit it along its length with the flat of a cleaver or the base of a heavy pan — firmly, four or five times, until it splits open and the seed core loosens. It should crack, not flatten. Repeat with the second.
2. Tear or cut the split cucumber into rough 3 cm pieces. Rough is the point: a smashed face is torn and porous where a knife-cut one is sealed and slick, and dressing clings to the first and slides off the second.
3. Toss the pieces with the salt in a colander set over a bowl and leave for 15 minutes. Water will bead on the surfaces and run out; by the end you should have two or three tablespoons in the bowl beneath.
4. Meanwhile stir the garlic, soy sauce, black vinegar, sugar and sesame oil together in a small bowl until the sugar has gone. Taste it. It should be sharp enough to make you blink — it is about to be diluted by whatever water the cucumber still holds.
5. Shake the colander hard and tip the cucumber into a serving bowl, leaving the drained liquid behind. Do not rinse it; the salt that is left is the seasoning.
6. Pour the dressing over, add the chilli oil, and toss with your hands or two spoons until every piece is coated and the bottom of the bowl has a shallow pool of red-brown dressing. Scatter the sesame seeds over and serve within the next ten minutes.

## Notes

The salting and draining is the recipe. A cucumber is 95% water and a dressed
one is a machine for releasing it: skip the fifteen minutes and what reaches the
table is correct for about ninety seconds, after which the cucumber weeps into
the bowl and you are eating dressing soup. Fifteen minutes takes out enough that
the dressing stays a dressing.

Buy the short ridged Chinese cucumbers or the Persian ones if the shop has them.
Thinner skin, fewer seeds, more crunch, and they smash into better pieces than
the long British sort, whose watery seed core is worth scraping out with a spoon
before smashing.

The garlic wants to be a paste rather than chopped — crushed with the side of a
knife and a pinch of the salt, or put through a press. Chopped garlic sits in
pieces on the cucumber and bites in one mouthful out of four; paste disperses
into the dressing and seasons all of it.

Chinkiang black vinegar is what makes this taste like the dish rather than like
a cucumber in vinaigrette. It is malty and slightly sweet where rice vinegar is
clean and sharp. If you only have rice vinegar, use it with a little more sugar
and accept that it is a different, brighter salad.

## Storage

An hour in the fridge is the outside limit, and it is already past its best at
thirty minutes — the pieces go from crisp to bendy and the dressing thins as more
water comes out. It is a salad to make and eat, not one to make ahead.

If some is left, it is still good the next day in the way a quick pickle is good:
soft, sour, fine chopped into fried rice or eaten out of the box standing up. It
is not the dish it was.

It does not freeze, and it would be an odd thing to try.
