## Ingredients

- 200 ml dashi
- 1.5 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1.5 tbsp mirin
- 300 g spinach
- 5 g katsuobushi

## Method

1. Bring the dashi, soy sauce and mirin to a simmer in a small pan, take it off the heat at the first bubbles, and leave it to cool to room temperature. Do not let it boil — boiling drives off the aroma that is the whole reason for using dashi rather than water.
2. Bring a large pan of water to a rolling boil. Do not salt it.
3. Hold the spinach by the stem ends and lower the stems in for 15 seconds before letting the leaves follow. Blanch 45 seconds more, until the leaves have gone dark and slack and the stems still have spring in them.
4. Lift it straight into a bowl of cold water. Spinach left to cool in its own heat goes olive and slimy, and no later step recovers it.
5. Gather the cooled spinach into a ball and squeeze hard. Turn it and squeeze again, until no more water comes out and you are holding a dense green log. Three hundred grams becomes about 120 g.
6. Cut the log across into 4 cm lengths, loosen the pieces apart, and put them in a bowl or a shallow box.
7. Pour the cooled liquid over. It should come about halfway up. Refrigerate for at least 30 minutes and up to overnight.
8. Lift the spinach out, squeeze it once gently — not hard this time — and pile it into small bowls. Spoon two tablespoons of the steeping liquid over each and scatter the katsuobushi on top. Eat it cold, and drink what is in the bottom of the bowl.

## Notes

This is Goma-ae's spinach with the dressing taken away, and it is worth having
both for exactly that reason. There the squeezed spinach is coated in a thick
sesame paste that clings; here it is steeped in a thin seasoned liquid that
soaks in, and the liquid at the bottom of the bowl is part of the serving
rather than something left behind. Same vegetable, same blanch, opposite idea.

The ratio is about eight parts dashi to one of soy and one of mirin, rounded to
spoons. It is worth knowing on its own: at that strength it is the standard
Japanese steeping liquid and it does the same job for blanched green beans,
tenderstem broccoli, asparagus, okra, or a mixture. Firmer vegetables want two
to three minutes of blanching rather than one and no squeezing at all.

Cool the liquid before the spinach goes in. Warm dashi poured over cold
blanched greens goes on cooking them, and the point of the ice water in step 4
was to stop that happening.

Squeeze hard once and gently once. The first squeeze, before steeping, is what
makes room for the liquid — spinach still full of water cannot take any up. The
second, on the way to the bowl, is only so it does not flood the plate, and
wringing it out here would undo the last half hour.

Katsuobushi on top is not a garnish. It goes limp in the liquid within a minute
and turns from a topping into part of the seasoning, which is why it is added at
the table rather than in the box.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge in its liquid, in a closed box, and it is better on the
second day than the first — the whole design of the dish is that time does the
work. Add the katsuobushi to each serving rather than to the box.

The greens do go progressively darker and softer, so by day three it is a
different texture. That is the limit rather than a safety question.

It does not freeze; blanched spinach does, but not sitting in liquid.
