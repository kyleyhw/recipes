## Ingredients

- 40 g zha cai
- 3 tbsp water
- 1 tsp cornflour
- 1 tbsp shaoxing wine
- 0.5 tsp granulated sugar
- 0.25 tsp white pepper
- 0.5 tsp sesame oil
- 250 g fatty pork mince
- 15 g ginger, finely minced
- 15 g spring onions, green parts only

## Method

1. Rinse the zha cai under cold water for 20 seconds, rubbing off the red paste it is packed in, then squeeze it dry in your fist. Chop it as finely as you have patience for — 2 mm dice, so that no single piece is a mouthful of salt on its own.
2. Put the water, cornflour, shaoxing wine, sugar, white pepper and sesame oil in a mixing bowl and whisk for 20 seconds, until no cornflour is left on the bottom.
3. Add the pork, the ginger and the zha cai. Stir hard with chopsticks in one direction only — never back and forth — for 60 to 90 seconds. Stop the moment it clings to the chopsticks in one mass. Carrying on past that point builds the springy gel a sausage is made of, and the patty steams rubbery.
4. Put a steaming rack in a wok or a wide pan, add water to just below it, and bring it to a rolling boil.
5. Spread the meat into a shallow heatproof dish in an even layer 1.5 cm thick, going right to the edges. Do not press it down.
6. Set the dish on the rack, cover with a tight lid, and steam over high heat for 12 minutes. It is done when the patty has shrunk from the sides of the dish, clear broth has gathered around it, and a knife pushed into the middle comes out with no pink on it.
7. Scatter the spring onion over the top and take it to the table in the dish it steamed in, with plain rice. Spoon the broth over the rice.

## Notes

This is the one people actually cook. Salted fish and dried squid are the
celebrated versions and this is the Tuesday one — a jar of zha cai keeps for a
month in the fridge and turns 250 g of mince into dinner.

No salt and no soy sauce anywhere. Forty grams of zha cai carries about 1700 mg
of sodium, which is more than two salted duck eggs, and the sugar is there to
push against it rather than to sweeten anything. Taste the broth before you
reach for soy at the table.

Rinse and squeeze, both. The paste on the outside is chilli and salt and belongs
down the sink; the squeezing is what stops it wetting the mixture, which matters
because the three tablespoons of water are measured on the assumption that the
zha cai brings none.

Chop it finely. Left in slivers it stays a pickle sitting in a patty, and the
point of the dish is that the sourness runs all the way through the meat.

The technique is the same in every steamed patty here and is written out at
length in Steamed Pork Patty with Preserved Bean Curd: stir until it clings and
no further, 1.5 cm thick, 12 minutes over a rolling boil.

Mince at 20% fat. Zha cai is lean, sour and salty, and there is nothing else in
the bowl to soften it.

Optional, over the rice: Lee Kum Kee's seasoned soy sauce for seafood, or Roast
Meat Soy Dressing if there is no bottle. Go carefully — this is the saltiest of
the patties before anything is poured on it.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge, in the dish, covered, with its broth. It reheats better
than the others: the zha cai keeps its crunch through a second steaming, where
water chestnut goes soft.

Steam it again for 5 minutes, or microwave it covered for 90 seconds. The raw
mixture freezes for a month — spread it in the dish, cover, and steam it for 14
minutes from defrosted.
