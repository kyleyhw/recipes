## Ingredients

- 150 g jasmine rice
- 1 tsp neutral oil
- 1800 ml water
- 20 g ginger, slivered
- 200 g lean pork
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1 tsp cornflour
- 120 g preserved eggs
- 1.5 tsp salt
- 0.5 tsp white pepper
- 20 g spring onions, finely sliced
- 1 tsp sesame oil

## Method

1. Rinse the rice in three changes of cold water, rubbing the grains between your fingers, until the water runs cloudy rather than milky. Drain it well, stir the oil through it, and leave it for 15 minutes. The oil coats the grains and helps them burst rather than stay whole, which is the difference between congee and rice soup.
2. Slice the pork 3 mm thick across the grain, then cut the slices into 5 cm strips. It cuts far more easily half frozen. Toss with the soy sauce and the cornflour until every strip is slick and no dry cornflour is left, and leave it in the fridge.
3. Peel the two preserved eggs — they are 60 g each — rinse them, and cut each into 6 wedges. Keep half the wedges back on a saucer for the top.
4. Bring the water to a rolling boil in a heavy pot that holds at least 3 litres. Add the rice and the ginger, bring it back to the boil, and stir once along the bottom to lift the grains off the base.
5. Turn the heat down to the lowest setting that keeps the surface moving, and cook uncovered — or with the lid a finger's width ajar — for 60 minutes. Stir every 10 minutes, scraping the base with a flat wooden spoon, which is the only thing standing between you and a scorched pot.
6. It is ready when no separate grains are left, the surface is one opaque cream with no watery ring at the edge, and a spoon drawn through leaves a track that closes slowly behind it. If it is still thin, boil it harder uncovered for 5 minutes; if it has gone stiff, stir in boiling water 100 ml at a time.
7. Stir in half the preserved egg wedges and cook for 5 more minutes. They will soften and cloud the congee grey-green, and that is the flavour of the dish rather than a fault.
8. Raise the heat to medium. Drop the pork in a strip at a time so it cannot clump, stir once, and poach for 2 minutes, until every piece has turned from pink to white the whole way through.
9. Stir in the salt and the white pepper, then taste it and adjust — congee absorbs salt and always needs more than seems right.
10. Ladle into bowls. Top with the reserved egg wedges, the spring onion and the sesame oil, and grind more white pepper over. Eat it hot enough to need blowing on.

## Notes

Twelve parts water to one of rice, which is 150 g to 1800 ml. That single number
decides the dish, and published versions run anywhere from 1:8 to 1:13 — 1:8 is
a thick spoonable porridge, 1:13 is the thin drinkable Cantonese jook served in
a bowl you can lift. Twelve is nearer the second. Whichever you pick, the congee
thickens as it cools and again overnight, so err thinner than you want it.

The ingredient list says 120 g of preserved eggs rather than two of them
because half of them go into the pot and half go on top, and the diagram shows
each half with its own weight. Two is what you buy; 60 g is what one weighs
shelled.

The hour is not negotiable and cannot be hurried by boiling harder. What takes
the time is the starch leaving the grain, and a hard boil just scorches the base
while the middle stays granular. Freezing the rinsed rice for an hour first
splits the grains and cuts perhaps 15 minutes off, which is a real trick and
still not a shortcut worth planning around.

Half the preserved egg goes in and half goes on top on purpose. What is stirred
in dissolves and gives the whole pot its faintly sulphurous, savoury depth;
what goes on top stays in recognisable wedges with the dark yolk soft in the
middle, and is what people are actually eating it for. Stir all of it in and you
lose the second thing entirely.

Preserved eggs are not an acquired taste so much as an acquired smell. If the
ammonia is strong when you peel them, they are old rather than bad; rinse them
and use them stirred in rather than on top.

The pork goes in for 2 minutes at the end and no longer. It is lean, it is
sliced thin, and it is sitting in something at 95 °C — anything more and it goes
from silky to chalky. The cornflour is what keeps it silky, so do not skip it.

White pepper twice, once in the pot and once over the bowl, is how this tastes
in a Hong Kong congee shop. Black pepper is not the same thing and does not
belong here.

The panel reads about 400 mg of sodium a serving, most of it the salt and the
preserved eggs. That is low for how savoury it tastes, and it is why congee is
what gets cooked for someone who is ill.

What goes on top, one or two at a time rather than all at once: fried dough
sticks torn into pieces, roasted peanuts, coriander, a raw egg cracked into the
hot bowl, or chopped Crispy Roast Pork Belly, which is the best possible use of
a leftover piece whose crackling has gone soft.

## Storage

Four days in the fridge, and it is better on the second day than the first. It
sets to a solid block when cold, which is normal — do not throw it away thinking
it has spoiled.

Reheat in a pan over low heat with 100 ml of boiling water per bowl, stirring
until it flows again. A microwave works if you stir it twice on the way; without
stirring it erupts.

Freezes for two months in single portions and comes back needing rather more
water than that. Freeze it without the toppings.
