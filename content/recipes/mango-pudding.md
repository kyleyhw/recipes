## Ingredients

- 10 g gelatine (5 leaves)
- 60 g granulated sugar
- 100 ml water
- 500 g mango (2 large), flesh only
- 150 ml evaporated milk

## Method

1. Put the gelatine leaves in a bowl of cold water and leave them for 5 minutes, until they are limp and floppy and have doubled in thickness.
2. Put the sugar and the water in a small pan over medium heat and stir until the sugar has completely dissolved and the liquid is clear. Take it off the heat.
3. Lift the gelatine out and squeeze it hard in your fist to get rid of the water it has taken up, then stir it into the hot syrup for 30 seconds. Hold the spoon up: there must be no threads or specks left on it. Undissolved gelatine never dissolves later, and it sets as rubbery strings through the pudding.
4. Peel the mangoes and cut the flesh off the stones. Blend it to a completely smooth purée — 60 seconds, and rub a drop between finger and thumb to check there is no fibre in it.
5. Pour the warm syrup into the purée in a thin stream, stirring, then stir in the evaporated milk. It will turn the pale opaque orange of the finished pudding.
6. Pour into four glasses or moulds, cover, and refrigerate for at least 4 hours and up to overnight.
7. It is set when a finger pressed lightly on the surface leaves a dent that stays, and tilting the glass moves the whole thing as one piece. Eat it cold, out of the glass.

## Notes

Everything here depends on the mango, and there is nothing else to hide behind.
It must be properly ripe: wrinkled at the shoulders, giving under a thumb near
the stem, and smelling sweet there. A firm supermarket mango is sour and stringy
and no amount of sugar corrects it. Frozen mango chunks blend straight from
frozen and are reliably better than a bad fresh one, which makes this a
year-round dessert rather than a July one.

Evaporated milk rather than cream. It gives the specific pale opaque colour a
dim sum mango pudding has, it does not split against the fruit acid, and it
keeps the thing light enough to eat after a meal. Cream makes a mango panna
cotta, which is a fine dessert and a different one.

Ten grams of gelatine to about 750 ml of liquid is a deliberately soft set — it
should wobble and give way at once under a spoon. Take it to 14 g if you want to
turn it out of a mould and have it stand up, and dip the mould in hot water for
3 seconds to release it.

Mango is safe with gelatine. Fresh pineapple, kiwi, papaya and figs are not:
they carry proteases that cut gelatine apart and leave you with a bowl of juice
in the morning. Mango has a little of the same enzyme and nowhere near enough to
matter, which is why nobody warns you about it and why this works.

For a firmer, sliceable, vegetarian version use 5 g of agar in place of the
gelatine. Agar has to be boiled for 2 minutes rather than merely dissolved, and
it sets firm at room temperature, so it is a different texture: clean and
brittle rather than wobbling.

Diced mango folded in before chilling, or spooned over at the table, is the
usual restaurant flourish. So is a spoonful of evaporated milk poured over the
top of each glass just before serving, which is worth doing.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge, covered — the surface takes on a skin and dries
otherwise, and it will pick up the smell of anything strong nearby.

It does not freeze. Gelatine breaks on thawing and what comes back is mango
juice with a curd in it.

Make it the day before. It needs 4 hours to set anyway, and it is better after
twelve.
