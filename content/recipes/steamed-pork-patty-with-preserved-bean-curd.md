## Ingredients

- 30 g preserved bean curd (2 cubes and brine)
- 1 tbsp shaoxing wine
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 0.5 tsp light soy sauce
- 0.25 tsp white pepper
- 0.5 tsp sesame oil
- 250 g fatty pork mince
- 15 g ginger, finely minced
- 15 g spring onions, green parts only

## Method

1. Put the bean curd and its brine in a mixing bowl with the shaoxing wine, sugar, soy sauce, white pepper and sesame oil. Mash with the back of a spoon for 60 seconds, until no lumps of bean curd are left and the mixture is a smooth grey-pink liquid.
2. Add the pork and the ginger. Stir hard with chopsticks or a spoon in one direction only — never back and forth — for 3 minutes. The mixture starts loose and wet and ends sticky enough to cling to the spoon in a mass when you lift it out. That change is the recipe: stirring one way lines up the proteins so they hold the fat and liquid, and a patty made without it steams into something crumbly and dry.
3. Put a steaming rack in a wok or a wide pan, add water to just below it, and bring it to a rolling boil.
4. Spread the meat into a shallow heatproof dish in an even layer 1.5 cm thick, going right to the edges. Do not press it down — the patty should look loosely packed, and the gaps are where the steam gets in.
5. Set the dish on the rack, cover with a tight lid, and steam over high heat for 13 minutes. It is done when the patty has shrunk from the sides of the dish, a pool of clear broth has gathered round it, and a knife pushed into the middle comes out with no pink on it.
6. Lift the dish out, scatter the finely chopped spring onion over the top, and take it to the table in the dish it steamed in. Serve with plain rice, and spoon the broth over the rice rather than pouring it away — it is the best thing on the plate.

## Notes

Two cubes rather than one and a half. The bean curd is the whole flavour of the
dish and half a cube is the difference between tasting it and wondering what it
is.

No salt goes in anywhere. Two cubes of fu yu carry about 900 mg of sodium on
their own, which is what the teaspoon of sugar is there to balance — leave the
sugar out and the patty tastes flatly salty rather than savoury.

Fatty mince, 10% or more. Lean pork steams into a firm grey disc; the fat is
what makes the texture bouncy, and it renders into the broth that ends up on
the rice.

The white bean curd is what this uses. The red kind, nan ru, cured with red
yeast rice, is sweeter and stronger and makes a darker, more pungent patty —
good, but a different dish, and it would want a cube less.

The panel counts the teaspoon of brine as more bean curd, because that is what
it is: the same ferment with more water in it.

Things that traditionally go into the mixture, one at a time rather than all at
once: a chopped water chestnut for crunch, a spoonful of soaked dried shrimp, or
a salted egg yolk pressed into the middle before it goes in to steam.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge, in the dish, covered, with its broth. Steam it again for
5 minutes to reheat, or microwave it covered for 90 seconds — this is one of the
few things that genuinely does not suffer for it, because it was steamed in the
first place.

The raw mixture freezes better than the cooked patty. Spread it in the dish,
cover, and freeze for up to a month; defrost overnight in the fridge and steam
it for 15 minutes rather than 13. A cooked patty frozen and thawed weeps and
goes grainy.
