## Ingredients

- 30 g preserved bean curd (2 cubes and brine)
- 3 tbsp water
- 1 tsp cornflour
- 1 tbsp shaoxing wine
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 0.5 tsp light soy sauce
- 0.25 tsp white pepper
- 0.5 tsp sesame oil
- 250 g fatty pork mince
- 15 g ginger, finely minced
- 15 g spring onions, green parts only

## Method

1. Put the bean curd and its brine in a mixing bowl with the water, cornflour, shaoxing wine, sugar, soy sauce, white pepper and sesame oil. Mash with the back of a spoon for 60 seconds, until no lumps of bean curd are left, no grains of cornflour rasp on the bottom of the bowl, and the mixture is a smooth grey-pink liquid.
2. Add the pork and the ginger. Stir hard with chopsticks or a spoon in one direction only — never back and forth — for 60 to 90 seconds. Stop the moment it clings to the spoon in one mass when you lift it out. That moment is the whole instruction: stirring one way lines up the proteins so they hold the fat and the water, and stopping too early gives a patty that steams crumbly. Going well past it is the other failure and the commoner one — three or four minutes of it builds the same springy gel a sausage is made of, and the patty comes out rubbery.
3. Put a steaming rack in a wok or a wide pan, add water to just below it, and bring it to a rolling boil.
4. Spread the meat into a shallow heatproof dish in an even layer 1.5 cm thick, going right to the edges. Do not press it down — the patty should look loosely packed, and the gaps are where the steam gets in.
5. Set the dish on the rack, cover with a tight lid, and steam over high heat for 12 minutes. It is done when the patty has shrunk from the sides of the dish, a pool of clear broth has gathered round it, and a knife pushed into the middle comes out with no pink on it.
6. Lift the dish out, scatter the finely chopped spring onion over the top, and take it to the table in the dish it steamed in. Serve with plain rice, and spoon the broth over the rice rather than pouring it away — it is the best thing on the plate.

## Notes

Two cubes rather than one and a half. The bean curd is the whole flavour of the
dish and half a cube is the difference between tasting it and wondering what it
is.

No salt goes in anywhere. Two cubes of fu yu carry about 900 mg of sodium on
their own, which is what the teaspoon of sugar is there to balance — leave the
sugar out and the patty tastes flatly salty rather than savoury.

The three tablespoons of water are not optional and they are the difference
between tender and rubbery. Stirring in one direction builds a protein gel; the
water is what that gel holds. Build it with nothing to hold and you have made a
small pale sausage. The cornflour is there to keep the water in during the
steaming rather than letting it weep into the dish.

Mince at 20% fat, and this is worth asking the butcher for. The library's row is
10%, so the panel below is computed at that and reads about 100 kcal a serving
low — the figure is a floor rather than an error. Fat interrupts the protein
network as well as basting the meat, so a lean mince is tough for two reasons
rather than one.

The white bean curd is what this uses. The red kind, nan ru, cured with red
yeast rice, is sweeter and stronger and makes a darker, more pungent patty —
good, but a different dish, and it would want a cube less.

The panel counts the teaspoon of brine as more bean curd, because that is what
it is: the same ferment with more water in it.

Things that traditionally go into the mixture, one at a time rather than all at
once: a chopped water chestnut for crunch, or a spoonful of soaked dried shrimp.

Optional: a spoonful of soy dressing over the rice, which is what a shop puts on
a boxed one — Lee Kum Kee's seasoned soy sauce for seafood out of the bottle, or
Roast Meat Soy Dressing. Made in a pan with this patty's own broth in place of
the water, it beats either.

The other patty in this collection, Steamed Pork Patty with Salted Egg, is the
same technique flavoured a different way. It is still the wetter of the two —
about 130 g of liquid into the same 250 g of pork against 90 g here, because
the egg whites bring their own.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge, in the dish, covered, with its broth. Steam it again for
5 minutes to reheat, or microwave it covered for 90 seconds — this is one of the
few things that genuinely does not suffer for it, because it was steamed in the
first place.

The raw mixture freezes better than the cooked patty. Spread it in the dish,
cover, and freeze for up to a month; defrost overnight in the fridge and steam
it for 14 minutes rather than 12. A cooked patty frozen and thawed weeps and
goes grainy.
