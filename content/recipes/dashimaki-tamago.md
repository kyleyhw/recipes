## Ingredients

- 4 eggs
- 60 ml dashi
- 1 tsp light soy sauce
- 1 tsp mirin
- 0.25 tsp salt
- 1 tbsp neutral oil

## Method

1. Beat the eggs with chopsticks, cutting through them rather than whipping, until the yolks and whites are one even yellow with no air in them. Beat in the dashi, soy sauce, mirin and salt.
2. Pour the mixture through a sieve into a jug. This takes out the chalazae — the white cords at each yolk — which never break down and show as pale threads in the finished roll.
3. Heat a tamagoyaki pan or a small frying pan over medium-low heat. Fold a piece of kitchen paper into a pad, dip it in the oil, and wipe the pan. Keep the pad; you will use it between every layer.
4. Test the heat with a drop of egg: it should sizzle faintly and set in about 3 seconds. Sizzling loudly is too hot, and this omelette is never allowed to colour.
5. Pour in a fifth of the mixture and tilt to cover the base. When the surface is set but still glossy and wet-looking, roll it away from you into a loose cylinder at the far side of the pan, using chopsticks or a spatula.
6. Wipe the empty part of the pan with the oil pad, pour in another fifth, and lift the roll so the new egg runs underneath it. That is the step people miss and it is what joins the layers into one thing rather than a stack.
7. When the new layer is set but still wet on top, roll the cylinder back over it. Repeat with the remaining three additions, oiling the pan each time.
8. Tip the roll onto a bamboo mat or a sheet of baking paper, wrap it, and press it gently into a neat rectangle. Rest 5 minutes.
9. Cut across into eight thick slices and serve, hot or at room temperature.

## Notes

Sixty millilitres of dashi to four eggs is a fifth of the mixture by weight, and
it is what separates this from any other rolled omelette. The liquid keeps the
set soft and slightly wobbly — dashimaki is closer to a savoury custard than to
an omelette, and a properly made one weeps very slightly when it is cut. It also
makes the mixture harder to handle, which is the trade: less dashi rolls more
easily and tastes of less.

Never let it colour. A brown patch is a fault, not a Maillard reaction you are
allowed to enjoy. Medium-low heat and the oil pad rather than a pool of oil are
what keep it pale.

Roll while the top is still wet. If you wait until a layer looks cooked, the two
surfaces will not fuse and the roll comes apart into leaves when it is cut.

The rectangular pan is genuinely better and genuinely not necessary. A 20 cm
round pan gives a fatter, shorter roll with rounded ends, which tastes the same
and cannot be cut into neat rectangles for a bento. Buy one if you find you make
this often; do not buy one to try it.

Grated daikon with a little soy poured over is the standard accompaniment and it
cuts the richness well. It is not in the ingredient list because it is a thing
served beside rather than in the dish.

Eggs and dashi at 60 ml means you have 440 ml of dashi left from a packet. Make
Ohitashi with 200 of it while the pan is still out.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge, wrapped, and it is meant to be eaten cold as well as
hot — this is bento food. Cold from the fridge it firms noticeably; twenty
minutes at room temperature brings it most of the way back.

Do not microwave it. The dashi in it turns to steam, the roll puffs and then
collapses, and what you get is rubbery in the way the whole method exists to
avoid.

It does not freeze.
