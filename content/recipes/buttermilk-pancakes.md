## Ingredients

- 200 g all-purpose flour
- 2 tbsp caster sugar
- 1.5 tsp baking powder
- 0.5 tsp bicarbonate of soda
- 0.5 tsp salt
- 2 eggs
- 300 ml buttermilk
- 50 g unsalted butter, melted and cooled
- 1 tbsp neutral oil
- 4 tbsp maple syrup

## Method

1. Whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, bicarbonate of soda and salt together in a large bowl for 15 seconds, so the two raising agents are through the flour before either meets anything wet. Bicarbonate that arrives in a lump leaves a soapy grey patch in the finished pancake.
2. Whisk the eggs and buttermilk in a second bowl until the colour is even, then whisk in the melted butter. It must be cooled to blood heat or it will scramble the eggs in threads.
3. Pour the wet into the dry and fold with a spatula — no more than 12 turns, stopping while lumps and a few dry streaks are still visible. This is the same instruction as the Kimchi Pancake and for the same reason: stirred smooth, the flour develops gluten and the pancakes come out tough and flat.
4. Leave it 10 minutes and no longer. The flour hydrates and the bicarbonate starts working against the buttermilk's acid, and you want to catch that reaction rather than wait it out — after about twenty minutes the bubbles have been and gone and the rise goes with them. The surface should be domed and pocked when you come back to it. Do not stir it again.
5. Heat a 24 cm frying pan over medium-low for 2 minutes and wipe it with the oil on kitchen paper. Medium-low is deliberate: these are 1 cm thick and want three and a half minutes to set through, and a hot pan gives you a dark crust over raw batter.
6. Drop in 60 ml of batter per pancake — two or three to a pan — and leave them completely alone.
7. Cook 2 minutes 30. They are ready to turn when bubbles have risen and burst across the whole surface leaving open holes, the edges have gone matt and set, and the underside is an even mid-gold.
8. Flip once, and do not press them down. Pressing is the commonest way to ruin these: it forces out the gas the last ten minutes were spent making. Cook 1 minute 30 more.
9. Move them to a rack in a low oven while you cook the rest — a plate steams the underside soft, and a stack goes soggy from the bottom up.
10. Serve in stacks with the maple syrup and a knob of butter on top.

## Notes

Buttermilk is not a garnish on this recipe, it is the mechanism. Its acid reacts
with the bicarbonate of soda to make carbon dioxide, and that reaction is what
raises the pancake — plain milk with the same bicarbonate produces nothing but a
soapy taste. If the shop has none, stir 1 tbsp of lemon juice into 300 ml of
milk and leave it 10 minutes until it curdles; that is a real substitute and not
a compromise.

Both raising agents, and they do different jobs. The bicarbonate reacts at once
with the buttermilk and is spent by the time the pan is hot; the baking powder
has a second, slower action that fires with the heat. One without the other
gives a pancake that is either flat by the time it is cooked or never rises much
at all.

Lumps stay. Twelve turns and stop. Everything about a thick pancake's texture is
decided in that half-minute, and it is the instruction people find hardest to
follow because a lumpy batter looks unfinished.

Ten minutes of rest and not thirty. This is the opposite of the Lemon and Sugar
Pancakes, where a long rest makes them better: there the point is hydration,
here the point is to bake a chemical reaction that is already running.

Medium-low, and be patient. A 1 cm pancake needs about four minutes in total,
and a pan hot enough to colour one in ninety seconds gives you a brown outside
and a wet middle every time.

Real maple syrup, and read the bottle — pancake syrup and maple-flavoured syrup
are corn syrup with flavouring in them and no maple at all. The darker grades
are the better ones here.

Blueberries at 150 g are dropped onto each pancake after it goes in the pan
rather than stirred into the batter, which turns the whole bowl grey and makes
the fruit sink.

## Storage

They keep two days in the fridge and freeze for two months, in both cases
stacked with baking paper between them and bagged. This is the pancake that
survives keeping, which the thin ones do not.

Reheat in a toaster, which sounds wrong and is the best method there is: 90
seconds on a low setting from frozen, and the outside crisps slightly while the
middle warms through. Failing that, 5 minutes at 160 °C on a rack. A microwave
makes them dense and damp.

The batter does not keep. The bicarbonate has finished reacting within half an
hour and a batter cooked the next morning is flat, whatever it looked like the
night before. Measure the dry ingredients into a jar the night before instead —
that is the part that takes the time.
