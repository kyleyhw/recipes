## Ingredients

- 800 g beef brisket, in one piece
- 2 tbsp neutral oil
- 150 g onion, sliced 5 mm
- 20 g ginger, minced
- 4 cloves garlic, minced
- 3 tbsp curry powder
- 1 tbsp shaoxing wine
- 600 ml chicken stock
- 2 star anise
- 2 bay leaves
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 15 g rock sugar
- 1 tsp salt
- 400 g daikon, peeled and in 3 cm chunks
- 300 g potato, peeled and in 3 cm chunks
- 150 g carrots, in 2 cm chunks
- 150 ml coconut milk
- 50 ml evaporated milk

## Method

1. Cut the brisket into 4 cm pieces across the grain. Cover with cold water in a 24 cm saucepan, bring to a boil, and boil 5 minutes until a grey scum has risen and set. Tip into a colander, rinse each piece under the cold tap, and wash the pan.
2. Heat the oil in the clean pan over medium heat. Fry the onion for 5 minutes, until it has gone limp and translucent with brown edges, then the ginger and garlic for 1 minute, until you can smell them over the onion.
3. Add the curry powder and fry for 90 seconds, stirring constantly. It goes from dusty to glossy as it takes up the oil and the smell turns from raw to toasted. This is the step the dish stands on: curry powder stirred into liquid instead tastes chalky and raw no matter how long it then simmers, and no amount of extra powder later fixes it.
4. Add the shaoxing wine and let it hiss for 20 seconds, scraping the base of the pan.
5. Return the brisket and turn it through the paste for 1 minute, until every piece is yellow to its edges. Add the stock, star anise, bay leaves, light soy sauce, rock sugar and salt. The liquid should just cover the meat.
6. Bring to a boil, then drop to a bare simmer, lid ajar, and cook for 90 minutes. Stir every 30 minutes and scrape the base, which is where a curry catches.
7. Add the daikon, potato and carrots. Simmer 40 minutes more, uncovered now, until a skewer goes through the daikon without resistance and the sauce has reduced to the point where it coats a spoon. The potato will have given up some of its starch, which is what thickens this — there is no cornflour in it.
8. Turn the heat to its lowest. Stir in the coconut milk and the evaporated milk and cook 5 minutes without letting it boil. Boiled coconut milk splits into oil and grain, and it cannot be brought back.
9. Taste for salt and sugar. Serve with plain rice, or over rice, or with a crusty roll to push through it.

## Notes

This is the cha chaan teng and dai pai dong curry, and it is a different dish
from Beef Brisket and Daikon Casserole despite sharing its two main
ingredients: that one is a clear soup eaten with a spoon, this one is thick,
yellow and forked over rice. Both are worth having and neither substitutes for
the other.

Bloom the powder. Ninety seconds in hot oil before any liquid goes in is the
difference between a curry and a pan of beef in yellow water — the flavour
compounds in turmeric, cumin and coriander are fat-soluble and simply do not
come out into stock.

An ordinary Madras curry powder from a supermarket is the right thing. Hong
Kong curry is not Indian curry and is not trying to be: it is milder, sweeter
and rounder, and it arrived through the same colonial kitchens as the milk tea.
If you can get a jar of Hong Kong curry paste (咖喱醬) from a Chinese
supermarket, use 4 tbsp of it in place of the powder and skip the blooming,
since it has already been fried.

Coconut milk and evaporated milk, and both are doing different jobs. The
coconut is the body and the sweetness; the evaporated milk is the cha chaan
teng finish, the same tinned milk that makes the tea, and it gives the sauce a
particular opaque paleness that coconut alone does not. One or the other alone
works — 200 ml of either — and the version with both is what a tea restaurant
sends out.

Both go in at the end and neither boils. This is the other rule that decides
whether the sauce is smooth or grainy, and it is why they are not in the pot
for the ninety minutes.

No cornflour. The potato is the thickener, which is why it goes in with forty
minutes left rather than at the end — it needs long enough to start breaking
down at the edges. A curry thickened with a slurry instead has a glassy sheen
that this should not have.

Beef tendon (牛筋) simmered alongside from the start is the addition worth
knowing, at 200 g. It takes the same time as the brisket and turns to gelatine,
and a Hong Kong shop selling this will usually offer 咖喱牛腩筋 as its own thing.

## Storage

Four days in the fridge and it is better on the second day than the first: the
daikon and the potato go on drinking sauce overnight, and the curry powder's
edges round off. This is a dish to make the day before.

Reheat over low heat for 10 minutes with a splash of water, stirring — it
thickens considerably in the fridge as the potato starch sets. Do not let it
boil, for the same reason it did not boil at step 8.

It freezes for three months without the vegetables and badly with them: the
daikon comes back spongy and the potato collapses to mush. Freeze the beef and
its sauce, and cook fresh daikon and potato into it for 40 minutes when you
thaw it, which is the same dish rather than a compromise.
