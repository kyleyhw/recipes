## Ingredients

- 50 g lap cheung
- 3 tbsp water
- 1 tsp cornflour
- 1 tbsp shaoxing wine
- 1 tsp light soy sauce
- 0.25 tsp white pepper
- 0.5 tsp sesame oil
- 250 g fatty pork mince
- 15 g ginger, finely minced
- 15 g spring onions, green parts only

## Method

1. Slice the lap cheung on a steep diagonal, 3 mm thick, which gives you long ovals rather than coins — more surface to render from, and they lie flat.
2. Put the water, cornflour, shaoxing wine, soy sauce, white pepper and sesame oil in a mixing bowl and whisk for 20 seconds, until no cornflour is left on the bottom. No sugar goes in: the sausage brings it.
3. Add the pork and the ginger. Stir hard with chopsticks in one direction only for 60 to 90 seconds, and stop the moment it clings to the chopsticks in one mass.
4. Put a steaming rack in a wok or a wide pan, add water to just below it, and bring it to a rolling boil.
5. Spread the meat into a shallow heatproof dish in an even layer 1.5 cm thick. Lay the sausage slices over the surface in overlapping rows, covering as much of it as they will, and press them in until they sit level. They go on top and never through the mixture — the fat has to run downwards to do anything, and slices buried in the meat just sit there.
6. Set the dish on the rack, cover with a tight lid, and steam over high heat for 12 minutes. It is done when the slices have gone translucent at the edges and curled slightly, the broth around the patty has turned pink-red from their fat, and a knife pushed into the middle comes out with no pink on it.
7. Scatter the spring onion over the top and take it to the table in the dish it steamed in, with plain rice. Spoon the coloured broth over the rice.

## Notes

The easiest of these patties by a distance. Nothing is soaked, rinsed, squeezed
or de-boned — you slice a sausage.

No sugar in the mixture. Lap cheung is cured with a great deal of it, about 13 g
per 100, and it renders out into the patty as it steams. Adding more makes the
dish cloying, which is the one way this version goes wrong.

Buy the pork kind rather than the liver kind for this. Yun cheong, the darker
liver sausage, is excellent and much stronger, and it overwhelms a patty that
has nothing else in it.

The technique is the same in every steamed patty here and is written out at
length in Steamed Pork Patty with Preserved Bean Curd: stir until it clings and
no further, 1.5 cm thick, 12 minutes over a rolling boil.

If the slices are still firm and pale at 12 minutes they were cut too thick.
Three millimetres on the diagonal renders; a 1 cm coin steams and stays waxy.

Optional, over the rice: Lee Kum Kee's seasoned soy sauce for seafood, or Roast
Meat Soy Dressing if there is no bottle. This is the version that least needs
it — the broth is already sweet and red.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge, in the dish, covered, with its broth. The cured
sausage keeps the whole thing a day longer than the other patties manage.

Steam it again for 5 minutes, or microwave it covered for 90 seconds. Cold, the
sliced patty in a lunchbox is genuinely good, which is not true of the salted
fish one.

The raw mixture freezes for a month. Freeze it with the sausage already laid on
top; unlike salted fish, lap cheung is cured dry and takes no harm from it.
