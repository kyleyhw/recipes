## Ingredients

- 300 g spinach
- 20 g toasted sesame seeds
- 1.5 tbsp light soy sauce
- 2 tsp granulated sugar
- 1 tbsp dashi

## Method

1. Bring a large pan of water to a rolling boil. Do not salt it; the dressing carries all the salt this needs.
2. Hold the spinach by the stem ends and lower the stems into the water for 15 seconds before letting the leaves follow. Blanch for 45 seconds more, until the leaves have gone dark and slack but the stems still have some spring in them.
3. Lift it straight into a bowl of cold water. The point is to stop the cooking within a few seconds — spinach left to cool in its own heat goes olive and slimy, and no amount of squeezing recovers it.
4. Gather the cooled spinach into a ball in both hands and squeeze. Hard. Turn it and squeeze again, and a third time, until no more water comes out and what you are holding is a dense green log about the size of your fist. Three hundred grams becomes roughly 120 g at this point.
5. Cut the log across into 4 cm lengths and loosen the pieces apart with your fingers.
6. Grind 15 g of the sesame seeds in a mortar or a suribachi until about half of them are broken and the rest are whole — 30 seconds of work. They will smell strongly toasted and start to look slightly oily. Stir in the soy sauce, sugar and dashi until the sugar has gone.
7. Fold the spinach through the dressing until every strand is coated and grey-flecked. Pile into a bowl, scatter the remaining 5 g of whole sesame seeds over, and serve at room temperature.

## Notes

The squeeze in step 4 is the whole recipe. Blanched spinach holds an astonishing
amount of water — more than half its cooked weight — and any that stays behind
comes out later, in the bowl, and turns the sesame dressing into a thin grey
puddle. Squeeze until you are slightly embarrassed by how hard you are squeezing,
and then once more.

Grinding the sesame matters nearly as much. Whole seeds pass through mostly
intact and taste of very little; broken ones release the oil that makes the
dressing coat rather than sit on top. Leaving a third of them whole is the
compromise, so there is still something to bite. A pestle and mortar does it in
half a minute; a spice grinder overshoots into sesame butter in about four
seconds, so use short pulses if that is what you have.

The dashi is one tablespoon and it is optional in the sense that the dish works
without it. It is not optional in the sense that with it the dressing tastes
finished and without it it tastes like soy and sugar. Water is the honest
substitute, and the salad is 90% as good.

Green beans, tenderstem broccoli and blanched carrot batons all take the same
dressing. Beans want three minutes rather than one and no squeezing at all.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge in a closed box. It is one of the few salads here that is
genuinely better made ahead: the dressing settles into the spinach and the raw
edge of the soy softens. Take it out twenty minutes before eating, since fridge-cold
mutes the sesame entirely.

It does not freeze — or rather the spinach would survive and the dressing would
separate into a gritty mess on thawing.

Blanched, squeezed, undressed spinach keeps three days and freezes for three
months, which is a useful thing to know independently of this recipe.
