## Ingredients

- 130 g orange, sliced 5 mm thick
- 85 g lemon, sliced 5 mm thick
- 150 g apple, cored and cut into 1 cm dice
- 50 g granulated sugar
- 60 ml brandy
- 750 ml red wine
- 1 cinnamon stick
- 500 g water, frozen into cubes
- 300 ml soda water

## Method

1. Slice the orange and the lemon into 5 mm rounds with the peel on, and cut each round in half. Core the apple and cut it into 1 cm dice, skin on. The peel is where the oil is and it is doing most of the flavouring, so none of it gets removed.
2. Put the fruit, the sugar and the brandy in a 2 litre jug and press them together with the back of a wooden spoon for 1 minute, until the sugar is wet through, the citrus has gone limp at the edges and there is a shallow layer of juice in the bottom of the jug. Sugar will not dissolve in cold wine; it dissolves in the brandy, and this is the step that decides whether it does.
3. Pour in the wine, drop in the cinnamon stick and stir for 20 seconds.
4. Cover the jug and refrigerate for 4 hours. The colour moves from purple-red to a browner red, the apple dice sink, and a spoonful tasted should read as orange first and wine second. Under 2 hours it is wine with fruit in it; the change is not gradual and you can taste when it has happened.
5. Lift the cinnamon stick out and throw it away. Left in past this point it takes over and the jug tastes medicinal.
6. Fill six tall glasses to the brim with ice. Pour the sangria to two thirds up each glass, top with 50 ml of soda water, and spoon some of the fruit into each. Serve at once, while it is still fizzing.

## Notes

The wine is a young, dry, fruity red — a Garnacha or a Tempranillo, a Rioja
joven, anything sold cheaply and drunk within a year of the harvest. What does
not work is an oaked wine, whose vanilla and smoke fight the citrus, and what is
wasted is a good bottle, since four hours with an orange in it removes everything
you paid extra for. It is equally not a way to rescue a bad bottle: sangria
tastes of the wine in it, and a thin sour wine makes thin sour sangria.

The brandy is not a garnish. It carries the sugar into solution, it lifts the
whole drink off the flat sweetness it otherwise settles into, and it is what
makes this sangría rather than fruit steeped in wine. Brandy de Jerez is the
Spanish one and is worth buying for it. Any grape brandy works.

Four hours, and not overnight. The peel keeps giving up oil for about that long
and then starts giving up pith instead, and a jug left in the fridge for a day
is noticeably bitter at the back of the throat with apple that has gone to mush.
If you want it ready a day ahead, macerate for the 4 hours, then lift all the
fruit out and keep the liquid on its own.

The soda goes in the glass and never in the jug, where it is flat within the
hour. Fifty millilitres a glass is a top-up rather than a dilution — enough to
lift it, not enough to make it taste watered. Leave it out entirely for the
undiluted version, which is what most of Spain would actually pour.

Two units a glass, and this is the thing about sangria that catches people out.
A bottle at 13% plus 60 ml of brandy is about 121 ml of alcohol in the jug, so a
glass of this carries roughly what a large glass of wine does — but it is cold,
it is sweet, it is full of fruit and it is topped with soda, and it tastes like
none of that. The ice does not help either: it dilutes the drink as it melts and
so makes the last mouthful weaker than the first, which is precisely backwards
from what your judgement is doing over the same period.

The ice is in the ingredient list as frozen water rather than left to the method
because it is a fifth of what ends up in the glass and it is meant to melt.

The panel is worth reading carefully here, because a glass comes to 188 kcal and
the protein, carbohydrate and fat on it only account for about 80 of those. The
missing hundred is the alcohol. Ethanol carries 7 kcal a gram — nearer fat than
sugar — and it is not a macronutrient, so it has no row to sit in and no share
of the bar that shows where the energy came from. Every figure on the panel is
correct; the bar is simply describing the 80 kcal it can see. This is the only
recipe in the collection where that gap is most of the total.

## Storage

Made and drunk the same day. A poured glass has perhaps ten minutes before the
soda is flat and the ice has taken the edge off it, which is what the fruit in
the glass is for — it gives you something to do while you get through it.

The jug keeps two days in the fridge, covered, but only if the fruit comes out
at the four hour mark. Left in, it is bitter by the next morning. Do not freeze
it: alcohol keeps it slushy rather than solid, and what thaws is the fruit,
which comes back as pulp.
