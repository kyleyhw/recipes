## Ingredients

- 300 g bean sprouts
- 0.5 tsp salt
- 9 g garlic, crushed to a paste
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced
- 1 tbsp sesame oil
- 1 tsp toasted sesame seeds
- 0.5 tsp gochugaru

## Method

1. Rinse the sprouts in a bowl of cold water and lift them out, leaving behind the loose husks that float free. Pick off any that are brown or slimy at the root.
2. Bring 500 ml of water to the boil in a lidded pan with a quarter teaspoon of the salt. Add the sprouts, put the lid on, and boil for 4 minutes.
3. **Leave the lid alone.** Either it stays on for the whole 4 minutes or it stays off for the whole 4 minutes — lifting it partway through is what makes bean sprouts smell aggressively beany, and once they do, nothing takes it out. Lid on is the easier discipline.
4. Drain into a colander and rinse under cold running water for 20 seconds, until the sprouts are cool to the touch. Shake hard and leave to drain for a minute. Do not squeeze them; they will bruise and go floppy.
5. Toss with the remaining salt, the garlic, spring onions, sesame oil and sesame seeds. Use your hands — a Korean cook does, and it is genuinely better, because tongs snap the sprouts and hands do not.
6. Add the gochugaru, toss once more, and serve at room temperature or cold.

## Notes

The lid rule sounds like superstition and is not. Bean sprouts give off a
volatile sulphurous compound as they cook; with the lid on it condenses and stays
in the pan water, with the lid off it leaves entirely, and lifting the lid partway
catches the worst of both — a pan full of it, released over the sprouts as they
finish. Four minutes with the lid on from the start is the reliable version.

Korean recipes mean soybean sprouts here, the big ones with the yellow bean still
attached, which are nuttier and stay firmer. Ordinary mung bean sprouts are what
most shops sell and are what this is written for; if you find soybean sprouts,
boil them for 6 minutes rather than 4, because the bean head takes longer than
the shoot.

There is no soy sauce in this, which surprises people. It is seasoned with salt
and sesame, and the result is pale, clean and nutty. The version with soy sauce
and more gochugaru exists and is a different banchan.

Cold, this is one of the components of bibimbap, and doubling the recipe to have
some for that is sensible.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge in a closed box, and it is meant to live there — banchan
is made in a batch and eaten over several meals. The garlic sharpens over the
first day, which is an improvement.

Take it out of the fridge fifteen minutes before serving; the sesame oil is muted
when it is cold.

It does not freeze.
