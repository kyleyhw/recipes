## Ingredients

- 160 g white bread (4 thick slices)
- 40 g peanut butter
- 2 eggs
- 60 ml evaporated milk
- 0.25 tsp salt
- 3 tbsp neutral oil
- 20 g unsalted butter
- 2 tbsp golden syrup

## Method

1. Cut the crusts off all four slices, square. This is not tidiness — a crust does not soak, so it stays dry and chewy inside a fried sandwich.
2. Spread half the peanut butter right to the edges of one slice and put a second slice on top. Do the same again. Press each sandwich flat with your palm for 5 seconds, so the two slices grip.
3. Beat the eggs, the evaporated milk and the salt in a wide shallow dish until the mixture is one even yellow with no ribbons of white left.
4. Heat the oil in a 24 cm frying pan over medium heat until a corner of bread dipped in sizzles steadily. It should be about 5 mm deep; add more if the pan is wide.
5. Lay a sandwich in the egg, count 10 seconds, turn it, count 10 more, then lift it and let it drip for 3 seconds. Both faces and all four cut sides should be wet and yellow. Do not leave it any longer — a sandwich that has soaked through falls apart in the pan.
6. Lower it into the oil away from you. Fry for 90 seconds, until the underside is an even deep gold and the edge has puffed slightly, then turn it with a spatula and a fork and fry for 90 seconds more. Press the four sides against the pan for 10 seconds each to set them.
7. Lift onto kitchen paper and blot both faces. Do the second sandwich.
8. Cut each in half on the diagonal, stack the halves on a plate, and put a 10 g pat of cold butter on top so it half melts. Pour the golden syrup over at the table. Eat it hot, while the outside is still crisp.

## Notes

The bread should be a day old and thick — 40 g a slice, which is thicker than a
supermarket standard slice. Fresh bread is too soft and collapses in the egg,
and thin bread gives you a fried sandwich with no soft middle, which is the
whole point of the thing. Stale bread soaks better than fresh: this is the best
use a forgotten half loaf has.

Evaporated milk in the egg rather than fresh milk, for the same reason as
everywhere else in this pair of recipes — more solids and less water, so the
soaked bread fries rather than steams, and the custard inside sets rather than
staying wet.

Shallow fried, not griddled. A cha chaan teng deep fries it, and 5 mm of oil in
a frying pan is the honest domestic compromise: it crisps all six faces and uses
a fifth of the oil. Dry-frying it in butter alone makes French toast, which is a
different and lesser thing here.

Peanut butter is the standard filling and Kaya, the coconut and egg jam, is the
other one. Condensed milk drizzled over instead of golden syrup is common and
sweeter still. Do not use all three at once, however much the menu photograph
suggests it.

There is no way to make this a light dish and no reason to try. The panel reads
771 kcal a portion and that is honest — it is an afternoon snack, ordered
with a milk tea, and it is meant to be the only thing you eat until dinner.

## Storage

Eat it immediately. Ten minutes on the plate and the crust has softened into the
custard beneath it; there is no version of this that survives being kept.

It does not refrigerate and does not freeze. If you have made too much, the
sandwiches can be assembled with the peanut butter, wrapped and kept a day in
the fridge before dipping, which is the only part of this worth doing ahead.
