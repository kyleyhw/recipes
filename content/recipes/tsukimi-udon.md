## Ingredients

- 400 ml dashi
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1 tbsp mirin
- 200 g udon noodles
- 1 egg
- 10 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Put the dashi, soy sauce and mirin in a small pan and bring to a bare simmer. Keep it there; it must be hot when it meets the egg.
2. Cook the udon in a separate pan of boiling water — 1 to 2 minutes for frozen or vacuum-packed, 8 to 10 for dried. Drain well.
3. Warm a deep bowl with hot water from the tap and tip it out. Put the noodles in.
4. Crack the egg into a small cup first, then slide it onto the noodles. Cracking it straight into the bowl risks shell, and a broken yolk is the end of the dish.
5. Pour the hot broth down the far side of the bowl, away from the egg, until the noodles are submerged and the white is covered but the yolk is still standing proud.
6. Leave it completely alone for 2 minutes. The white turns from clear to opaque and just sets; the yolk stays liquid under its film. Do not stir, and do not cover it, which would set the yolk too.
7. Scatter the spring onion round the edge and take it to the table. Break the yolk into the broth yourself, at the table, with the first mouthful.

## Notes

The broth does the cooking and it only just manages it. Four hundred millilitres
at a bare simmer carries enough heat to set an egg white in two minutes and not
enough to set the yolk, which is exactly the margin the dish lives in. Broth off
the boil leaves you with raw white in soup; broth boiling hard, or a lid on,
gives you a hard yolk and no moon.

Pour down the far side. Poured straight onto the egg it breaks the yolk and
clouds the broth with white, and both are unrecoverable.

Raw yolk, so the same rule as everywhere else: an egg you would eat raw, which
in the UK means a British Lion mark. Tamago Kake Gohan's notes have the full
version of that argument.

This is the plainest udon in the collection and deliberately so — dashi, soy,
mirin and nothing else, which is kake-jiru, the base every other udon broth is a
variation on. Tori Nanban Udon is this broth with seared chicken and leek in it.
Learn the proportions here and the rest follow.

Tsukimi means moon-viewing, and the yolk is the moon. It is a September dish in
Japan for that reason, though nothing about it minds being eaten in March.

Kamaboko, a torn sheet of nori, or a spoonful of tenkasu are the usual
additions. None of them is necessary and the last one is the best.

## Storage

None. The egg is the dish and it sets further every minute; five minutes in it
is a soft-boiled egg in soup, which is fine and is not this.

The broth alone keeps three days in the fridge and freezes for two months, so
the sensible thing is to make three times as much of it and have a bowl of this
in eight minutes whenever you want one.
