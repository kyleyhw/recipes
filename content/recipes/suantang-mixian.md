## Ingredients

- 1 tbsp neutral oil
- 150 g pork mince
- 8 g garlic, finely chopped
- 10 g ginger, finely chopped
- 4 tbsp sour soup base
- 300 g tomato (2), in wedges
- 800 ml chicken stock
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 0.5 tsp granulated sugar
- 400 g mixian (2 portions)
- 40 g chinese chives, in 4 cm lengths
- 20 g coriander, roughly chopped
- 1 tbsp chilli oil

## Method

1. Heat the oil in a saucepan over high heat. Add the pork, press it flat, and fry 3 minutes, breaking it up after the first minute, until it is dry and browned at the edges.
2. Add the garlic and ginger and fry 45 seconds, until they smell sweet.
3. Turn the heat to medium and add the sour soup base. Fry it for 2 minutes, stirring, until it darkens and the oil around it turns orange-red. This is the step that decides the bowl — a paste tipped straight into stock tastes raw and thin, and two minutes in hot oil is what turns it into a broth.
4. Add the tomato wedges and cook 5 minutes, pressing them against the side of the pan, until they have collapsed and their skins have curled away.
5. Pour in the stock, add the soy sauce and sugar, and simmer for 10 minutes. Taste it. It should be sour first, savoury second, and only then hot; add a little salt if the stock was unsalted.
6. Bring it to a hard boil, add the mixian, and boil for 60 seconds. Vacuum-packed mixian is already cooked and wants warming, not cooking.
7. Add the chives, count to ten, and divide between two bowls. Scatter the coriander over, spoon the chilli oil on, and eat at once.

## Notes

The sourness is fermented, not vinegar. Guizhou's 红酸汤 is tomatoes and chillies
left to ferment with glutinous rice flour, and the acid is lactic — round and
slightly sweet, the way sauerkraut is sour rather than the way a vinaigrette is.
Nothing in a British cupboard reproduces it, which is why the jar is the
ingredient rather than a convenience.

Fry the base. This is the same rule as a curry paste or a doubanjiang and it is
the commonest thing to skip. Two minutes in oil until the colour deepens and the
fat runs red; poured into stock unfried, the same four tablespoons give a thin,
raw, faintly tinny soup.

Fresh tomatoes on top of the fermented ones. It sounds redundant and is not: the
jar brings the sour and the fresh wedges bring sweetness and body, and a bowl
made without them tastes flat in a way more base does not fix.

Brands differ enormously, in heat most of all. Some red sour soup is barely
spicy and some is fierce. Four tablespoons is the amount for a middling jar of
the pourable kind — taste at step 5 and adjust, and expect to recalibrate the
first time you open a new brand.

If what you have is **Haidilao's Guizhou-style sour hot pot base**, which is
the one on most UK shelves, use half. It comes as a 240 g block meant for a
whole pot: oilier, far more concentrated, and four tablespoons of it would be
a different and much heavier bowl.

Fish is the other version and arguably the original: 酸汤鱼 is the Guizhou dish
this bowl borrows from, made with a whole freshwater fish rather than mince. A
white fish fillet cut in chunks goes in at step 6 for 4 minutes instead of the
pork, and it is very good.

## Storage

The broth keeps four days in the fridge and freezes for two months, and it is
better on the second day — the fermented and fresh tomato settle into each
other. Keep it separately and boil the noodles into it fresh; mixian left in
liquid overnight is paste.

The assembled bowl keeps for nothing.
