## Ingredients

- 500 g aubergine
- 5 tbsp peanut oil
- 25 g doubanjiang
- 30 g pickled chillies, finely chopped
- 4 cloves garlic, minced
- 15 g ginger, minced
- 1½ tbsp granulated sugar
- 1½ tbsp black vinegar
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 2 tsp cornflour
- 150 ml water
- 30 g spring onions, green parts only, cut into 3 cm lengths
- 1 tsp sesame oil

## Method

1. Stir the sugar, black vinegar, light soy sauce, cornflour and water together in a bowl until nothing gritty is left on the bottom when you tilt it. Leave it beside the hob. The cornflour drops out of suspension within a minute, so it gets stirred again before it goes in.
2. Cut the aubergine into batons 2 cm thick and 7 cm long, skin on, and do it now rather than earlier — cut faces turn brown and then grey within ten minutes, and the skin is what holds each baton together in the wok.
3. Heat 2 tbsp of the peanut oil in a 28 cm wok over high heat for 90 seconds, until it shimmers and runs like water. Lay in half the aubergine in one layer, cut faces down.
4. Fry for 4 minutes, turning the batons once at the halfway point. The pan goes dry in the first 30 seconds as the aubergine drinks the oil, and wet again as the flesh collapses and gives most of it back — do not add more in between. They are done when two faces are deep gold and a baton bends under the tongs instead of snapping. Lift them onto a plate.
5. Add 2 tbsp more oil and fry the rest the same way, 4 minutes. Lift them out onto the same plate.
6. Turn the heat down to medium. Add the last 1 tbsp of oil, the doubanjiang and the pickled chillies. Fry for 90 seconds, stirring constantly, until the oil has turned deep red and the paste smells sweet rather than raw. This is the step the dish is won or lost on: over high heat the paste scorches and takes the whole wok bitter with it.
7. Add the garlic and ginger. Fry for 30 seconds, until you can smell the garlic over the paste. It must not colour.
8. Return the aubergine and turn it through the paste for 30 seconds, until every baton is red to the ends.
9. Stir the sauce and pour it round the rim of the wok. Bring it to a boil and cook for 3 minutes, turning the aubergine twice. The sauce clouds, then clears and goes glossy, and reduces by half. It is done when a baton lifted out carries the sauce with it rather than shedding it, and a spoon dragged across the wok leaves a track that closes slowly.
10. Take the wok off the heat. Add the spring onion greens and the sesame oil, turn it twice, and serve at once with plain rice.

## Notes

There is no fish in it and there never was. Yu xiang — fish fragrance — is the
name of a seasoning, the pickled chilli, garlic, ginger, sugar and vinegar that
Sichuan cooks put on fish, and the dish is the seasoning applied to something
else. Aubergine is the thing it is applied to most often.

The pickled chillies are what make it yu xiang rather than a sweet and sour
stir-fry, so do not leave them out on the grounds that there is already
doubanjiang in the pan. They are sold as pao jiao in a jar, whole and red, in
any Chinese supermarket; chop them with their seeds. The doubanjiang brings salt
and depth, the pickled chillies bring the sour fruity heat that gives the sauce
its name, and neither substitutes for the other.

Sugar and vinegar in equal measure is the ratio, and at a tablespoon and a half
each for two people it is meant to taste openly sweet and openly sour. This is
the upper end of what published versions use. It is the right end: the sauce has
to carry 500 g of very mild vegetable, and a cautious version of it tastes of
salt and nothing else.

Chinkiang black vinegar, not cider or white wine vinegar. It is malty and dark
and only mildly acidic, and the sweetness in this sauce is balanced against
that. Rice vinegar is the nearest thing if you have no Chinkiang, but it is
sharper and thinner, so start at 1 tbsp and taste.

Aubergine and oil is the whole technical problem of the dish. The flesh is a
foam of air pockets, so it takes up oil the moment it hits the pan and then
gives most of it back as the cells break down and the pockets close. The
mistake is adding more during the dry-looking window in between, and the pan
that results is genuinely swimming. Five tablespoons for 500 g is enough,
divided as this recipe divides it.

A restaurant deep-fries the aubergine instead — 500 ml of oil at 180 °C for
3 minutes, until it is gold and floating. The texture is better, silkier and
more even, and it is a litre of hot oil to strain and store for a dish that
takes fifteen minutes. Shallow-frying in two batches gets most of the way there.

The common home variation adds meat: 100 g of fatty pork mince, fried until dry
and separate before the doubanjiang goes in, in place of the first tablespoon of
oil. It is good, and it is why the dish is often listed as a pork one. As
written here there is none, which is also how Sichuan restaurants serve it in
the vegetable section of the menu.

A serving carries 1,610 mg of sodium, which is 67% of a day's reference intake,
and it arrives in four parts: the doubanjiang at 750 mg, the tablespoon of soy
sauce at 445 mg, the pickled chillies at 300 mg, and the black vinegar at
105 mg. Dropping the soy sauce and taking the doubanjiang to 15 g loses a third
of it and leaves the sauce still recognisable, because the sugar and the vinegar
are doing the work the salt gets credit for. Of the 484 kcal a serving, the oil
is two thirds — there is no version of this dish that is not mostly oil, and a
version fried in less is a version that has stewed.

## Storage

Two days in the fridge, covered. It reheats better than most fried vegetables:
a frying pan over medium heat with 2 tbsp of water, turning rather than
stirring, for 3 minutes. The aubergine comes back softer than it went in, which
suits it.

It is also good straight from the fridge, cold, where the vinegar reads sharper
and the oil reads heavier. Do not freeze it — aubergine that has been frozen
thaws watery and stringy, and the sauce separates.
