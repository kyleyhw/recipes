## Ingredients

- 3 eggs
- 225 ml water, warm to the touch
- 0.5 tsp salt
- 1 tsp light soy sauce
- 0.5 tsp sesame oil
- 10 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Beat the eggs in a bowl with chopsticks or a fork for 30 seconds, cutting through them rather than whipping, until the yolks and whites are one even yellow and no ribbon of white is left. Stop before it foams.
2. Warm 225 ml of water to blood heat — it should feel neither warm nor cool on the inside of your wrist, and it must not be hot. Stir in the salt until it has gone, then pour the water into the eggs in a thin stream, stirring the whole time.
3. Pour the mixture through a fine sieve into a shallow heatproof bowl 16 to 18 cm across, so it stands 2 cm deep. What stays in the sieve is the stringy chalaza and any white that never mixed in, and those are exactly the lumps a rough custard is made of. Draw a spoon across the surface to lift off every bubble: a bubble left there steams into a crater.
4. Put a rack in a wok or a wide pan, add water to just below it, and bring it to a rolling boil. Cover the bowl tightly with foil, or with a plate that sits inside its rim.
5. Set the bowl on the rack and turn the heat down until the water is barely moving — a lazy bubble every second or two, no more. Steam covered for 12 minutes.
6. It is done when the surface is matt and unbroken, tilting the bowl sends a single slow wobble across it with no liquid running at the edge, and a knife slipped into the centre comes out clean with clear liquid — not cloudy — filling the slit.
7. Take the bowl out, lift the foil away from you so no condensation drops on it, and pour the soy sauce and sesame oil over the top. Scatter the spring onion on. Eat it out of the bowl with a spoon, with rice.

## Notes

The ratio is the recipe: one part beaten egg to one and a half parts water, by
weight or by volume, which for three eggs is 150 g of egg and 225 ml of water.
Equal parts sets firm and rubbery and tastes of egg rather than of custard. Two
parts water barely sets, and slumps into a puddle the moment a spoon goes in.
If you are working from eggs rather than a scale, measure the beaten egg in a
jug and add half again — a large egg is 50 g out of the shell, but not every egg
is large.

Low heat is the other half of it, and the mistake everyone makes once. Steam
this over a rolling boil and the water inside the custard boils too; the egg
sets around the bubbles and you get a stiff yellow sponge full of holes, wet
underneath. It is edible and it is not this dish. If your smallest flame still
runs the pan hard, prop the pan lid open with a chopstick, which vents enough
heat to do the same job.

The water goes in warm rather than cold because the salt dissolves in it and the
custard sets a couple of minutes sooner. Keep it under blood heat. Hot water
poured into beaten egg cooks threads of it on the way in, and those threads
never smooth out again — straining catches them but you have lost the egg.

Cover the bowl. An uncovered custard gets rained on by the lid for twelve
minutes, and every drop leaves a pit in the surface.

The panel reads 811 mg of sodium a serving, nearly all of it the half teaspoon
of salt, and none of it gets poured away. That is a lot for 120 kcal, and it is
the right amount: an under-salted custard tastes of nothing at all. It is a dish
for the middle of a table with rice and two other things, not a bowl on its own.

The soy and the sesame oil go on after steaming, never into the mixture. Soy in
the bowl turns the whole thing grey-brown and, being salty, tightens the set.

Things that go in with the water, one at a time: a spoonful of soaked dried
shrimp, a few clams, or a chopped soaked shiitake, all of which sink and sit at
the bottom. Chicken stock in place of the water makes a richer custard and a
less delicate one — worth doing once to see which you prefer, though the plain
version is the one people go back to.

Eaten alongside a steamed patty, this is the standard Cantonese pair: Steamed
Pork Patty with Preserved Bean Curd or Steamed Pork Patty with Salted Egg on one
side, the custard on the other, and a lot of rice.

## Storage

Eat it within a few minutes of it leaving the steamer. It is at its best warm,
and it is one of the few things here that genuinely does not keep well: a set
egg custard chilled overnight weeps water, and no reheating puts it back.

If you must, two days covered in the fridge. Steam it again for 4 minutes over
the same low heat to take the chill off — pour the pooled water away first, and
expect a firmer, coarser custard than the one you made.

It does not freeze at all. The water separates as ice, and what thaws is curds
in a puddle.
