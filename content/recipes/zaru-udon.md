## Ingredients

- 200 ml dashi
- 4 tbsp light soy sauce
- 3 tbsp mirin
- 400 g udon noodles
- 20 g ginger, finely grated
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Put the dashi, soy sauce and mirin in a small pan and bring to a boil. Boil for 30 seconds — this burns off the alcohol in the mirin, which is sharp when the tsuyu is drunk cold. Take it off the heat.
2. Cool it to room temperature, then refrigerate for at least an hour. It must be properly cold; a lukewarm dipping sauce is the commonest fault in this dish.
3. Bring a large pan of water to a rolling boil and cook the udon: 1 to 2 minutes for frozen or vacuum-packed, 8 to 10 for dried.
4. Drain and tip them into a bowl of cold water. Now wash them: work the strands between your hands under running cold water for 30 seconds, until the water runs clear and the noodles squeak against each other. This takes off the surface starch, and skipping it gives you cold noodles glued into a mass.
5. Tip them into a bowl of water with ice in it for 1 minute, until they are genuinely cold to the hand, then drain them thoroughly and shake the colander hard. Water left on them dilutes the tsuyu.
6. Pile the noodles on two flat plates or draining trays. Pour the cold tsuyu into two small deep bowls, about 100 ml each, and put the grated ginger and spring onion on a saucer between them.
7. To eat: stir as much ginger and spring onion into your tsuyu as you want, lift a few strands of noodle, dip the bottom third of them, and eat. Do not tip the tsuyu over the noodles — the whole point is that each mouthful is dipped to the strength you like.

## Notes

Rinse them, and rinse them properly. This is the one udon dish where the surface
starch is the enemy: Kamatama Udon wants it, this wants it gone. Thirty seconds
of actual washing, hands in the bowl, until they squeak.

Then ice them. Cold from the tap is not cold enough; the noodles need to be
below room temperature or they go soft in the minute it takes to plate them, and
half the pleasure of the dish is that they are firm and slippery and cold.

The tsuyu is a dipping sauce, not a soup, and it is roughly three times the
strength of the broth in Tsukimi Udon — 200 ml of dashi to 4 tbsp of soy here
against 400 ml to 1 tbsp there. Made at soup strength it tastes of nothing once
a mouthful of noodles has been through it. If it tastes unpleasantly strong on
its own, it is right.

Boil the mirin for 30 seconds. In a hot broth the alcohol cooks off on its own;
in something served cold it does not, and raw mirin has a sharp edge that is
obvious at this concentration.

The panel reads 2056 mg of sodium a serving and it is the one figure on this
site that overstates by design. It counts the whole 100 ml of tsuyu as eaten,
and the whole point of dipping is that most of it stays in the bowl — a third
of it reaching the noodles would be a lot. Half that number is nearer the
truth, and unlike the pork belly's rendered fat there is no honest way to
compute it, since it depends entirely on how deep you dip.

Ginger rather than wasabi, and both are correct — wasabi is more usual with
soba, ginger with udon. Grated daikon is the third option and the most
refreshing of them.

This is the only cold savoury dish here besides Chilled Egg Tofu with Dashi
Jelly, and it is a July dish. It is also the best possible use of a hot kitchen:
nothing is cooked for more than two minutes and nothing is served warm.

## Storage

The tsuyu keeps a week in the fridge, covered, and is worth making four times
over — it is also the dipping sauce for cold soba and for tempura let down with
a little more dashi.

The noodles do not keep at all once boiled and iced. They stiffen and dry within
an hour on the plate and go chalky in the fridge overnight. Cook what you will
eat.

Neither part freezes.
