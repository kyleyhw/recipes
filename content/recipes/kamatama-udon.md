## Ingredients

- 1 egg
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1 tsp mirin
- 200 g udon noodles
- 10 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Crack the egg into the bowl you are going to eat from — a deep one, warmed with hot water from the tap and tipped out. Beat it for 15 seconds with chopsticks, cutting rather than whipping, until no clear ribbon of white is left.
2. Stir in the soy sauce and mirin.
3. Bring a pan of water to a rolling boil and cook the udon: 1 to 2 minutes for frozen or vacuum-packed, 8 to 10 for dried.
4. Lift the noodles straight out with tongs or a spider into the bowl with the egg. Do not rinse them and do not drain them thoroughly — a little water clinging to them is what keeps the sauce loose.
5. Stir hard and immediately, for about 20 seconds. The egg turns from liquid to a pale, glossy sauce that clings to every strand and pools slightly at the bottom of the bowl. If it looks watery, keep stirring; if it looks scrambled, the noodles were too hot and you were too slow.
6. Scatter the spring onion over and eat at once, before it sets any further.

## Notes

Kama-tama is *kama*, the pot, and *tama*, the egg: noodles taken straight from
the boiling pot into a raw egg. Everything about it depends on that being
literal. Noodles that have sat in a colander for a minute have lost the heat
that thickens the egg, and you get raw egg on warm noodles.

Do not rinse. Every other udon dish that gets served cold, and half of the hot
ones, want the noodles rinsed to take the surface starch off. This one wants
that starch — it is part of what makes the sauce cling.

It is the same trick as Tamago Kake Gohan, one carbohydrate over. Hot rice
thickens a raw egg into a sauce; hot noodles do the same and hold it better,
having more surface for it to cling to. If you like one you will like the other.

Raw egg, so it has to be an egg you would eat raw — a British Lion mark in the
UK, which the Food Standards Agency says is safe raw including for vulnerable
groups. The full argument is in Tamago Kake Gohan's notes.

Frozen udon, if you can get it. This dish is 90% noodle and the difference
between frozen and dried udon is the difference between a chewy, slippery,
substantial strand and a soft one. Two hundred grams is a proper portion here
because there is nothing else in the bowl.

Things that go on, one at a time: a spoonful of tenkasu for crunch, grated
ginger, a torn sheet of nori, a pinch of shichimi, or a second yolk if you are
feeling like it. Butter is not traditional and is very good.

## Storage

None, and this one really means it. The egg keeps setting from the moment it
meets the noodles and the sauce breaks within a couple of minutes. Cold, it is a
solid block.

Cook it when you are standing there ready to eat it. It takes three minutes,
which is the entire argument for the dish.
