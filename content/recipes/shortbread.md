## Ingredients

- 200 g unsalted butter, cold and cubed
- 100 g caster sugar
- 0.5 tsp salt
- 250 g all-purpose flour
- 50 g cornflour

## Method

1. Heat the oven to 150 °C. Line a 20 cm square tin with baking paper, cutting the corners so the paper sits flat against the sides.
2. Rub the cold butter into the sugar and salt with your fingertips for 2 minutes, lifting the mixture and letting it fall. It goes from cubes to coarse crumbs and should stay cool — if it starts to look like a paste, put the bowl in the fridge for 10 minutes. Cold is the whole method: warm butter makes shortbread that spreads and goes greasy rather than short.
3. Add the flour and cornflour and work them in with your hands for 2 minutes. It stays as loose rubble for a long time and then, quite suddenly, comes together when squeezed. Stop the moment a handful holds its shape. There is no liquid in this, so any gluten it develops is permanent.
4. Tip the rubble into the tin and press it flat with the base of a glass, working from the middle outwards, until the surface is level and even. Prick it all over with a fork, right through to the base, at 2 cm intervals.
5. Bake for 45 minutes. It is done when the surface is a pale biscuit colour, the edges have gone one shade darker, and the middle no longer looks damp. It should not brown. If the top is golden the sugar has caramelised, which is a different and lesser biscuit.
6. Take it out and cut it in the tin immediately, while it is too hot to touch: 12 fingers, one cut down the middle and then five across. Left to cool whole it becomes impossible to cut without shattering.
7. Sprinkle 1 tsp of caster sugar over the top, then leave it in the tin for 1 hour without moving it. It is soft coming out of the oven and sets as it cools; lifted early, a finger snaps under its own weight.

## Notes

Three butter : two flour : one sugar, by weight, and the cornflour is a quarter
of the flour taken out and replaced. That swap is the only modern part of the
recipe and it is worth keeping: cornflour has no gluten to develop, so it makes
the crumb shorter and sandier, which is the texture the word shortbread is
actually describing.

Do not cream the butter. Every other butter-and-sugar recipe in this collection
starts by beating air into softened butter; this one starts by rubbing cold
butter into sugar, and doing it the other way gives a cake-like biscuit that
puffs and then sinks. It is the clearest illustration in the collection of
method mattering more than ingredients — the same five things, creamed, are a
different food.

Salted butter instead of unsalted, and drop the half teaspoon of salt, is the
traditional Scottish way and is very slightly better. It is not written that
way here only because the salt in a block of salted butter varies by about a
factor of two between brands, and this recipe has nowhere to hide an error that
size.

The temperature is low and the time is long on purpose. Shortbread is being
dried as much as baked, and the water in the butter has to leave before the
sugar starts to colour. Rushing it at 180 °C for 20 minutes gives a biscuit
that is brown outside and damp in the middle.

For a Christmas version, work the finely grated zest of an orange into the
sugar with your fingers before the butter goes in, which spreads the oil
through the whole batch in a way that adding zest later does not.

## Storage

Two weeks in a tin at room temperature, and it is better after two days than on
the first — the flavour rounds out and the texture settles. This is a biscuit
built to keep, which is why it was ever made in quantity.

Nothing brings back shortbread that has gone soft, because what has happened is
that it has taken up water, and drying it in the oven turns it hard rather than
short. Keep the tin properly shut and keep it away from anything moist.

It freezes for three months, baked and cut, in a sealed box with paper between
the layers, and thaws in 30 minutes on a rack. The unbaked dough freezes
pressed into the lined tin: bake it from frozen at 150 °C for 55 minutes.
