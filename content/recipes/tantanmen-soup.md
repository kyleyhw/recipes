## Ingredients

- 1 tbsp neutral oil
- 200 g fatty pork mince
- 10 g garlic, finely chopped
- 10 g ginger, finely chopped
- 40 g zha cai, finely chopped
- 1 tbsp shaoxing wine
- 1 tbsp dark soy sauce
- 3 tbsp sesame paste
- 2 tbsp light soy sauce
- 2 tbsp chilli oil
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 1 tsp ground sichuan pepper
- 700 ml chicken stock
- 180 g egg noodles
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Heat the oil in a frying pan over high heat until it shimmers. Add the pork and press it flat against the base. Fry 4 minutes without stirring, then break it up and fry 4 minutes more, until the water it released has gone and the meat is dark brown, dry and crumbly with crisp edges. This is dry-fried mince, not browned mince, and the difference is about five extra minutes.
2. Add the garlic, ginger and zha cai and fry 60 seconds. Add the shaoxing wine and dark soy sauce and stir until the pan is dry again, about 60 seconds more. Take it off the heat.
3. In each of two deep bowls, put half the sesame paste, light soy sauce, chilli oil, sugar and ground sichuan pepper. Stir each to a thick, smooth paste with the back of a spoon — it will look far too concentrated and it is meant to.
4. Bring the stock to a boil. Ladle about 100 ml into each bowl and whisk hard with a fork or small whisk for 20 seconds. The paste will resist and then give, and the liquid turns from oily and separated to one opaque, pale brown emulsion. Do not add the rest until this has happened.
5. Whisk in the remaining stock, 150 ml at a time.
6. Cook the noodles in boiling water — 90 seconds for thin egg noodles, 4 minutes for medium — and drain well.
7. Lower the noodles into the bowls, pile the pork on top, scatter the spring onion, and eat at once. Stir from the bottom as you go; the sesame settles.

## Notes

The broth is an emulsion and it is made in the bowl, not the pan. Sesame paste
whisked into a full litre of stock will not come together — it needs to be
beaten with a small amount of very hot liquid first, the way you would loosen
tahini or make a mayonnaise, and then let down. Get this wrong and you have a
slick of oil on thin soup, which is what most disappointing tantanmen is.

Chinese sesame paste, not tahini. Tahini is ground from hulled unroasted seeds
and is pale and mild; Chinese sesame paste is roasted whole seeds and is the
darker, stronger, faintly bitter thing this dish is built on. Japanese neri goma
is the same product. Using tahini gives you a broth that tastes of very little.

Dry-fry the pork properly. Eight minutes, most of it undisturbed, until it is
crumbly and dark and the pan is dry. Mince that is merely cooked through goes
soft in the broth and disappears; mince that is dry and crisp stays a separate
texture, which is the point of putting it on top rather than in.

Zha cai is what makes this taste like a shop's. Forty grams of preserved mustard
stem, chopped small and fried with the aromatics, gives the salty-sour crunch
running through the pork. Pickled mustard greens work; nothing else really does.

No clear soup anywhere. This is the opposite end of the noodle collection from
Tsukimi Udon — that one is a transparent dashi where every ingredient is
visible, this is opaque and thick enough to coat the noodles. Both are correct
and it is worth cooking them a week apart.

Tantanmen is Japan's version of Sichuan dan dan noodles, which have almost no
liquid at all and no sesame in some versions. The soup, the emulsion and the
volume of it are all Japanese — Dan Dan Noodles, in this collection, is the
Chengdu dish this one descends from, and the two eat nothing alike.

The panel is high in fat and it is nearly all the sesame paste and chilli oil,
which are the dish. There is no version of this that is not rich.

## Storage

The pork keeps four days in the fridge and freezes for two months, and it is
worth making three times as much — it goes on rice, on plain noodles, into
lettuce cups.

The broth does not keep well. The emulsion breaks in the fridge and comes back
grainy and separated however hard you whisk. Make the paste in the bowl fresh
each time; it takes a minute.

Noodles never go in the fridge in soup. They swell into the broth and both are
ruined by morning.
