## Ingredients

- 1 tbsp neutral oil
- 300 g beef sirloin, sliced as thinly as you can
- 500 g potato (3), in 4 cm chunks
- 110 g onion (1), in 8 wedges
- 120 g carrots (2), in 3 cm chunks
- 400 ml dashi
- 2 tbsp sake
- 1 tbsp granulated sugar
- 3 tbsp mirin
- 3 tbsp light soy sauce
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Heat the oil in a heavy 24 cm pan over high heat. Add the beef and stir for 2 minutes, until it has gone from red to brown and no pink is left on the outside. It does not need to be cooked through; it will simmer for half an hour.
2. Add the potato, onion and carrot and stir for 2 minutes, until every piece is coated in the fat and the onion edges have gone translucent.
3. Pour in the dashi and the sake. It should come about two thirds of the way up the food — not cover it. Bring to a simmer and skim the grey foam off for the first 2 minutes.
4. Add the sugar and the mirin. Cut a circle of baking paper the width of the pan, make a small hole in the middle, and lay it directly on the surface of the food. Simmer gently for 15 minutes.
5. Add the soy sauce, put the paper back, and simmer 12 minutes more, until a skewer goes into a potato with no resistance and the liquid has reduced to about a third of what you poured in.
6. Take the pan off the heat and leave it, uncovered, for 20 minutes. It is not finished cooking — it is finished absorbing, and this is when that happens.
7. Warm it through, spoon into bowls with plenty of the liquid, and scatter the spring onion over.

## Notes

**Sugar before soy, and it is not arbitrary.** Japanese kitchens teach the order
as sa-shi-su-se-so — sugar, salt, vinegar, soy, miso — and the reason for the
first two is physical: sucrose is a much larger molecule than sodium chloride
and diffuses into a potato far more slowly. Salt the pan first and the sugar
never gets in; sweeten it first and both do. Fifteen minutes between them is
enough.

**The floating lid.** Otoshibuta is a wooden disc that sits on the food rather
than on the pan, and baking paper does the same job: it holds the liquid against
the top of the potatoes, which are not covered, and it stops them knocking about
and breaking up as they soften. Without one you have to turn everything halfway
and you will lose a potato to it.

**The twenty minutes off the heat.** Simmering drives liquid *out* of a potato;
cooling draws it back in, seasoned. This is why nikujaga is a dish every
Japanese recipe tells you to make in advance, and why it is genuinely better the
next day rather than merely acceptable.

Thin beef is the ingredient. What this wants is the sliced beef sold frozen in
Japanese and Korean shops for shabu-shabu — a millimetre thick, cooked in
seconds. Failing that, freeze a piece of sirloin for 30 minutes and slice it as
thinly as your knife allows, which is the same trick Gyudon uses and takes the
same half hour.

Not enough liquid to cover, on purpose. Nikujaga is a simmered dish and not a
stew: what you want at the end is a shallow pool of concentrated, glossy liquid
that the potatoes have soaked up, not a broth. If it looks dry at step 5, add
50 ml of water rather than more dashi — the seasoning is already where it should
be.

Shirataki noodles and snap peas are the two things most often in this and are
both left out here. The noodles need a Japanese grocer; the peas go in for the
last two minutes if you have them, and they are colour rather than flavour.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge, and this is the rare dish where day two is the
recommendation rather than the fallback. The potatoes go on taking up the
liquid, the beef softens, and the whole thing tastes more like itself. Reheat
gently — a hard boil breaks the potatoes.

It does not freeze. Boiled potato comes out of a freezer grainy and collapsing,
and there is no version of this without them.
