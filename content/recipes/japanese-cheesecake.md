## Ingredients

- 250 g cream cheese
- 60 g unsalted butter
- 100 ml milk
- 6 egg yolks
- 1 tbsp lemon juice
- 60 g all-purpose flour
- 20 g cornflour
- 0.25 tsp salt
- 6 egg whites
- 120 g caster sugar

## Method

1. Heat the oven to 150 °C. Line an 18 cm round tin — base and sides, with the paper standing 5 cm above the rim — and wrap the outside in two layers of foil, right up to the top, so no water can get in.
2. Melt the cream cheese, butter and milk together in a bowl set over a pan of simmering water, whisking, for 5 minutes, until completely smooth with no flecks of cheese left. Take it off and let it cool for 10 minutes, to no warmer than your hand.
3. Whisk in the egg yolks two at a time, then the lemon juice. Sift the flour, cornflour and salt over and whisk for 20 seconds to a batter the consistency of pouring cream. Set it aside.
4. In a spotlessly clean bowl, whisk the egg whites on medium for 1 minute, until opaque and foaming. Add the caster sugar in three lots with 20 seconds between, then whisk 3 minutes more.
5. Stop at soft peaks — a lifted whisk carries a peak that stands and then curls over. Stiff peaks are the failure that produces a cracked top and a rubbery seam at the bottom.
6. Fold a third of the meringue into the cheese batter to loosen it, roughly. Fold in the rest in two lots, cutting down and lifting up, 15 turns each, until there are no white streaks and the batter still mounds on the spatula.
7. Pour into the tin from a height of 20 cm, which breaks the large bubbles on the way in. Stand the tin in a roasting tray and pour boiling water into the tray to a depth of 3 cm.
8. Bake for 75 minutes. It rises 4 cm above the tin at around 40 minutes and settles back over the last 20, which is normal and is not a collapse. It is done when the top is an even pale gold, the middle wobbles as one piece rather than rippling, and a skewer comes out clean.
9. Turn the oven off, open the door 5 cm, and leave the cake in it for 30 minutes. A cheesecake taken straight from a 150 °C oven into a kitchen shrinks away from its sides and wrinkles across the top.
10. Take it out, cool on a rack for 30 minutes, then refrigerate for 4 hours in the tin. Turn it out and peel the paper away only when it is cold — warm, the sides tear off with the paper.

## Notes

Three cheesecakes go by this name and this is the soufflé one — cotton
cheesecake, the tall pale wobbling cylinder from a Japanese bakery window. It
has a third of the cream cheese of the baked American kind, half of it replaced
by milk and butter, and it is leavened entirely by meringue. The Mascarpone
Cheesecake already here is the other tradition, and the two are not variations
on each other.

Soft peaks, and this is where it is won or lost. Over-whipped whites cannot be
folded without breaking, and broken meringue is what cracks the top and leaves
a dense band at the base. A peak that flops at the tip is right.

Full-fat cream cheese only. The light version is the same solids with water
where the fat was; it weeps in the water bath and the cake never sets.

The water bath is not optional and neither is the foil. What you are after is
steam and gentle heat: the water holds the tin at about 100 °C so the outside
never sets hard while the middle cooks, and that is why the crumb is uniform
all the way through. Water that gets in through a seam gives a soggy base that
nothing will fix.

The slow cool in the oven is the last real step. Cooled fast, the cake contracts
faster than its structure can take and wrinkles like a deflating balloon —
which is a cosmetic failure only, and it will still taste right.

It is much less sweet than any Western cheesecake, at 120 g of sugar in a cake
this size. Apricot jam warmed with a teaspoon of water and brushed over the top
is the bakery finish.

## Storage

Four days in the fridge, covered, and it is at its best on the second — the
flavour rounds out and the texture firms just enough to slice cleanly. Cut it
with a hot dry knife, wiped between slices.

Take it out 30 minutes before eating. Straight from the fridge the butter in it
is solid and the whole point of the texture is lost; at room temperature it
goes back to being what it was.

It freezes for a month, whole or in slices, wrapped tightly, and thaws in the
fridge overnight rather than on the counter. The texture comes back about nine
tenths of the way, which is better than most baked custards manage.
