## Ingredients

- 350 g all-purpose flour
- 4 tsp baking powder
- 0.5 tsp salt
- 85 g unsalted butter, cold and cubed
- 40 g caster sugar
- 175 ml milk
- 1 egg

## Method

1. Heat the oven to 220 °C and line a baking tray. The high heat is the whole rise: baking powder gives one burst of gas as it wets and another as it heats, and a slow oven wastes both.
2. Stir the flour, baking powder and salt together in a large bowl for 15 seconds.
3. Rub in the cold butter with your fingertips for 2 minutes, lifting the mixture high and letting it fall so the air cools it. Stop at coarse breadcrumbs with a few pea-sized lumps still in it. Those lumps become steam pockets; rubbing on until the mixture is uniform gives a scone with the texture of a bun.
4. Stir in the sugar. Make a well, pour in the milk all at once, and bring it together with a table knife held on its edge, cutting through the mixture rather than stirring it, until it is a shaggy mass with no dry flour left — 20 seconds.
5. Tip onto a lightly floured surface and fold it over on itself 4 times, pressing down gently between folds. That is all the handling it gets. A worked scone dough goes tough and rises lopsided.
6. Press it out to 3 cm thick — thick, not thin; a scone rises to about twice its height and starting at 2 cm gives a flat one.
7. Cut 8 rounds with a floured 6 cm cutter. Push the cutter straight down and lift it straight up. Do not twist it: twisting seals and drags the cut edge, and the scone rises in a spiral or not at all. Press the offcuts together once, gently, for the last two.
8. Set them on the tray 4 cm apart. Beat the egg and brush the tops only, keeping it off the cut sides, where it glues the layers shut.
9. Bake for 12 minutes, until well risen, deep gold on top and pale at the sides, and the sides have a visible seam where they split as they rose.
10. Eat within the hour, split by hand rather than cut with a knife.

## Notes

Cold butter, cold milk, hot oven, and as little contact with your hands as the
job allows. Everything else in this recipe is negotiable and those four things
are not.

Do not twist the cutter. It is the single commonest reason home scones come out
squat, and it is invisible while you are doing it.

Self-raising flour and 175 ml of milk is the version most British recipes are
written for, and it works — use 350 g of it and drop the baking powder to
1 tsp. Plain flour with the raising agent measured separately is written here
because it gives 4 tsp of baking powder rather than whatever a bag has had in
it since it was milled, and because everything else in this collection uses
plain flour.

For fruit scones, stir 75 g of sultanas in with the sugar. For cheese scones,
leave the sugar out entirely, add 100 g of grated mature cheddar and a
half-teaspoon of mustard powder, and take the salt up to a teaspoon.

Buttermilk in place of the milk gives a softer, tangier scone and a slightly
better rise, since the acid gives the baking powder more to react with. It is
not written that way because it is a thing people buy for one recipe and throw
away three-quarters of.

Jam or cream first is a real argument with no correct answer, and Devon and
Cornwall disagree about it in opposite directions. Clotted cream is what both
mean by cream; whipped double cream is a different pudding.

## Storage

They are at their best within an hour of coming out of the oven and are notably
worse the next day — this is a recipe for eating, not for keeping, and it takes
25 minutes from start to finish for that reason.

A day-old scone is rescued by heat and nothing else: 5 minutes at 160 °C, split
side up, and it is most of the way back. Toasting the split halves under a
grill is better still.

They freeze well, baked and cooled, bagged, for two months, and reheat from
frozen at 180 °C for 8 minutes. Unbaked, cut rounds also freeze on a tray for a
month and bake from frozen at 220 °C for 15 minutes, which is the best way to
have fresh scones for two people without making eight.
