## Ingredients

- 1 tbsp neutral oil
- 150 g pork belly, sliced 3 mm, in 3 cm pieces
- 150 g onion, sliced 5 mm
- 700 ml dashi
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1 tbsp mirin
- 40 g japanese curry roux (2 blocks)
- 2 tsp cornflour
- 400 g udon noodles
- 15 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Heat the oil in a saucepan over medium-high heat until it shimmers. Add the pork in one layer and fry for 3 minutes without stirring, until browned underneath and the fat has run.
2. Add the onion and fry 3 minutes, until softened and translucent at the edges.
3. Pour in the dashi, add the soy sauce and mirin, bring to a boil, then drop to a simmer and cook 5 minutes, skimming any foam.
4. Take the pan off the heat. Break the curry roux into pieces and stir them in until completely dissolved — off the heat, because roux dropped into a boiling pan clumps around a lump of undissolved fat and never recovers. Put it back on low heat and simmer 2 minutes, stirring, until it has thickened and darkened.
5. Stir the cornflour into 2 tablespoons of cold water until smooth and pour it in, stirring. Simmer 1 minute more. It should now be thick enough to coat a spoon heavily and to hold a channel when you drag a spoon across the base.
6. Cook the udon in a separate pan of boiling water — 1 to 2 minutes for frozen or vacuum-packed, 8 to 10 for dried — and drain well.
7. Put the noodles in two deep warmed bowls and ladle the curry over. Scatter the spring onion and eat at once, with a spoon as well as chopsticks.

## Notes

Cornflour on top of the roux, which sounds like belt and braces and is the
difference between curry udon and curry with noodles in it. Roux alone thickens
a curry meant for rice, where the rice does the holding; here the sauce has to
cling to smooth wet noodles and slide off nothing, and two teaspoons of starch
is what makes it do that. It is also what stops it separating as it cools in the
bowl.

Roux off the heat. Dropped into a boiling pan it seizes into lumps with dry
powder inside them, and no amount of whisking gets them out. Off the heat it
melts.

This is a leftovers recipe first. If there is Japanese curry in the fridge, this
is what to do with it: thin about 400 ml of it with 300 ml of dashi, bring to a
simmer, add the cornflour slurry, and skip everything above step 3. That is the
version most Japanese households actually make and it takes eight minutes.

Dashi rather than water, and it matters more here than it looks. Curry roux is
strong enough to bury most things, but the dashi is what makes this taste
Japanese rather than like curry sauce on udon — it is the same reason the broth
in every other bowl in this section starts there.

It will stain everything within a metre. Japanese restaurants that serve this
hand out paper bibs and it is not a joke; the sauce is thick, it is bright, and
noodles flick. Eat it in something you do not mind.

Forty grams of roux is two blocks and carries about 950 mg of sodium a serving
on its own — see the ingredient row, which explains why the figure is high and
how it was arrived at. Use one block for a milder, thinner bowl and let the
cornflour do more of the work.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge, without the noodles. It thickens to a solid as it
chills, which is normal — reheat with 100 ml of dashi or water per bowl and
stir until it flows.

Freezes for two months, again without noodles. Cornflour-thickened sauces can go
slightly grainy on thawing; a hard whisk over low heat puts it back.

Never store the noodles in it. They swell, the sauce turns claggy round them,
and by morning it is a solid block.
