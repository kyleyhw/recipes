## Ingredients

- 250 g unsalted butter, softened
- 100 g icing sugar
- 0.5 tsp salt
- 1 egg
- 1 tsp vanilla extract
- 300 g all-purpose flour

## Method

1. Take the butter out an hour before you start. It is ready when a finger goes into it without effort and comes out clean. This dough is piped, and butter that is even slightly cold makes a dough that will not leave the nozzle.
2. Beat the butter, icing sugar and salt together with an electric whisk on medium for 4 minutes. It goes from yellow and dense to nearly white, and it has trebled in lightness rather than in volume — the mixture should fall off a lifted beater in a soft ribbon that holds for 2 seconds.
3. Beat in the egg and the vanilla for 1 minute. It will look briefly curdled and come back together.
4. Sift the flour over the bowl in two goes and fold each in with a spatula until no dry flour is visible, 20 turns in all. Stop there. Beaten past that, the dough develops gluten and pipes into a cookie that is tough rather than short.
5. Fit a piping bag with a 10 mm open star nozzle and fill it half full. Pipe 24 rings 5 cm across onto two lined baking trays, 12 to a tray, 4 cm apart. Pipe with your whole hand rather than your fingers; this dough takes real pressure.
6. Chill the trays for 30 minutes, until a ridge pressed with a fingertip holds its shape and feels firm. This is what keeps the ridges sharp in the oven, and a cookie with no ridges is a round biscuit.
7. Heat the oven to 190 °C.
8. Bake both trays for 12 minutes, swapping the shelves at 6 minutes. They are done when the ridges and the outer rim have gone a definite gold and the flat parts are still pale. The colour is the flavour here: a pale cookie tastes of raw flour.
9. Leave them on the trays for 5 minutes, then move them to a rack. Warm, they are soft; cold, they snap.

## Notes

The ratio is 2 butter : 1 sugar : 3 flour, and every good version of this cookie
is a rearrangement of it. There is nothing to hide behind — no chocolate, no
spice, barely any egg — so this is the one recipe where the brand of butter is
worth caring about. A block with 82% fat rather than the usual 80 makes a
noticeably better cookie.

Icing sugar, not caster. Caster sugar does not fully dissolve in a dough this
stiff, and the crystals that survive melt in the oven and leave the cookie
grainy and spread. Icing sugar also brings the cornflour it is cut with, which
is doing quiet work on the texture.

The nozzle has to be metal and it has to be at least 10 mm. A plastic one
splits under the pressure this takes, and a narrow nozzle produces ridges too
fine to survive the oven. If the dough will not come out at all, the answer is
almost never more force: warm the bag between your palms for a minute.

Piping is the only hard part and it is optional. The same dough rolled into a
28 cm log, wrapped, chilled for 2 hours and cut into 8 mm discs makes an
excellent icebox biscuit, and the log keeps a week in the fridge.

This is the Danish cookie, and the blue tin it comes in is a fixture of Chinese
and Hong Kong households far beyond Denmark, which is why it is here. Jenny
Bakery's cookie is a different animal — much more butter, cornflour in place of
some of the flour, and a texture that collapses on the tongue rather than
snapping. Worth knowing the two are not the same recipe with different piping.

## Storage

Three weeks in a tin at room temperature with a tight lid, which is unusually
long and is the point of the thing: there is almost no water in the dough, so
there is very little for staleness to work on. They soften rather than harden,
and 4 minutes at 160 °C dries them out crisp again.

Do not keep them in the same tin as anything soft. A butter cookie takes up
moisture from a brownie or a piece of fudge overnight and will not come back
from it.

The piped, unbaked rings freeze on their trays in 2 hours and then bag for 3
months. Bake from frozen at 190 °C for 14 minutes.
