## Ingredients

- 400 g pork belly, in one piece, skin on
- 20 g ginger, in thick slices
- 30 g spring onions
- 2 tbsp shaoxing wine
- 200 g cucumber (1 small)
- 15 g garlic, crushed to a paste
- 2 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1 tbsp black vinegar
- 2 tsp granulated sugar
- 1 tsp sesame oil
- 2 tbsp chilli oil
- 1 tsp toasted sesame seeds

## Method

1. Put the pork belly in a pan that fits it snugly, cover with cold water by 3 cm, and add the ginger, two thirds of the spring onions and the shaoxing wine. Bring to a bare simmer.
2. Skim the grey foam off the surface for the first few minutes — there will be a lot of it, and it is the difference between a clean-tasting slice and a muddy one. Then simmer, barely moving, for 40 minutes. A skewer pushed into the thickest part should meet no resistance and come out with clear juice.
3. Lift the pork out, put it on a plate, and refrigerate for at least 90 minutes, until it is cold right through and firm. Keep 100 ml of the cooking liquid. This chilling is not optional: warm pork belly cannot be sliced thin, and this dish is only good when it is thin.
4. While it chills, cut the cucumber into ribbons with a peeler, or into 2 mm slices, and lay them over the base of a serving plate.
5. Stir the garlic, soy sauce, black vinegar, sugar, sesame oil and 2 tablespoons of the reserved cooking liquid together until the sugar has gone. The liquid is what turns a sharp dressing into one that tastes of the pork it is going on.
6. Slice the cold pork as thinly as you can with your sharpest knife — 2 mm, through skin, fat and meat together, so every slice shows all three layers. Fifteen or twenty slices from 400 g is right.
7. Drape the slices over the cucumber, overlapping them like roof tiles. Spoon the dressing over, add the chilli oil, scatter the sesame seeds and the remaining spring onions finely sliced, and serve cold.

## Notes

Everything here is a slicing problem. Bai rou means "white meat" and refers to
plainly poached pork; the dish has no seasoning inside the meat at all, so what
you are eating is fat, texture and dressing. Thick slices are chewy and greasy;
paper-thin ones are silky and take the sauce. Cold pork and a sharp knife are the
only two things that get you there, and both are free.

Poach it, do not boil it. A rolling boil for 40 minutes gives tight, grainy meat
and cloudy liquid. Bare simmer — the occasional bubble — gives meat that stays
soft when cold, which is the whole trick of eating pork belly at fridge
temperature.

Two tablespoons of the cooking liquid in the dressing is a small thing that does
a lot. It is unsalted pork stock with ginger and wine in it, and without it the
dressing sits on top of the meat as a separate flavour rather than joining it.

The cucumber is not a garnish. It is half the dish: cold, wet and crunchy against
warm-flavoured fat, and it is what keeps a plate of poached pork belly from being
too much. Do not salt it — it needs its water here.

This is a cold dish served with rice and other things, not a main course on its
own, which is why it is filed as a side despite the 400 g of pork. As one of
three or four dishes it feeds four comfortably.

The panel says 615 kcal a serving and it overstates this dish, for the reason
recorded against pork belly in the library: the figure is for the raw cut, half
of which is fat, and a good deal of that fat renders into the poaching water and
is poured away with it. How much depends on the belly. The honest statement is
that it is a rich dish and that the number is an upper bound.

## Storage

The poached pork keeps three days in the fridge, whole and wrapped, and is
easier to slice on day two than on day one. Poaching it the day before is the
sensible way to cook this.

Assembled, it is a two-hour dish: the cucumber weeps, the dressing thins, and the
fat firms up past pleasant. Slice and dress only what is going to be eaten.

The pork freezes for three months, cooked and whole, and thaws well enough to
slice. The assembled dish does not freeze in any sense.
