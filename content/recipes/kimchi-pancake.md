## Ingredients

- 90 g all-purpose flour
- 30 g cornflour
- 1 tsp granulated sugar
- 1 tsp gochugaru
- 120 ml water, ice-cold
- 200 g kimchi, sour, chopped into 2 cm pieces, with 60 ml of its brine
- 30 g spring onions, cut into 3 cm lengths
- 4 tbsp neutral oil

## Method

1. Whisk the flour, cornflour, sugar and gochugaru together in a bowl for 5 seconds. Pour in the ice-cold water and the 60 ml of kimchi brine and stir with chopsticks for 15 seconds only, until there is no dry flour left. It should still be lumpy. Stirred smooth, the flour develops gluten and the pancake fries chewy instead of crisp, and no amount of oil or heat gets that back.
2. Fold in the kimchi and the spring onions with five turns. The batter should barely coat them — it is glue holding kimchi together, not a batter with kimchi in it.
3. Heat 2 tbsp of the oil in a 24 cm frying pan over medium-high heat for 90 seconds, until it shimmers and a drop of batter sizzles hard on contact. A cool pan is what makes a soggy pancake, and so does being careful with the oil.
4. Pour in half the batter and push it out to the edge of the pan with the back of a spoon, 5 mm thick. Do not touch it again.
5. Fry for 4 minutes. The edges dry into a dark lace and lift away from the pan, and the underside is deep gold with darker blisters where it met the metal.
6. Flip it in one movement. Pour 1 tbsp of oil around the rim so it runs underneath, and press the pancake flat with a spatula — once, firmly. Fry 3 minutes.
7. Flip it back for 30 seconds to re-crisp the first side, then slide it onto a rack. Not a plate: a plate traps steam and the base you just crisped goes soft in a minute.
8. Fry the second one the same way with the remaining oil.
9. Cut into squares with kitchen scissors, straight in the pan or on the rack, and eat at once.

## Notes

Old, sour kimchi. This is the same argument as Army Stew and it matters more
here, because there is almost nothing else in the pancake: a fresh jar makes
something that tastes of flour, and three-month-old kimchi — soft, sour, fizzing
when you open it — is the whole dish. The brine is not optional either. It is a
third of the liquid and all of the seasoning, which is why there is no salt in
the ingredient list.

Barely mix it. Fifteen seconds and lumps left in. This is the single thing that
separates a crisp pancake from a bready one, and it is the opposite instinct
to every other batter.

Ice-cold water, for the same reason: cold slows the gluten and the sharper
temperature difference against a hot pan crisps the surface faster.

Cornflour is standing in for Korean frying flour (튀김가루), which is what a
Korean kitchen would reach for and is worth buying if you make these often — it
is flour with starch and raising agent already in it and gives a lighter,
shatterier crust. Potato starch does the same job as the cornflour here.

Thin, and more oil than feels right. Four tablespoons for two pancakes is
shallow frying rather than greasing, and a pancake spread to 5 mm cooks through
before the outside is past its best. A thick one is doughy in the middle and
there is no fixing it in the pan.

No egg. Plenty of recipes add one and it makes the pancake softer and more
omelette-like, which is a legitimate different thing rather than an
improvement. If you want it, one egg beaten into the batter, and expect less
crunch.

Eaten plain in Korea more often than not, since the kimchi is already seasoned.
If you want a dip, 1 tbsp light soy sauce, 1 tbsp rice vinegar, a pinch of
gochugaru and a few toasted sesame seeds.

Compare it with Spring Onion Pancakes, which reaches a similar place from the
opposite direction: that one is a dough, rested and laminated and rolled, and
this one is a batter that goes from bowl to pan in ninety seconds.

## Storage

Eat it standing at the stove. This has the shortest good window of anything in
the collection — about four minutes, after which the steam trapped inside has
softened the crust from within, and even the rack only delays that.

Leftovers keep a day in the fridge and come back further than they have any
right to: a dry frying pan over medium heat, 2 minutes a side, no oil. A
microwave produces a hot flannel.

The batter does not keep. Made ahead, the flour hydrates and the kimchi weeps
salt into it, and what you fry an hour later is limp. Chop the kimchi and
measure the dry ingredients in advance if you want to get ahead; mix at the
moment the pan goes on.
