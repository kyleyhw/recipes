## Ingredients

- 4 g gelatine (2 leaves)
- 300 ml dashi
- 1 tbsp light soy sauce
- 1 tbsp mirin
- 250 g egg tofu (1 tube)
- 10 g ginger, finely grated
- 10 g spring onions, finely sliced

## Method

1. Put the gelatine leaves in a bowl of cold water for 5 minutes, until limp and doubled in thickness.
2. Warm the dashi, soy sauce and mirin in a small pan over medium heat until it steams and the first bubbles show at the edge. Do not let it boil — boiling drives off the aroma that is the entire point of dashi.
3. Take the pan off the heat. Lift the gelatine out, squeeze it hard, and stir it in for 30 seconds until no threads or specks are left on the spoon.
4. Pour into a shallow dish or tray so it stands about 1 cm deep. Cool for 15 minutes on the counter, then refrigerate for 3 hours, until set firm enough that a finger pressed on it leaves a dent that stays.
5. Chill the unopened tube of tofu in the fridge at the same time. It should be properly cold, not merely not-warm.
6. To serve, slit the tube and slide the tofu out. Cut it into rounds 2 cm thick and put two or three in each of four small bowls.
7. Drag a fork through the jelly to break it into rough glassy crystals — do not chop it neatly. Spoon it over and around the tofu until each bowl has as much jelly as tofu.
8. Put a small heap of grated ginger on top of each and scatter the spring onion. Eat cold, with a spoon.

## Notes

This is what egg tofu was before it was fried. Tamago-dofu is a summer dish in
Japan, served cold in a lacquer bowl with a cold dashi-soy sauce poured over it,
and setting that sauce into a jelly is the version that survives a journey from
the fridge to the table without going everywhere. Everything else on this site
made with the tube is a Chinese use of a Japanese ingredient; this one is not.

Four grams of gelatine to 300 ml is deliberately weak — about half what a
dessert jelly uses. It should collapse under a fork into crystals rather than
holding its shape, and if it needs sawing it has too much gelatine in it. Two
leaves is right; three is a mistake.

Do not boil the dashi. It is the one ingredient here with anything to lose, and
what it loses first is the smell.

Grated ginger is the topping this is written for, and wasabi is the other
standard one — a pea of it per bowl, stirred in by the eater rather than by you.
Wasabi is not in the ingredient list because the tube paste's published figures
disagree with each other by a factor of two and this collection would rather say
nothing than print a number it cannot stand behind.

Serve it colder than feels right. This is a dish about temperature as much as
flavour, and at room temperature it is bland and slightly slimy. Bowls out of
the fridge, if you have the space.

The jelly is worth making double. It keeps a week and goes over cold silken
tofu, blanched greens, or a poached chicken breast, which is roughly what
Japanese cooks use it for.

## Storage

The jelly keeps five days in the fridge, covered, in the tray. Break it up only
as you serve, because broken jelly weeps and reverts to liquid at the edges
within a few hours.

Assembled, this does not keep at all — the tofu sheds water into the jelly and
by the next day there is a puddle. Assemble in the bowl, in front of whoever is
eating it.

Neither part freezes. Gelatine breaks on thawing, and the tofu comes back
spongy.
