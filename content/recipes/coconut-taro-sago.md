## Ingredients

- 60 g tapioca pearls
- 600 ml water
- 400 g taro, in 2 cm cubes
- 80 g granulated sugar
- 200 ml coconut milk
- 100 ml evaporated milk

## Method

1. Bring 1 litre of water to a rolling boil in a small pan. Rain in the tapioca pearls, stirring at once so they cannot stick. Boil for 10 minutes, stirring every couple of minutes, then take the pan off the heat, cover it, and leave it for 10 minutes. Every pearl should be clear through with no white dot at its centre.
2. Drain the pearls, rinse them under cold running water for 30 seconds until they stop feeling slippery, and leave them sitting in a bowl of cold water.
3. Peel the taro with a knife rather than a peeler, wearing gloves. Raw taro sap makes most people's hands itch and burn for an hour, and it is the one genuinely unpleasant thing about this recipe. Cut the peeled corm into 2 cm cubes.
4. Put the 600 ml of water and the taro in a pot and bring to a boil. Turn the heat down and simmer, uncovered, for 20 minutes. It is ready when a skewer goes into a cube with no resistance and about a third of them have started to fray at the corners, clouding the water.
5. Add the sugar and simmer for 3 minutes more, until it has dissolved and the liquid has thickened enough to coat the back of a spoon — the fraying taro is what thickens it, so there is no starch here.
6. Take the pot off the heat. Stir in the coconut milk and then the evaporated milk. Do not put it back on the heat: coconut milk boiled after it goes in splits into oil and grains and will not come back.
7. Drain the tapioca and stir it through. Ladle into bowls and eat it warm.

## Notes

Wear gloves for the peeling, or peel it under running water. The sap carries
needle-shaped calcium oxalate crystals, which is a precise way of saying it will
itch for an hour and nothing washes it off. Cooking destroys them entirely, so
this only matters for the two minutes you are holding the raw corm.

Frozen taro cubes, sold in the freezer of any Chinese supermarket, skip the
peeling altogether and are barely worse than fresh. They go into step 4 straight
from frozen and want 5 minutes longer.

Let a third of it fall apart. That is the whole texture of the dish: some cubes
whole enough to find with a spoon, the rest dissolved into the liquid so the
broth is thick and starchy without a spoonful of cornflour in it. Take it off
when everything is still intact and you have taro in coconut water; take it off
when nothing is and you have soup.

Off the heat before the coconut milk. This is the one instruction people
ignore. Coconut milk is an emulsion held together by protein that boiling
denatures, and a split bowl tastes fine and looks curdled, which for a dessert
is the same as ruined.

The taro to buy is the big cylindrical dasheen with the hairy brown skin and the
purple-flecked flesh, not the small egg-sized cocoyams, which are waxier and
refuse to break down. Press it: it should give like a raw potato, not feel woody.

Cold is the other way to eat this. Chill it for 2 hours and it thickens
considerably, closer to a thin pudding than a soup, and it wants a splash more
evaporated milk loosening it before it goes in the bowl. Warm is how it is
served in a tong sui shop and is the better of the two on any evening below
fifteen degrees.

Sweet potato works in exactly this recipe and needs 5 minutes less, though it
never gets that faintly savoury, nutty quality taro has. Mango Pomelo Sago uses
the same pearls cooked the same way, cold and fruit-led rather than warm and
starchy.

## Storage

Three days in the fridge, covered. It thickens to a near-solid when cold, which
is normal, and the tapioca goes firm and slightly chewy — some people prefer it
that way.

Reheat gently in a pan over low heat with 50 ml of water per bowl, stirring, and
stop before it simmers, for the same reason the coconut milk went in off the
heat in the first place. A microwave is fine at half power.

It does not freeze. The coconut milk splits and the tapioca turns to paste.
