## Ingredients

- 1 tbsp neutral oil
- 100 g pork mince
- 2 cloves garlic, minced
- 60 g pickled mustard greens
- 40 g zha cai
- 0.5 tbsp hot pot base paste
- 0.5 tbsp doubanjiang
- 2 tsp light soy sauce
- 0.25 tsp salt
- 0.25 tsp granulated sugar
- 0.5 tsp chicken bouillon powder
- 700 ml water
- 2 tbsp white vinegar
- 100 g dried rice noodles
- 1 tsp sichuan peppercorn oil

## Method

1. Chop before the pan goes on: the stems of the pickled greens finely, the leaves into 5 mm strips, the zha cai finely, the garlic to a mince. Once the pork is in, there is no point at which you can stop and catch up.
2. Heat the oil in a 20 cm saucepan over medium-high heat for 60 seconds, until a scrap of pork dropped in sizzles at once.
3. Add the pork mince. Cook for 4 minutes, breaking it apart and then leaving it alone in the last minute, until the grains have gone from grey to brown and some are crisp at the edges.
4. Add the garlic, the pickled greens and the zha cai. Stir-fry for 90 seconds, until the pickles smell sour and toasted rather than raw and the pan is dry.
5. Add the hot pot base paste and the doubanjiang. Stir-fry for 60 seconds, until the fat running out of them is deep red and the paste no longer smells raw. Keep it moving — doubanjiang burns in a dry pan and takes the whole bowl bitter with it.
6. Add the soy sauce, salt, sugar and chicken bouillon powder, then pour in the water. Bring to a boil, then simmer for 5 minutes over medium heat, until the broth has darkened and a spoonful tastes of the pickles rather than of the paste.
7. While it simmers, boil the rice noodles in a separate pan for the time on the packet, less 1 minute. Drain and put them in a deep bowl.
8. Take the broth off the heat and stir in the vinegar. Adding it off the heat is the point: boiled vinegar loses its sharpness and you get sour-tasting salt rather than a hot and sour broth.
9. Ladle the broth and everything in it over the noodles, and drizzle the peppercorn oil over the top. Serve at once, before the noodles soften any further.

## Notes

The quantities are the source's own.

It is very salty. The panel says 5400 mg of sodium, which is more than twice
a day's reference intake in one bowl, and that is with the broth drunk as well
as the noodles eaten — which is how a noodle soup is eaten. Both pickles are
preserved in salt, and 40 g of zha cai alone carries more than a quarter
teaspoon of it.

Three things bring it down without changing what the dish is: rinse the pickled
greens under cold water and squeeze them dry, which takes about a third off
their salt for very little sourness; leave the 0.25 tsp of salt out until you
have tasted the broth, which is the last thing it needs; and leave some of the
broth in the bowl.

Toppings are the point of the dish in Chongqing: blanched greens, a soft-boiled
egg, a handful of roasted peanuts, or fried peanuts and coriander. Add them in
the bowl, on top, after the broth.

Two tablespoons of vinegar is the upper end of what the source suggests. It
should taste properly sour, not faintly of vinegar — but add the second
tablespoon after tasting, because the pickles vary in how sour they are.

Sweet potato starch noodles are the other way this is made, and the more usual
one for this bowl. They take longer to cook and stay slippery instead of going
soft, but they are a different row in the library, so the macros here are for
rice noodles.

## Storage

Eat it immediately. Rice noodles sitting in hot broth keep cooking until they
are paste, and this bowl is soft within ten minutes of being poured.

The broth alone keeps three days in the fridge and is worth making ahead —
everything through step 6, cooled and covered. Bring it back to a simmer, add
the vinegar off the heat, and cook fresh noodles into it. Do not freeze it: the
pickles come out of the freezer limp.
